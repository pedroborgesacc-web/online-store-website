## 2. Why it matters

**In practice.** Investor clients buy rental homes, duplexes and small apartment buildings in Florida for the income they produce. They will ask you what a property "is worth at an 8% cap rate", or why the listing price looks high for the rent. You need to build net operating income correctly, and above all leave the mortgage payment out of it. You also need to know where your role stops. You may prepare a price opinion, but a federally related loan needs an appraisal from an appraiser, however experienced you are.

**On the exam.** Appraisal is **8%** of the exam (V-02, confirmed), about 8 of the 100 questions (V-01). This lesson holds the section's heaviest math (NOI, IRV, GRM, GIM) and two of the manual's three planted traps: treating mortgage payments as operating expenses, and "averaging the three approaches". The third trap, adjusting the subject, is in L16-2. Questions on who may appraise are short, and the wrong answers sound reasonable.

## 3. Simple explanation

*Source status: the methods and the rules on who may appraise come from the study manual. No official-source verification entry covers them, so they are taught as (manual; not yet verified). The tax depreciation facts marked V-77 are verified.*

### Step 1: What it represents

The **income capitalization approach** values income property by the income it produces. It rests on **anticipation** (L16-1): a buyer pays today for expected future benefits. An investor is really buying a stream of net income, and the price depends on the return the investor requires.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Potential gross income (PGI)** | Total yearly rent if every unit were rented all year and every tenant paid |
| **Vacancy and collection loss** | Rent lost to empty units (vacancy) and to tenants who do not pay (collection loss) |
| **Effective gross income (EGI)** | PGI − vacancy and collection loss |
| **Operating expenses** | Costs of running the property: **fixed** (do not change with occupancy, such as property taxes and insurance), **variable** (change with use, such as utilities, repairs, management) and **reserves for replacement** (money set aside each year for items that wear out, such as a roof or appliances) |
| **Net operating income (NOI)** | EGI − operating expenses |
| **Debt service** | Mortgage payments (principal and interest). **Not** an operating expense. |
| **Capitalization (cap) rate** | NOI ÷ value: the yearly return the market expects from this kind of property |

**IRV.** **I**ncome (NOI) = **R**ate × **V**alue. Cover the letter you want: **Value = NOI ÷ Rate**, Rate = NOI ÷ Value, NOI = Value × Rate.

**Why debt service is excluded.** NOI measures what the **property** earns, whoever owns it and however it is financed. If mortgage payments counted, two buyers with different loans would get two different values for the same building.

**Why depreciation is excluded.** Here "depreciation" means the income-tax deduction: residential rental property over **27.5 years**, nonresidential over **39 years**, straight-line, never on land (V-77, confirmed). It is a tax-accounting entry, not money spent running the property.

**Reconciliation** is the appraiser's last step: weighing the values given by the approaches, according to how reliable each one is **for this property**, to reach one final value. It is never a simple average. **USPAP** (the Uniform Standards of Professional Appraisal Practice) governs appraisal practice.

### Step 2: Units

- **PGI, EGI, operating expenses and NOI are yearly figures.** Monthly rent × 12 × number of units = PGI.
- **The cap rate is a yearly percentage.** Convert it to a decimal: 8.5% = 0.085.
- **GRM uses MONTHLY gross rent. GIM uses ANNUAL gross income.** For the same property, the GRM is 12 times the GIM (a GIM of 12 equals a GRM of 144).
- "Gross" means **before** any expenses or vacancy. That is why GRM and GIM are rougher shortcuts than NOI ÷ cap rate.

### Step 3: Formula

**NOI:** PGI − vacancy and collection loss = EGI; EGI − operating expenses (fixed + variable + reserves) = **NOI**

**IRV:** **Value = NOI ÷ cap rate**

**Gross rent multiplier:** GRM = price ÷ **monthly** gross rent → Value = monthly rent × GRM

**Gross income multiplier:** GIM = price ÷ **annual** gross income → Value = annual gross income × GIM

### Step 4: When it applies

- **Income approach (IRV):** income property, such as apartment buildings, offices and shops.
- **GRM:** houses and duplexes, using monthly rent. **GIM:** the same idea with annual gross income.
- **Operating expenses include:** property taxes and special assessments, hazard and liability insurance, repairs, maintenance, management, owner-paid utilities and reserves for replacement.
- **Never operating expenses:** **debt service** (mortgage principal and interest) and **depreciation**.
- **Reconciliation:** each approach gets the weight its reliability deserves. Sales comparison is best for homes and vacant land (L16-2), cost for new and special-purpose properties (L16-3), and income for income property.
- **Who may appraise** (manual; not yet verified). Broadly, a *federally related transaction* is a real estate financing transaction connected with federally regulated lenders; the exact federal definition and its exceptions are outside this course (requires verification).
  - A federally related transaction requires a **state-licensed or certified appraiser** following USPAP. A broker's experience, or following USPAP, does not qualify her: what stops her is the license class, not the standards.
  - A real estate licensee may prepare a **CMA** or **BPO** and **may charge for it**, but may **never call it an appraisal**.
  - Per the manual, a licensee who does appraisal work outside federally related transactions must still comply with USPAP.

### Step 5: Worked example

**A. NOI and value (Tallahassee).** Ten apartments rent for $1,400 a month each. Vacancy and collection loss: 6%. Yearly costs: property taxes $19,400; insurance $16,800; common-area utilities $4,320; repairs and maintenance $7,500; management $4,900; reserves for replacement $3,000. The owner's mortgage payments are $78,000 a year. Market cap rate: 8.5%.

1. PGI: 10 × $1,400 × 12 = $168,000.
2. Vacancy and collection loss: $168,000 × 0.06 = $10,080. EGI = $157,920.
3. Operating expenses: $19,400 + $16,800 + $4,320 + $7,500 + $4,900 + $3,000 = $55,920. The $78,000 of mortgage payments is **left out**.
4. NOI: $157,920 − $55,920 = $102,000.
5. Value: $102,000 ÷ 0.085 = **$1,200,000**. Check: $1,200,000 × 0.085 = $102,000.
6. **The trap:** subtracting the mortgage gives $24,000 ÷ 0.085 = $282,352.94, a value wrong by almost $918,000.

**B. GRM (Port St. Lucie).** A similar rental house sold for $322,000 and rents for $2,300 a month: GRM = $322,000 ÷ $2,300 = 140. The subject rents for $2,450 a month: $2,450 × 140 = **$343,000**.

**C. GIM.** A small office building sold for $1,440,000 with annual gross income of $160,000: GIM = 9. The subject's annual gross income is $175,000: $175,000 × 9 = **$1,575,000**.

**D. Reconciliation (Orlando home in a subdivision with many recent sales).**

| Approach | Indicated value | Reliability for this property |
|---|---|---|
| Sales comparison | $455,000 | High: many recent, close comps |
| Cost | $490,000 | Lower: a 20-year-old house, so depreciation is hard to measure |
| Income (GRM) | $430,000 | Low: few homes here are rented |

The appraiser gives most weight to sales comparison and reports a value at or near **$455,000**. The simple average, $458,333.33, is the trap answer.

### Step 6: Reasonableness check

- PGI > EGI > NOI. Each step down is smaller.
- The value is many times the NOI. A value smaller than the NOI means you multiplied by the rate instead of dividing.
- A **higher cap rate gives a lower value** for the same NOI: $102,000 ÷ 0.08 = $1,275,000, but $102,000 ÷ 0.09 = $1,133,333.33.
- A GRM answer about 12 times too large (millions for an ordinary rental house) means you used annual rent with a monthly multiplier.

### Step 7: Common errors

| Error | What it looks like | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Deducting debt service | NOI − mortgage payments | Mortgage payments are not operating expenses |
| Deducting depreciation | NOI − tax depreciation | Depreciation is not an operating expense |
| Capitalizing PGI or EGI | $168,000 ÷ 0.085 | Capitalize **NOI** only |
| Multiplying by the cap rate | $102,000 × 0.085 | Value = NOI **÷** rate |
| Annual rent with a GRM | $2,450 × 12 × 140 | GRM uses **monthly** rent |
| Monthly rent with a GIM | $14,583 × 9 | GIM uses **annual** income |
| Averaging the approaches | (455 + 490 + 430) ÷ 3 | Reconcile: weigh by reliability |

### Step 9: Independent practice

**I1.** An apartment building sold for $2,000,000. Its NOI is $150,000. What cap rate does the sale show?

<details><summary>Check your answer</summary>

Rate = NOI ÷ value = $150,000 ÷ $2,000,000 = 0.075 = <b>7.5%</b>.

</details>

**I2.** Eight units rent for $1,500 a month each. Vacancy and collection loss 5%. Property taxes $14,000; insurance $12,600; repairs $6,000; management $5,700; reserves $2,500. Mortgage payments $60,000 a year. Cap rate 7.5%. Value?

<details><summary>Check your answer</summary>

PGI: 8 × $1,500 × 12 = $144,000. Loss: $7,200. EGI: $136,800. Operating expenses: $40,800 (the mortgage is ignored). NOI: $96,000. Value: $96,000 ÷ 0.075 = <b>$1,280,000</b>.

</details>

**I3.** A strip shopping center sold for $2,100,000 with annual gross income of $210,000. The subject's annual gross income is $230,000. Indicated value by GIM?

<details><summary>Check your answer</summary>

GIM = $2,100,000 ÷ $210,000 = 10. Value: $230,000 × 10 = <b>$2,300,000</b>.

</details>

**I4.** A rental house sold for $360,000 and rents for $2,400 a month. The subject rents for $2,250 a month. Indicated value by GRM? What answer would annual rent produce?

<details><summary>Check your answer</summary>

GRM = $360,000 ÷ $2,400 = 150. Value: $2,250 × 150 = <b>$337,500</b>. With annual rent: $27,000 × 150 = $4,050,000, an obviously absurd figure.

</details>

**I5.** NOI is $84,000. What is the value at 7% and at 8%? Which rate gives the lower value?

<details><summary>Check your answer</summary>

$84,000 ÷ 0.07 = $1,200,000. $84,000 ÷ 0.08 = $1,050,000. The <b>higher rate (8%) gives the lower value</b>. Buyers who see more risk usually demand a higher rate.

</details>

**I6.** A broker with 20 years of experience who carefully follows USPAP is asked to appraise a home for a federally related loan. May she? What may she offer instead?

<details><summary>Check your answer</summary>

<b>No.</b> A federally related transaction requires a state-licensed or certified appraiser; her license class, not her method, is the barrier. She may prepare a CMA or BPO, may charge for it, and must never call it an appraisal (manual; not yet verified).

</details>

### Step 10: Exam application

1. **NOI questions:** PGI → minus vacancy and collection loss → EGI → minus operating expenses → NOI. Cross out debt service and depreciation before you start.
2. **IRV:** cover the unknown. Value = I ÷ R; Rate = I ÷ V; Income = V × R.
3. **GRM:** monthly rent. **GIM:** annual income. Read the period before you multiply.
4. **Reconciliation:** the answer always says "weigh" or "reliability", never "average", "highest" or "always sales comparison".
5. **Who may appraise:** federally related → state-licensed or certified appraiser. Experience plus USPAP is not enough.

## 4. Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Income capitalization approach | Value = NOI ÷ cap rate; for income property |
| IRV | Income = Rate × Value |
| PGI / EGI | Potential gross income / effective gross income (after vacancy and collection loss) |
| Operating expenses | Fixed, variable and reserves for replacement; not debt service or depreciation |
| NOI | Net operating income: EGI minus operating expenses |
| Capitalization rate | NOI ÷ value |
| Debt service | Mortgage principal and interest payments |
| GRM / GIM | Price ÷ monthly rent / price ÷ annual gross income |
| Reconciliation | Weighing the approaches to reach a final value estimate (not averaging) |
| USPAP | Uniform Standards of Professional Appraisal Practice |
| Federally related transaction | Broadly, financing connected with federally regulated lenders; requires a state-licensed or certified appraiser |
| CMA / BPO | Comparative market analysis / broker price opinion: a licensee's chargeable price opinion, never an "appraisal" |

## 5. Practical example

**A fourplex in St. Petersburg.** Your investor client, Mr. Teixeira, wants to buy a fourplex. Each unit rents for $1,650 a month. You prepare an income analysis for him:

| Line | Amount |
|---|---|
| PGI: 4 × $1,650 × 12 | $79,200 |
| − Vacancy and collection loss (5%) | −$3,960 |
| = EGI | $75,240 |
| − Property taxes $9,800 · insurance $11,400 · repairs and maintenance $4,500 · management $5,000 · water, sewer and trash $3,240 · reserves $2,300 | −$36,240 |
| **= NOI** | **$39,000** |
| Value at a 6.5% cap rate: $39,000 ÷ 0.065 | **$600,000** |
| Cross-check: GRM of recent fourplex sales 90 × $6,600 monthly rent | $594,000 |

Mr. Teixeira wants to subtract his planned mortgage payment of $3,100 a month. You explain that debt service is not an operating expense: it affects **his** cash flow, not the building's value.

His lender then says the loan is a federally related transaction and orders an appraisal. Mr. Teixeira asks you to "just sign it as the appraisal, since you already did the math". You cannot: only a state-licensed or certified appraiser may appraise for a federally related transaction, and your analysis may never be called an appraisal (manual; not yet verified). The appraisal later shows sales comparison $590,000, income $600,000 and cost $640,000 (an older building, so depreciation is hard to measure). The appraiser weighs income and sales comparison most and reconciles to $597,000, not the $610,000 average.

## 6. Portugal comparison

**The familiar principle.** If you have quoted investors a **gross yield** (annual rent ÷ price), you already know the GIM upside down: a 5% gross yield is the same as a GIM of 20. Valuing an investment by its net income is general finance, not national law.

**The Florida rule.** The exam uses IRV with NOI, excludes debt service and depreciation, uses GRM with monthly rent and GIM with annual income, and treats reconciliation as weighing, not averaging. Federally related transactions require a state-licensed or certified appraiser (manual; not yet verified).

**The precise difference.** This course has no verified Portugal–Florida comparison entry on income valuation or on who may value property for lenders. Any Portuguese rule on valuers, bank valuations or price opinions by agents **requires verification**.

**Exam implication.** Answer who-may-appraise questions only with the Florida rule above. Do not carry over any assumption about who signs valuations elsewhere.

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"The mortgage payment is an operating expense."** No. Debt service is never deducted to find NOI.
- **"A higher cap rate means a higher value."** No. For the same NOI, a higher rate means a lower value.
- **"The final value is the average of the three approaches."** No. Reconciliation weighs them by reliability.
- **"An experienced broker who follows USPAP may appraise for a federally related loan."** No. The license class is the barrier.
- **"A licensee's CMA can be called an appraisal if the client pays for it."** No. A licensee may charge for a CMA or BPO but may never label it an appraisal.
