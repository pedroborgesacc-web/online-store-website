## 2. Why it matters

**In practice.** Investors buying Florida duplexes, small apartment buildings or shops talk in **NOI** (net operating income) and **cap rates**, and a seller's flyer often makes both look better than they are. You need to rebuild the numbers yourself. You also need to know where your role ends: you may give a price opinion, but some transactions require an appraisal by an appraiser.

**On the exam.** Appraisal is **8%** of the exam (V-02, confirmed), so about 8 of the 100 questions (V-01). Expect an IRV calculation from raw income and expenses, a GRM question, a reconciliation question and a "who may appraise" question. The manual warns of three planted errors that keep coming back: adjusting the subject (L16-2), treating mortgage payments as operating expenses, and averaging the three approaches.

## 3. Simple explanation

> **Source status.** The income approach, the multipliers, reconciliation and the "who may appraise" rules come from the study manual. No verification entry covers them, so they are taught as **(manual; not yet verified)**. The tax depreciation facts used for contrast are verified (V-77). The arithmetic itself needs no verification.

### Step 1: What it represents

The **income capitalization approach** values a property by the income it produces. It rests on **anticipation** (L16-1): an investor pays today for the income expected in the future. It is the approach for **income property**: rentals and commercial buildings.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Potential gross income (PGI)** | All the income the property could earn in a year if fully rented and every tenant paid |
| **Vacancy and collection loss** | Income lost to empty units and unpaid rent |
| **Effective gross income (EGI)** | PGI − vacancy and collection loss |
| **Operating expenses** | Costs of running the property: **fixed** (property taxes, insurance), **variable** (utilities, repairs, maintenance, management) and **reserves for replacement** (money set aside for items such as roofs and appliances) |
| **Net operating income (NOI)** | EGI − operating expenses |
| **Capitalization (cap) rate** | NOI ÷ value: the yearly return investors expect from the property's income |
| **Debt service** | Mortgage payments (principal and interest) |
| **GRM / GIM** | Gross rent multiplier / gross income multiplier: quick shortcuts from gross income to value |

### Step 2: Units

- In the NOI build, every figure is **annual** dollars. Convert monthly rent: × 12.
- A vacancy percentage is applied to **PGI**.
- The cap rate is a yearly percentage. Turn it into a decimal before dividing: 7.2% = 0.072.
- **GRM uses monthly** gross rent. **GIM uses annual** gross income. Both multipliers are plain numbers, without a dollar sign.

### Step 3: Formula

**Build NOI:** PGI − vacancy and collection loss = **EGI**; EGI − operating expenses (fixed + variable + reserves) = **NOI**.

**IRV:** **I**ncome = **R**ate × **V**alue. Cover the letter you want:

| To find | Formula |
|---|---|
| Value | NOI ÷ cap rate |
| Cap rate | NOI ÷ value (or sale price) |
| Income (NOI) | value × cap rate |

**Multipliers:** GRM = price ÷ **monthly** gross rent, so value = monthly rent × GRM. GIM = price ÷ **annual** gross income, so value = annual gross income × GIM.

| Deduct as operating expenses | Never deduct |
|---|---|
| Property taxes and special assessments | **Debt service** (mortgage principal and interest) |
| Hazard and liability insurance | **Depreciation** |
| Utilities, repairs, maintenance, management | |
| Reserves for replacement | |

**Why these two are excluded.** The building produces the same income whether the buyer pays cash or borrows, so financing (debt service) cannot change the property's value. Depreciation is a tax deduction (residential rental 27.5 years, nonresidential 39 years, V-77), not money spent running the building.

### Step 4: When it applies

- **Income approach:** income property (manual).
- **GRM:** a rougher shortcut for **houses and duplexes** (manual). **GIM:** the same idea with **annual** gross income. Neither looks at expenses or vacancy, which is why they are rough.

**Reconciliation.** The appraiser's last step is **reconciliation**: weighing the approaches by their **reliability for this property** to reach one final value. It is **never a simple average**. It is also not "always take the highest" or "always use sales comparison". Homes lean on sales comparison (L16-2), new or special-purpose buildings on cost (L16-3), and income property on income.

**USPAP.** The **Uniform Standards of Professional Appraisal Practice** govern appraisal practice.

**Who may appraise.** A **federally related transaction** is, in plain terms, a real estate financing transaction covered by federal financial regulators that requires an appraisal (for example, a mortgage loan from a federally regulated lender that needs one). This course's sources do not give the detailed federal definition (requires verification); on the exam, the phrase itself is your cue.

| Work | Who may do it | May charge? | May be called an "appraisal"? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Appraisal for a **federally related transaction** | A **state-licensed or certified appraiser**, following USPAP | Yes | Yes |
| **CMA** (comparative market analysis): sold, listed and expired comparables shape a price range | A real estate licensee | **Yes** | **Never** |
| **BPO** (broker price opinion): ordered by a lender or investor, e.g. for a short sale (a sale for less than the loan balance) or a portfolio check | A real estate licensee | **Yes** | **Never** |

The manual's trap: "an experienced broker may appraise for a federally related transaction if she follows USPAP." **No.** The licence class is the barrier, not the standards. Per the manual, a licensee who does appraisal work **outside** federally related transactions must still comply with USPAP (manual; not yet verified).

### Step 5: Worked example

**A. NOI and IRV: an 8-unit building in Jacksonville.** Each unit rents for $1,750 per month.

| Line | Working | Amount |
|---|---|---|
| PGI | 8 × $1,750 × 12 | $168,000 |
| − Vacancy and collection loss | 5% × $168,000 | −$8,400 |
| **= EGI** | | **$159,600** |
| − Property taxes (fixed) | | −$18,500 |
| − Insurance (fixed) | | −$14,200 |
| − Utilities, common areas (variable) | | −$6,300 |
| − Repairs and maintenance (variable) | | −$9,800 |
| − Management (variable) | | −$9,600 |
| − Reserves for replacement | | −$4,000 |
| **= NOI** | $159,600 − $62,400 | **$97,200** |

The owner's statement also lists mortgage payments of $58,000 and a tax depreciation deduction of $24,000. **Ignore both.**

Cap rate from a comparable: a similar building sold for $1,250,000 with an NOI of $90,000. R = $90,000 ÷ $1,250,000 = 0.072 = **7.2%**.

Value: $97,200 ÷ 0.072 = **$1,350,000**.

*The trap:* deducting the mortgage gives an NOI of $39,200 and a "value" of about $544,444, which is badly wrong.

**B. GRM: a single-family rental in Orlando.** A comparable rental house sold for $297,000 while renting for $2,200 per month: GRM = $297,000 ÷ $2,200 = **135**. The subject rents for $2,300 per month: $2,300 × 135 = **$310,500**.

**C. GIM: a small retail building.** Annual gross income is $96,000; similar buildings sell at a GIM of 9.5. Value: $96,000 × 9.5 = **$912,000**.

**D. Reconciliation: a Gainesville duplex about 40 years old.**

| Approach | Indication | Reliability for this property |
|---|---|---|
| Sales comparison | $612,000 | High: several recent nearby duplex sales |
| Income | $598,000 | High: duplex buyers buy rent; good rent and expense data |
| Cost | $655,000 | Low: an old building, with much depreciation to estimate |

The appraiser gives most weight to sales comparison and income and reconciles to a figure such as **$605,000**, explaining why. The simple average ($621,667) would be pulled up by the least reliable approach.

### Step 6: Reasonableness check

- **PGI > EGI > NOI**, always.
- **Work backwards:** value × rate = NOI. $1,350,000 × 0.072 = $97,200 ✓.
- **Rate up, value down.** The same $97,200 at 8% gives $1,215,000, less than at 7.2%.
- **Size:** at 7.2%, the value is about 13.9 times NOI (1 ÷ 0.072). If your value is smaller than the NOI, you multiplied instead of dividing. If it is about 12 times too small, you used monthly income.
- **Multipliers:** when rent is the only income, a GRM is 12 times the GIM for the same property.

### Step 7: Common errors

| Error | Correction |
|---|---|
| Deducting mortgage payments (debt service) | Never an operating expense |
| Deducting depreciation | Never an operating expense |
| Skipping vacancy and collection loss | PGI − vacancy = EGI comes first |
| Using monthly rent in the NOI build | Annual figures: × 12 |
| Dividing by 7.2 instead of 0.072 | Convert the percentage to a decimal |
| NOI × rate to find value | Value = NOI **÷** rate |
| Applying a GRM to annual rent | GRM = monthly; GIM = annual |
| Averaging the three approaches | Reconcile: weigh by reliability |
| "A broker following USPAP may appraise for a federally related transaction" | Only a state-licensed or certified appraiser |

### Step 9: Independent practice

**I1.** Six units at $1,600 per month each. Vacancy and collection loss: 5%. Yearly costs: property taxes $12,400; insurance $9,800; repairs and maintenance $6,900; management $5,500; reserves $2,400; mortgage principal and interest $41,000; owner's tax depreciation $9,000. Cap rate: 8%. Find the value.

<details><summary>Check your answer</summary>

PGI: 6 × $1,600 × 12 = $115,200. Vacancy: 5% = $5,760. EGI: $109,440. Operating expenses (taxes, insurance, repairs, management, reserves): $37,000. Mortgage and depreciation are excluded. NOI: $109,440 − $37,000 = $72,440. Value: $72,440 ÷ 0.08 = **$905,500**.

</details>

**I2.** A small office building sold for $1,140,000 with annual gross income of $120,000. Find its GIM, then value a similar building with annual gross income of $134,000.

<details><summary>Check your answer</summary>

GIM: $1,140,000 ÷ $120,000 = **9.5**. Value: $134,000 × 9.5 = **$1,273,000**.

</details>

**I3.** A duplex has two units at $1,400 per month each. Similar duplexes sell at a GRM of 120. Find the value, then the GIM that this value implies.

<details><summary>Check your answer</summary>

Monthly rent: 2 × $1,400 = $2,800. Value: $2,800 × 120 = **$336,000**. Annual rent: $2,800 × 12 = $33,600. GIM: $336,000 ÷ $33,600 = **10** (= 120 ÷ 12).

</details>

**I4.** (a) A lender in a federally related transaction that requires an appraisal asks your broker, who has 20 years of experience and follows USPAP, to appraise the property. Allowed? (b) A lender considering a short sale asks your broker for a BPO and offers a fee. Allowed? What may it never be called?

<details><summary>Check your answer</summary>

(a) **No.** A federally related transaction requires a **state-licensed or certified appraiser**; experience and USPAP do not change the licence class. (b) **Yes.** A licensee may prepare a BPO and charge for it, but may **never** call it an appraisal.

</details>

### Step 10: Exam application

| If the question… | Answer pattern |
|---|---|
| Gives rents, vacancy, expenses and a rate | PGI → EGI → NOI → ÷ rate; leave out mortgage payments and depreciation |
| Gives a rent and a multiplier | Monthly rent × GRM, or annual income × GIM |
| Asks how the final value is reached | Reconciliation: weigh by reliability, never average |
| Asks who appraises for a federally related transaction | A state-licensed or certified appraiser (not a broker, however experienced) |
| Asks about a licensee's CMA or BPO | Allowed and chargeable, never called an appraisal |

## 4. Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Income capitalization approach | Value from income; for income property; IRV |
| PGI | Potential gross income: full rent if fully occupied and paid |
| Vacancy and collection loss | Income lost to empty units and unpaid rent |
| EGI | Effective gross income = PGI − vacancy and collection loss |
| Operating expenses | Fixed, variable and reserves; not debt service or depreciation |
| NOI | Net operating income = EGI − operating expenses |
| Capitalization rate | NOI ÷ value |
| IRV | Income = Rate × Value; Value = NOI ÷ rate |
| GRM / GIM | Price ÷ monthly gross rent / price ÷ annual gross income |
| Reconciliation | Weighing the approaches to reach a final value (not averaging) |
| USPAP | Uniform Standards of Professional Appraisal Practice |
| Federally related transaction | A financing transaction under federal regulators' rules that requires an appraisal; the appraiser must be state-licensed or certified |
| CMA / BPO | A licensee's pricing analysis / a broker price opinion for a lender or investor: chargeable, never an "appraisal" |

## 5. Practical example

**Setting.** Sofia, an investor from Porto, asks you about a fourplex in St. Petersburg listed at $860,000. The seller's flyer says: "4 units × $2,100 = $8,400/month. Taxes $15,200, insurance $13,900. NOI $71,700, cap rate 8.3%. Mortgage payments $38,000."

**Your rebuild:**

| Line | Amount |
|---|---|
| PGI: $8,400 × 12 | $100,800 |
| − Vacancy and collection loss (5%; the flyer ignored it) | −$5,040 |
| = EGI | $95,760 |
| − Taxes $15,200, insurance $13,900 | −$29,100 |
| − Repairs and maintenance $6,100, management $7,660, reserves $2,400 (all missing from the flyer) | −$16,160 |
| **= NOI** | **$50,500** |

The flyer's NOI took only taxes and insurance off the full rent. The mortgage payments are correctly left out of NOI, but they are not the reason for the gap. Recent fourplex sales nearby show a cap rate of 6.25%: $50,500 ÷ 0.0625 = **$808,000**. As a rougher check, those sales show a GIM of 8.5: $100,800 × 8.5 = $856,800. The GIM ignores expenses, so you rely more on the income figure.

Sofia then asks: "Can you give me an appraisal?" You may prepare a price opinion and charge for it, but never call it an appraisal. If her loan is a federally related transaction, the lender's appraisal must come from a state-licensed or certified appraiser. And her own mortgage payments will never enter the property's NOI.

## 6. Portugal comparison

**The familiar principle.** Investors everywhere compare rent with price; you may know this as a "gross yield" (annual rent ÷ price). That is the GIM turned upside down: a GIM of 10 is a 10% gross yield.

**The Florida rule.** The exam distinguishes a gross measure (GRM on **monthly** rent, GIM on **annual** income) from the **cap rate**, which uses **NOI** after vacancy and operating expenses but before debt service and depreciation. The appraisal for a federally related transaction requires a state-licensed or certified appraiser.

**The precise difference.** This course has no verified Portugal–Florida comparison entry on income valuation or on who may value property. Any Portuguese rule on who may value property for banks, courts or tax purposes **requires verification**.

**Exam implication.** Do not confuse a gross yield with a cap rate. Answer "who may appraise" questions with the Florida/US rule only; never let a Portuguese rule answer a Florida question.

## 7. Legal and numerical requirements

**Memorize exactly**

*Appraisal method and appraisal-practice rules: manual; not yet verified (no verification entry covers them).*

- Income capitalization approach = **income property**. **IRV: Value = NOI ÷ cap rate.**
- **PGI − vacancy and collection loss = EGI; EGI − operating expenses (fixed, variable, reserves) = NOI.**
- **Debt service and depreciation are not operating expenses.**
- **GRM** = price ÷ **monthly** gross rent (houses, duplexes). **GIM** = price ÷ **annual** gross income.
- **Reconciliation**: weigh the approaches by reliability for the property; **never a simple average**.
- **USPAP** governs appraisal practice.
- **Federally related transactions** require a **state-licensed or certified appraiser**.
- A licensee may prepare **CMAs and BPOs** and **may charge** for them, but may **never** label them appraisals.
- Per the manual, a licensee doing appraisal work outside federally related transactions must still follow **USPAP**.

*Verified:*

- Appraisal = **8%** of the exam (V-02).
- Tax depreciation periods (27.5 / 39 years, V-77) belong to income tax, not to NOI.

**Understand**

- Why financing and depreciation stay out of NOI.
- Why a higher cap rate means a lower value.
- Why reconciliation weighs instead of averaging.
- Why experience and USPAP knowledge do not make a broker an appraiser for a federally related transaction: the licence class is the barrier.
- Do not confuse "federally related transaction" (the appraisal rule) with RESPA's "federally related mortgage loans" on 1–4 family residential property (V-53): different law, different purpose.

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"Mortgage payments are operating expenses."** No. Debt service is never deducted to find NOI.
- **"Depreciation is an operating expense."** No. It is a tax deduction (V-77), not a cost of running the building.
- **"GRM uses annual rent."** No. GRM uses **monthly** rent; GIM uses **annual** income.
- **"A higher cap rate means a higher value."** No. Value = NOI ÷ rate, so a higher rate gives a lower value.
- **"The final value is the average of the three approaches."** No. Reconciliation weighs them by reliability.
- **"An experienced broker who follows USPAP may appraise for a federally related transaction."** No. A state-licensed or certified appraiser is required.
- **"A licensee may not charge for a CMA or BPO."** No. A licensee may charge, but may never call the work an appraisal.

## 10. Memory sheet

- **IRV**: Value = NOI ÷ rate; Rate = NOI ÷ value; NOI = value × rate.
- PGI − vacancy/collection = EGI − operating expenses (fixed, variable, reserves) = **NOI**.
- **NOI excludes debt service and depreciation.**
- **GRM monthly** (houses, duplexes); **GIM annual**.
- Rate up → value down.
- **Reconcile, don't average.**
- **USPAP** governs appraisal.
- Federally related transaction → **state-licensed or certified appraiser**.
- CMA / BPO: may charge, **never** "appraisal".
