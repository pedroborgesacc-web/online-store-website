## 2. Why it matters

In daily brokerage work you identify a property by its street address. In Florida that is not enough for the documents that move ownership. A valid deed must contain a **legal description**: words that identify one parcel of land so precisely that it cannot be confused with any other (L09-2; manual; not yet verified). A written listing agreement must also contain a description of the property (s. 475.25(1)(r); V-49, verified). Agents usually take the legal description from the seller's recorded deed or title documents. A wrong lot number or a missing fraction can describe the wrong land, so copy it exactly.

On the exam, Section 10 "Legal Descriptions" carries about 5% of the questions (V-02), which is about five of the 100 questions (V-01). This lesson covers the concepts. The next lesson (L10-2) covers the arithmetic. Typical concept questions:

- Which system must begin and end at the same point?
- Which system describes most homes in platted urban subdivisions?
- Where do Florida's principal meridian and base line intersect?
- Is a street address a legal description? (Never.)

## 3. Simple explanation

### What a legal description is, and why an address is not one

A **legal description** identifies a specific parcel so that a surveyor could find its boundaries on the ground. Florida exam materials use **three systems**: **metes and bounds**, **lot and block**, and the **government (rectangular) survey** (manual; not yet verified).

A **street address is never a legal description** (manual; not yet verified). The reason: an address tells a mail carrier which door to go to, but it says nothing about where the boundary lines are. Streets can be renamed and numbers can change. One address can cover several parcels, and vacant land often has no address at all.

### System 1: Metes and bounds

"Metes" means measurements and "bounds" means boundaries. The description "walks" the boundary of the parcel:

1. It starts at a **point of beginning (POB)**.
2. It follows **courses** (directions, such as "North" or "North 45 degrees East") and **distances** (in feet) from one corner to the next.
3. It **returns to the POB**.

Why must it return to the POB? A boundary line that does not close back on itself does not enclose any land. The return to the POB is what turns a list of lines into a parcel. Metes and bounds is **used for irregular parcels**, whose shape does not fit a grid or a subdivision map (manual; not yet verified).

### System 2: Lot and block

When land is divided into a subdivision, a **plat** is prepared: a map of the subdivision showing its blocks, lots and streets. The plat is then **recorded** in the public records. From that moment each lot can be described simply by its lot number, its block number, the subdivision name and a reference to the recorded plat. Lot and block is the **usual description in platted urban subdivisions**, so most of the homes you will sell are described this way (manual; not yet verified).

The condition that matters is a **recorded plat**. Without a recorded plat, "Lot 14, Block C" points to nothing.

### System 3: Government (rectangular) survey

Imagine a giant grid laid over the land, measured from two reference lines:

- a **principal meridian**, a reference line running north–south;
- a **base line**, a reference line running east–west.

**Florida's principal meridian and base line intersect in Tallahassee** (N-105; manual; not yet verified, V-26). From these lines the land is divided into **townships** of 6 × 6 miles, and each township into **36 sections** of 1 square mile (640 acres) (manual; not yet verified). A government survey description names a fraction of a section, the section number and its township. L10-2 teaches the numbering and the arithmetic.

Real descriptions also say where the township lies relative to the two lines, for example "Township 12 South, Range 17 East": the township row counted south of the base line and the column (called a **range**) counted east of the principal meridian. *This range vocabulary is standard survey usage but is not covered by the course sources; not yet verified.*

### The three systems side by side

| | Metes and bounds | Lot and block | Government survey |
|---|---|---|---|
| Starting reference | A **point of beginning (POB)** | A **recorded plat** | A **principal meridian** and **base line** (Florida: Tallahassee) |
| How land is described | Courses and distances, **returning to the POB** | Lot number in a block on the recorded plat | Townships, sections and fractions of sections |
| Typical use | **Irregular parcels** | **Platted urban subdivisions** | Land described by sections (see L10-2) |
| Clue words in a question | "point of beginning", "thence North ... feet" | "Lot", "Block", "plat" | "Section", "Township", "NW¼" |

All rows: manual; not yet verified.

## 4. Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Legal description | Words that identify one specific parcel precisely enough to locate its boundaries. Required in a deed |
| Metes and bounds | Description using a point of beginning, courses (directions) and distances, returning to the POB |
| Point of beginning (POB) | The starting and ending point of a metes-and-bounds description |
| Course | A direction in a metes-and-bounds description, such as "North 45 degrees East" |
| Distance | The length of each boundary line, in feet |
| Lot and block | Description by lot and block numbers on a recorded plat |
| Plat | Recorded map of a subdivision showing its blocks, lots and streets |
| Recorded | Entered in the public records, so that anyone can find and rely on it |
| Government (rectangular) survey | System of townships and sections measured from principal meridians and base lines |
| Principal meridian | The north–south reference line of the government survey |
| Base line | The east–west reference line of the government survey |
| Township | A 6 × 6 mile square containing 36 sections |
| Section | 1 square mile, 640 acres |

## 5. Practical example

**Luís**, a buyer from Porto, asks you to help him buy three Florida properties. You look at the legal description in each seller's recorded deed.

| Property | Legal description (illustrative) | System |
|---|---|---|
| A house in an Orlando subdivision, "4127 Heron Pointe Drive" | "Lot 14, Block C, HERON POINTE, according to the plat thereof recorded in Plat Book 62, Page 115, of the Public Records of Orange County, Florida" | **Lot and block** |
| 40 acres of pasture in Levy County, no street address | "The SW¼ of the NE¼ of Section 22, Township 12 South, Range 17 East" | **Government survey** |
| An irregular lot on the St. Johns River | "Beginning at the point of beginning (POB) on the north line of River Road; thence North 200 feet; thence East 300 feet; thence South 36°52' East 250 feet along the river bank; thence West 450 feet along River Road back to the POB" | **Metes and bounds** |

The names, plat book and page numbers, and distances above are invented for illustration.

What you explain to Luís:

1. **The house** sits in a platted subdivision, so its description refers to the recorded plat. "4127 Heron Pointe Drive" is the mailing address only. It appears in marketing, but the deed must carry the lot-and-block description.
2. **The pasture** has no address at all. The government survey locates it within a section of a township measured from Florida's principal meridian and base line, which intersect in Tallahassee (manual; not yet verified). In L10-2 you will calculate that "the SW¼ of the NE¼" of a section is 40 acres.
3. **The riverfront lot** is irregular, so it is described by metes and bounds. Check that the description ends "back to the POB". If the last course does not return to the starting point, the boundary does not close and the parcel is not properly described. Ask the title company or a surveyor to review it.

## 6. Portugal comparison

**The familiar principle.** Any system that transfers land needs a way to identify exactly which land is transferred. How Portuguese documents identify a property is not covered by the course's comparison entries: *requires verification*. Do not rely on it for any Florida answer.

**The Florida rule.** The deed carries a legal description in one of three systems: metes and bounds, lot and block (recorded plat) or the government survey (Tallahassee origin). A street address never qualifies (manual; not yet verified).

**The precise difference that is verified for this course (PT-17).** Units. Portugal uses the metric system (m², hectares; 1 ha = 10,000 m²). Florida descriptions use US customary units: distances in **feet**, areas in **square feet** and **acres**, and **miles** for townships and sections. For intuition only: 1 acre ≈ 4,047 m² ≈ 0.405 ha, and 1 mile ≈ 1.61 km.

**Exam implication.** Answer with the three Florida system names and Florida's survey origin (Tallahassee). Read every distance in a metes-and-bounds description in feet. Exam math never uses metric (PT-17).

## 7. Legal and numerical requirements

**Memorize exactly**

- Three systems: **metes and bounds**, **lot and block**, **government (rectangular) survey** (manual; not yet verified).
- Metes and bounds: **POB → courses (directions) and distances → back to the POB**. Used for **irregular parcels** (manual; not yet verified).
- Lot and block: a lot number in a block on a **recorded plat**. The usual description in **platted urban subdivisions** (manual; not yet verified).
- Government survey: **townships and sections** measured from a **principal meridian** and **base line**. **Florida's intersect in Tallahassee** (N-105; manual; not yet verified, V-26).
- A **street address is never a legal description** (manual; not yet verified).
- A valid deed requires a legal description (L09-2; manual; not yet verified). A written listing agreement must include a property description (s. 475.25(1)(r); V-49, verified).

**Understand**

- Each system answers the same question, "which land, exactly?", from a different starting reference: a point (POB), a map (plat) or a grid (meridian and base line).
- Metes and bounds must close: without the return to the POB, no area is enclosed.
- Lot and block depends entirely on the recorded plat. The description is only as good as the map it points to.
- The government survey is a grid, so its parcels are described by section numbers and fractions. That is why it lends itself to arithmetic (L10-2).
- Source status: everything in this lesson except the listing-agreement rule (V-49) comes from the study manual and is labelled "not yet verified" under V-26.

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"The street address is enough for the deed."** No. A street address is never a legal description.
- **"Metes and bounds just has to list the boundary lines."** The lines must start at the POB and **return to the POB**. That is the classic exam answer to "which description begins and ends at the same point?"
- **"Lot and block works for any parcel."** Only for land shown on a **recorded plat**.
- **"Most subdivision homes are described by metes and bounds."** The usual description in platted urban subdivisions is **lot and block**. Metes and bounds is for irregular parcels.
- **"Florida's survey starts in Miami, Orlando or Jacksonville."** Florida's principal meridian and base line intersect in **Tallahassee**.
- **"I can read the distances in metres, as at home."** Florida descriptions use feet and acres (PT-17), and Portuguese practice never answers a Florida question.
