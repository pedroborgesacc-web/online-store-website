## 2. Why it matters

**In practice.** Whenever you price a Florida home, you do a rough sales comparison: you look at what similar homes sold for and ask how this one is different. Appraisers do the same work formally, on an **adjustment grid**. When a lender's appraisal arrives, or a seller argues with your CMA, you need to read that grid and explain why each figure was added or subtracted.

**On the exam.** Appraisal is **8%** of the exam (V-02, confirmed), so about 8 of the 100 questions (V-01). Sales comparison questions are short and predictable. You will be asked which way to adjust, what one comparable's adjusted price is, and which comparable deserves the most weight. The wrong answers are built on two mistakes: adjusting the subject, and adjusting in the wrong direction.

## 3. Simple explanation

*Source status: the method in this lesson comes from the study manual. No official-source verification entry covers appraisal methods, so the rules are taught as (manual; not yet verified). The arithmetic itself needs no verification.*

### Step 1: What it represents

The **sales comparison approach** estimates a property's value from the prices of similar properties that sold recently. It rests on **substitution** (L16-1): an informed buyer will not pay more than the cost of an equally desirable substitute. So recent sales of similar homes show what this home is worth.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Subject (property)** | The property being valued. Its value is the unknown. |
| **Comparable ("comp")** | A similar property that sold recently. Its sale price is known. |
| **Adjustment** | A dollar amount for one difference between a comp and the subject |
| **Adjusted sale price** | The comp's price after all adjustments: the subject's value **as indicated by that comp** |

**Why you adjust the comparable, never the subject.** The subject has no price yet: its value is what you are looking for. The comp has a known price. So you ask one question: *"If this comp were an exact twin of the subject, what would it have sold for?"*

- The comp has something the subject lacks. Its price includes that extra, so you remove it: **subtract**.
- The comp lacks something the subject has. Its price is too low for a twin, so you **add**.

Mnemonic: **CBS** = Comparable Better, Subtract. **CIA** = Comparable Inferior, Add.

**Best for:** homes and vacant land. They sell often enough to give plenty of recent, similar sales. Where sales are scarce, such as special-purpose buildings, the cost approach (L16-3) works better.

### Step 2: Units

- Sale prices and adjustments are in **dollars**.
- Size is the living area in **square feet (sq ft)**. A size difference is often priced per square foot: 150 sq ft × $100/sq ft = $15,000. *(Metric aside: 1 m² ≈ 10.76 sq ft, so 150 sq ft ≈ 14 m².)*
- Every adjustment has a sign: **−** when the comp is better, **+** when the comp is inferior. Write the sign each time.
- An adjustment equals what the feature **adds to market value** (contribution, L16-1), not what it cost to build. A pool that cost $85,000 may justify an adjustment of only $25,000. In exam questions the feature's value is given.

### Step 3: Formula

**Adjusted sale price = comp's sale price − value of features the comp has and the subject lacks + value of features the subject has and the comp lacks**

The question nearly always gives the value of each feature. Your job is the direction (+ or −) and the sum.

### Step 4: When it applies

- **Use recent sales of similar properties.** An old sale reflects an old market.
- **Adjust each difference separately:** a pool, a garage stall, a bathroom, size, location (for example a canal lot against an inland lot) or condition.
- **Weigh the comps.** After adjusting, the comparable with the **fewest and smallest adjustments** deserves the **most weight**. The reason: every adjustment is an estimate and may be wrong, so a comp that needs little adjusting is already close to a twin of the subject. Judge "smallest" by the **size** of each adjustment, ignoring its sign. A +$20,000 and a −$20,000 cancel out on paper, but they are $40,000 of estimates.
- **Bracketing.** The adjusted prices of good comps form a range around the subject's value; the manual says the adjusted comparables "bracket" it.
- **Not a simple average.** The final figure is a judgment weighted toward the most similar comps. This is the same idea as reconciliation (L16-4).

### Step 5: Worked example

**A. One comp (the manual's grid example).** A comp sold for **$410,000**. It has a pool worth $25,000 that the subject lacks. The subject has a renovated kitchen worth $15,000 that the comp lacks.

1. Pool: the comp is better → **−$25,000**.
2. Kitchen: the comp is inferior → **+$15,000**.
3. $410,000 − $25,000 + $15,000 = **$400,000**, the value indicated by this comp.

**B. A three-comp grid.** Subject in Brandon (Tampa area): 3 bedrooms, 2 baths, 1,850 sq ft, two-car garage, no pool, no lake view. Values of the differences: pool $30,000; lake view $20,000; third bathroom $12,000; second garage stall $8,000; size $110/sq ft.

| | Comp A | Comp B | Comp C |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sale price | $384,000 | $410,000 | $419,000 |
| Pool | None (same) | Has one: −$30,000 | None (same) |
| Size | Same | 1,750 sq ft, 100 smaller: +$11,000 | Same |
| Garage | One-car: +$8,000 | Same | One-car: +$8,000 |
| Lake view | No (same) | No (same) | Has one: −$20,000 |
| Bathrooms | Same | Same | Has 3: −$12,000 |
| **Adjusted price** | **$392,000** | **$391,000** | **$395,000** |
| Number of adjustments | 1 | 2 | 3 |
| Total size of adjustments | $8,000 | $41,000 | $40,000 |

1. Comp A: $384,000 + $8,000 = $392,000.
2. Comp B: 100 × $110 = $11,000. $410,000 − $30,000 + $11,000 = $391,000.
3. Comp C: $419,000 − $20,000 − $12,000 + $8,000 = $395,000.
4. Bracket: $391,000 to $395,000.
5. **Most weight: Comp A**, with the fewest (one) and smallest ($8,000) adjustments. The evidence supports a value close to $392,000. The simple average, $392,666.67, is not the method.

### Step 6: Reasonableness check

- Did the comp's price go **down** for every extra the comp has, and **up** for every feature it lacks?
- Did you change the subject? You should not have.
- Adjusted prices of good comps sit **closer together** than the raw sale prices. Above, the raw prices spread $35,000 ($384,000 to $419,000); the adjusted prices spread $4,000. If adjusting pushes the comps further apart, check your signs.
- The final estimate lies inside the bracket, near the most similar comp.

### Step 7: Common errors

| Error | What it looks like | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Adjusting the subject | "The subject lacks a pool, so subtract $25,000 from the subject" | Adjust the comp: it has the pool, so subtract from the comp's price |
| Reversed sign | The comp has a pool → add $25,000 | Comp better → **subtract** |
| Using what a feature cost | The pool cost $85,000 → −$85,000 | Use what it adds to value (the figure given) |
| Weighting the highest-priced comp | "Comp C sold highest, so it counts most" | Weight the comp with the fewest and smallest adjustments |
| Judging similarity by the net adjustment | "+$20,000 and −$20,000 means no adjustment" | Add up the sizes of the adjustments |
| Averaging the adjusted prices | ($392,000 + $391,000 + $395,000) ÷ 3 | Weight toward the most similar comp |

### Step 8: Guided practice

**G1.** A comp in Ocala sold for $356,000. It has a screened lanai (a covered, screened patio, very common in Florida) worth $9,000 that the subject lacks. The subject has a two-car garage and the comp a one-car garage, a difference worth $7,000. What is the comp's adjusted price? *Hint: one subtraction and one addition.*

<details><summary>Check your answer</summary>

Lanai: the comp is better → −$9,000. Garage: the comp is inferior → +$7,000. $356,000 − $9,000 + $7,000 = <b>$354,000</b>.

</details>

**G2.** The subject has 2,000 sq ft. A comp with 2,150 sq ft sold for $465,000. The market value of size is $120/sq ft. What is the adjusted price? *Hint: which one is bigger?*

<details><summary>Check your answer</summary>

The comp is 150 sq ft larger, so it is better. 150 × $120 = $18,000, subtracted. $465,000 − $18,000 = <b>$447,000</b>.

</details>

**G3.** Three adjusted comps for a Palm Bay home. Comp 1: adjusted $338,000 after one adjustment (−$4,000). Comp 2: adjusted $345,000 after three adjustments (+$22,000, −$18,000, +$9,000). Comp 3: adjusted $332,000 after two adjustments (−$15,000, +$6,000). Which deserves the most weight? *Hint: count, then add the sizes.*

<details><summary>Check your answer</summary>

<b>Comp 1</b>: the fewest adjustments (one) and the smallest ($4,000). Comp 2 has a net adjustment of only +$13,000, but its adjustments total $49,000. Comp 3's total $21,000.

</details>

### Step 9: Independent practice

**I1.** In Fort Myers a comp sold for $528,000. It has a pool worth $32,000 that the subject lacks. The subject has a fourth bedroom worth $18,000 that the comp lacks. What is the adjusted price?

<details><summary>Check your answer</summary>

$528,000 − $32,000 + $18,000 = <b>$514,000</b>.

</details>

**I2.** The subject sits on a Gulf-access canal lot (a canal with a boat route to the Gulf of Mexico). A comp on an inland lot sold for $612,000; the canal location is worth $45,000. The comp has a new roof worth $10,000 that the subject lacks. What is the adjusted price?

<details><summary>Check your answer</summary>

Location: the comp is inferior → +$45,000. Roof: the comp is better → −$10,000. $612,000 + $45,000 − $10,000 = <b>$647,000</b>.

</details>

**I3.** After a −$20,000 adjustment for a pool and a +$6,000 adjustment for a missing garage stall, a comp's adjusted price is $401,000. What did the comp sell for?

<details><summary>Check your answer</summary>

Undo each adjustment: $401,000 + $20,000 − $6,000 = <b>$415,000</b>. Check: $415,000 − $20,000 + $6,000 = $401,000.

</details>

**I4.** Jacksonville subject: 1,600 sq ft, one-car garage, 2 baths, no pool. Values: pool $22,000; second garage stall $6,000; bathroom $10,000; size $95/sq ft. Comp X sold for $318,000 and is identical except that it has a pool. Comp Y sold for $297,000 and is identical except for a two-car garage. Comp Z sold for $279,000; it has 1,450 sq ft and one bath, otherwise identical. Find each adjusted price, the bracket, and the comp that deserves the most weight.

<details><summary>Check your answer</summary>

X: $318,000 − $22,000 = $296,000 (one adjustment, $22,000). Y: $297,000 − $6,000 = $291,000 (one adjustment, $6,000). Z: 150 × $95 = $14,250; $279,000 + $14,250 + $10,000 = $303,250 (two adjustments, $24,250). Bracket: $291,000 to $303,250. Most weight: <b>Comp Y</b>, as few adjustments as X and the smallest.

</details>

### Step 10: Exam application

1. Identify the subject and the comp. Circle the comp's sale price: it is your starting number.
2. For each difference ask: "Is the **comp** better or worse here?"
3. Better → subtract (CBS). Inferior → add (CIA).
4. Add up the adjustments.
5. If asked which comp counts most: the fewest and smallest adjustments. Never "the highest price", "the oldest sale" or "the farthest away".
6. Eliminate any answer choice that changes the subject.

## 5. Practical example

**A CMA in Cape Coral.** Rita, a sales associate, is preparing a CMA for Mr. and Mrs. Duarte. A CMA uses the same comparison logic, but it is a licensee's pricing tool and is never called an appraisal (manual; not yet verified; see L15-1). The subject: 3 bedrooms, 2 baths, 1,800 sq ft, pool, on a Gulf-access canal. Values Rita uses from local sales: pool $30,000; Gulf access compared with a freshwater canal $40,000; new roof $15,000; boat lift $12,000; size $100/sq ft.

| | Comp 1 | Comp 2 | Comp 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sale price | $545,000 | $468,000 | $585,000 |
| Differences from the subject | New roof: −$15,000 | No pool: +$30,000 · freshwater canal: +$40,000 | 300 sq ft larger: −$30,000 · boat lift: −$12,000 |
| **Adjusted price** | **$530,000** | **$538,000** | **$543,000** |
| Adjustments | 1, total $15,000 | 2, total $70,000 | 2, total $42,000 |

Mrs. Duarte asks: *"Our house has a pool. Shouldn't you add $30,000 to our price?"* Rita explains that the subject is never adjusted. Comps 1 and 3 also have pools, so their prices already include one. Comp 2 has no pool, and that is why $30,000 was **added to Comp 2**.

The adjusted prices bracket the likely value: $530,000 to $543,000. Comp 1 is the closest twin (one $15,000 adjustment), so Rita leans on it and suggests a range starting around $530,000, not the simple average of $537,000.

## 6. Portugal comparison

**The familiar principle.** You have almost certainly priced homes by looking at similar nearby sales and then allowing for the differences. That is the logic of this approach. It is market practice, not national law.

**The Florida rule.** The Florida exam tests a fixed method and vocabulary: adjust the comparables, never the subject; comparable better → subtract, inferior → add; the comp with the fewest and smallest adjustments carries the most weight (manual; not yet verified).

**The precise difference.** This course has no verified Portugal–Florida comparison entry on valuation methods. Any statement about how Portuguese valuers, banks or tax authorities adjust comparables **requires verification**. Expect a different unit: Florida sizes and prices per unit of area are in square feet.

**Exam implication.** Use only the method above and the figures in the question. A habit from elsewhere, such as adjusting the property you are selling instead of the comp, produces the exam's favourite wrong answer.

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"If the subject lacks a feature, subtract it from the subject."** No. The subject is never adjusted. Subtract the feature from the **comp** that has it.
- **"The comp has a pool, so add the pool."** No. Comp better → **subtract**.
- **"The comp with the highest sale price counts most."** No. The comp with the fewest and smallest adjustments counts most.
- **"The final value is the average of the adjusted comps."** No. The appraiser weighs the comps by reliability.
