## 2. Why it matters

**In practice.** Every time you price a Florida home, you do a rough sales comparison. You look at what similar homes sold for and ask how this one is different. An appraiser does the same work formally, on an **adjustment grid**: a table that lists each comparable sale and the dollar corrections made to it. When a buyer's lender orders an appraisal, or a seller disputes your CMA (comparative market analysis: a licensee's pricing tool, never called an appraisal), you must be able to read that grid and explain every plus and minus.

**On the exam.** Appraisal is **8%** of the exam (V-02, confirmed), so about 8 of the 100 questions (V-01). Sales comparison questions are short and predictable. They ask which way to adjust, what one comparable's adjusted price is, and which comparable deserves the most weight. The wrong answers are built on two mistakes: adjusting the subject, and adjusting in the wrong direction.

## 3. Simple explanation

> **Source status.** The appraisal method in this lesson comes from the study manual. No official-source verification entry covers appraisal methods, so the rules are taught as **(manual; not yet verified)**. The arithmetic itself needs no verification.

### Step 1: What it represents

The **sales comparison approach** estimates a property's value from the prices of **recent sales of similar properties**. It rests on **substitution** (L16-1): an informed buyer will not pay more than the cost of an equally desirable substitute. So what buyers just paid for similar homes shows what this home is worth.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Subject (property)** | The property being valued |
| **Comparable ("comp")** | A recent sale of a similar property, ideally nearby and an arm's-length sale (independent parties, L16-1) |
| **Adjustment** | A dollar amount added to or subtracted from a comp's sale price for one difference from the subject |
| **Adjusted sale price** | The comp's price after all adjustments: what the comp would have sold for if it were just like the subject |
| **Adjustment grid** | The table showing the comps, their differences and the adjustments |

The approach is **best for homes and vacant land** (manual), because these sell often and buyers really do shop for them by comparison.

### Step 2: Units

- Sale prices and adjustments are in **US dollars**.
- A feature adjustment is a **lump sum** (for example, a pool: $25,000).
- A size adjustment is **dollars per square foot × the difference in square feet**. US living area is measured in square feet. *Metric aside only: 1 m² ≈ 10.76 sq ft.*
- The amount of each adjustment is the feature's **contribution** (L16-1): what it adds to market value, **not what it cost** to build.

### Step 3: Formula

**Adjusted sale price = comp's sale price − value of what the comp has that the subject lacks + value of what the subject has that the comp lacks**

You adjust the **comparable, never the subject**. The question to ask is: "If this comp were exactly like the subject, what would it have sold for?"

| Situation | Adjustment to the comp's price | Mnemonic |
|---|---|---|
| **C**omp **B**etter (it has a feature the subject lacks, or more of it) | **S**ubtract | **CBS** |
| **C**omp **I**nferior (it lacks a feature the subject has, or has less) | **A**dd | **CIA** |
| Both have the same feature | No adjustment | — |

**Why subtract when the comp is better?** The comp's price already includes the value of its extra feature. The subject does not have that feature, so it would not earn that money. You take it out.

### Step 4: When it applies

- **Property types:** best for homes and vacant land (manual). Brand-new or special-purpose buildings with few sales point to the cost approach (L16-3); income property points to the income approach (L16-4).
- **Choosing comps:** use **recent**, similar, nearby, arm's-length **sales**. A licensee's CMA also shows currently listed and expired listings to shape a price range (manual), but only sold properties show what buyers actually paid.
- **Bracketing:** the adjusted prices of good comps **bracket** the subject's value, meaning some fall above it and some below (manual).
- **Weighting:** the comp with the **fewest and smallest adjustments** is the most similar to the subject. It is the most reliable, so it carries **the most weight** (manual). Judge "smallest" by the size of each adjustment, ignoring the plus or minus sign: +$15,000 and −$15,000 net to zero, but they are still two large adjustments.
- **Final figure:** a weighted judgement, never a simple average (reconciliation, L16-4).

### Step 5: Worked example

**A. The manual's grid example.** A comp sold for **$410,000**. It has a pool worth $25,000 that the subject lacks. The subject has a renovated kitchen worth $15,000 that the comp lacks.

| Item | Who is better? | Amount |
|---|---|---|
| Comp's sale price | | $410,000 |
| Pool (only the comp has it) | Comp better → subtract | −$25,000 |
| Renovated kitchen (only the subject has it) | Comp inferior → add | +$15,000 |
| **Indicated value from this comp** | | **$400,000** |

**B. A three-comp grid in Lakeland.** Subject: 1,800 sq ft, 3 bedrooms, 2 baths, 2-car garage, no pool. The appraiser's market data show these contributions: pool $25,000; living area $100 per sq ft; 2-car garage instead of 1-car $8,000.

| | Comp A | Comp B | Comp C |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sale price | $392,000 | $441,000 | $388,000 |
| Living area | 1,800 (same): 0 | 1,900, better: −$10,000 | 1,650, inferior: +$15,000 |
| Garage | 1-car, inferior: +$8,000 | 2-car (same): 0 | 1-car, inferior: +$8,000 |
| Pool | None (same): 0 | Pool, better: −$25,000 | Pool, better: −$25,000 |
| **Adjusted price** | **$400,000** | **$406,000** | **$386,000** |
| Number of adjustments | 1 | 2 | 3 |
| Total size of adjustments | $8,000 | $35,000 | $48,000 |

Comp A needs the fewest and smallest adjustments, so it carries the most weight, and the indicated value is close to **$400,000**. The adjusted prices ($386,000 to $406,000) bracket that figure. The simple average of the three ($397,333) is **not** the method.

### Step 6: Reasonableness check

- **Direction test.** Comp better → its adjusted price goes **down**. Comp inferior → it goes **up**. Ask: "Would the subject sell for more or less than this comp?"
- **Clustering.** Adjusted prices should sit closer together than raw prices. In example B the raw prices span $53,000 ($388,000 to $441,000); the adjusted prices span only $20,000.
- **Size.** Each adjustment is a market contribution, often much less than what the feature cost.
- **Work backwards.** $400,000 − $15,000 + $25,000 = $410,000 ✓.

### Step 7: Common errors

| Error | Correction |
|---|---|
| Adjusting the subject's value | Adjust only the comparables |
| Adding the value of a feature only the comp has | Comp better → subtract (CBS) |
| Using what the feature cost | Use what it adds to market value (contribution) |
| Adjusting for a feature both properties share | No difference, no adjustment |
| Giving most weight to the highest price or the most recent sale | Most weight = fewest and smallest adjustments |
| Treating a small net adjustment as "few adjustments" | Count each adjustment and its size, ignoring signs |
| Averaging the adjusted prices | Weigh them; never a simple average |
| Using a sale between relatives as a comp | Comps should be arm's-length sales (L16-1) |

### Step 8: Guided practice

**G1.** A comp sold for $289,000. It has a 2-car garage; the subject has a 1-car garage. The second garage space adds $9,000 of value. What is the comp's adjusted price? *Hint: which property is better?*

<details><summary>Check your answer</summary>

The comp is better → subtract. $289,000 − $9,000 = **$281,000**.

</details>

**G2.** A Sarasota comp sold for $352,000. The subject has a screened lanai (a covered, screened patio, very common in Florida) worth $7,500 that the comp lacks. The comp has a new roof worth $11,000 that the subject lacks. What is the comp's adjusted price? *Hint: one plus, one minus.*

<details><summary>Check your answer</summary>

Lanai: comp inferior → +$7,500. Roof: comp better → −$11,000. $352,000 + $7,500 − $11,000 = **$348,500**.

</details>

**G3.** The subject has a pool that adds $28,000; the comp has none. Does the comp's price go up or down, and by how much? *Hint: which property is inferior?*

<details><summary>Check your answer</summary>

The comp is inferior → add. Its price goes **up by $28,000**. The subject is never adjusted.

</details>

### Step 9: Independent practice

**I1.** The subject has 2,100 sq ft of living area. A comp with 1,950 sq ft sold for $415,000. The market pays $95 per sq ft of living area. Find the comp's adjusted price.

<details><summary>Check your answer</summary>

Difference: 2,100 − 1,950 = 150 sq ft; the comp is smaller (inferior) → add. 150 × $95 = $14,250. $415,000 + $14,250 = **$429,250**.

</details>

**I2.** Vacant land in Cape Coral. A comp lot sold for $185,000. It has canal frontage worth $40,000 that the subject lot lacks. The subject lot is 2,000 sq ft larger, and land sells at $6 per sq ft. Find the comp's adjusted price.

<details><summary>Check your answer</summary>

Canal: comp better → −$40,000. Size: comp inferior → +(2,000 × $6) = +$12,000. $185,000 − $40,000 + $12,000 = **$157,000**.

</details>

**I3.** After adjusting: Comp X = $512,000 (4 adjustments, $61,000 in total size); Comp Y = $498,000 (1 adjustment of $4,000); Comp Z = $530,000 (2 adjustments, $27,000 in total size). Which comp carries the most weight? Is the value $513,333?

<details><summary>Check your answer</summary>

**Comp Y**: the fewest and smallest adjustments. $513,333 is the simple average ($1,540,000 ÷ 3); the appraiser weighs the comps instead of averaging, so the value will be closest to Comp Y's $498,000.

</details>

**I4.** A comp sold for $468,000. It has a pool worth $30,000 that the subject lacks. The subject has a 3-car garage and the comp a 2-car garage (difference: $7,000). The comp has 200 sq ft more living area, worth $110 per sq ft. The subject has a lake view worth $20,000 that the comp lacks. Find the comp's adjusted price.

<details><summary>Check your answer</summary>

$468,000 − $30,000 (pool) + $7,000 (garage) − $22,000 (200 × $110) + $20,000 (view) = **$443,000**.

</details>

### Step 10: Exam application

| If the question says… | Answer pattern |
|---|---|
| "The comparable has a feature the subject lacks" | Comp better → subtract from the comparable's price |
| "The subject has a feature the comparable lacks" | Comp inferior → add to the comparable's price |
| An option that adjusts the subject | Trap: never adjust the subject |
| "Which comparable deserves the most weight?" | Fewest and smallest adjustments |
| "Best approach for a single-family home or a vacant lot?" | Sales comparison |
| A dollar answer | Write + or − next to each feature, then total |

## 5. Practical example

**Setting.** You are preparing a CMA for Mr. and Mrs. Alvarez, who own a 3-bedroom, 2-bath home without a pool in Port St. Lucie. Last year they renovated the kitchen for $55,000. You review possible comps:

| Candidate | Your decision | Rule applied |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Same subdivision, sold 2 months ago for $398,000, same size, has a pool, older kitchen | **Use** | Recent, similar, nearby sale |
| 2. Same model, sold to the seller's daughter at a discount | **Reject** | Not an arm's-length sale (L16-1) |
| 3. Same model, but in a different market area across the county | **Weak**; prefer nearby sales | Comps should be similar and nearby |
| 4. Active listing at $455,000 | **Show as competition**, not as proof of value | A listing is not a sale |
| 5. Expired listing at $470,000 | **Show**: buyers did not pay that price | CMAs include expired listings (manual) |

Market data show a pool adds about $24,000 and a renovated kitchen about $15,000. For comp 1: comp better (pool) → −$24,000; comp inferior (older kitchen) → +$15,000. $398,000 − $24,000 + $15,000 = **$389,000**.

Mrs. Alvarez objects: "The kitchen cost us $55,000, so add $55,000." You explain contribution: buyers pay about $15,000 more for a renovated kitchen, so that is the adjustment. Mr. Alvarez asks you to "raise our value because comp 1 has a pool". You explain that the comp is adjusted, never the subject: the pool comes **off** the comp's price. Finally, you label your work as a CMA. It is not an appraisal, and you may never call it one (manual; not yet verified; see L16-4).

## 6. Portugal comparison

**The familiar principle.** You have probably priced homes in Portugal by comparing them with similar nearby sales and adjusting mentally for a terrace, a garage or a renovation. That is the same market logic: substitution and contribution.

**The Florida rule.** The Florida exam turns that logic into a fixed drill. Adjust the comparables, never the subject; CBS / CIA; the comp with the fewest and smallest adjustments carries the most weight.

**The precise difference.** This course has no verified Portugal–Florida comparison entry on valuation. Any statement about how Portuguese valuers, banks or tax authorities compare sales, or how sale-price data is obtained in Portugal, **requires verification**. Sizes also differ: Florida works in square feet, not square metres.

**Exam implication.** Your experience helps you sense the right *size* of an adjustment. The exam, however, tests the *direction* and the vocabulary. Never let a Portuguese practice answer a Florida question.

- Why the most similar comp is the most reliable: fewer estimates, less room for error.

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"The comp has a pool, so add the pool to the subject."** No. The subject is never adjusted. Subtract the pool's value from the comp.
- **"A better comp means a higher value for the subject."** No. A better comp is adjusted **down** to look like the subject.
- **"The adjustment equals what the feature cost."** No. It equals what the feature adds to market value.
- **"The highest adjusted price gets the most weight."** No. The comp with the fewest and smallest adjustments does.
- **"Average the adjusted prices to get the value."** No. Reconciliation weighs; it never simply averages.
- **"Active listings prove value."** No. They show competition. Sales show what buyers actually paid.
