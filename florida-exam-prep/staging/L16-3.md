## 2. Why it matters

**In practice.** Some Florida properties almost never sell: a church, a school, a fire station. Others are so new that the clearest way to value them is to ask what they would cost to build. The cost approach also gives you the words to explain why a 1970s house in Ocala is worth less than a new house of the same size next door: the old house has suffered **depreciation**. In appraisal, depreciation means any loss in value, from any cause.

**On the exam.** Appraisal is **8%** of the exam (V-02, confirmed), so about 8 of the 100 questions (V-01). Typical questions are a calculation (land + cost new − depreciation), a classification of depreciation ("an airport is built nearby…") and a definition (reproduction or replacement cost). The traps: depreciating the land, calling external obsolescence curable, and borrowing the tax depreciation periods (V-77) for an appraisal question.

## 3. Simple explanation

> **Source status.** The cost approach and the three types of depreciation come from the study manual. No verification entry covers appraisal methods, so they are taught as **(manual; not yet verified)**. The tax depreciation facts used for contrast are verified (V-77). The arithmetic itself needs no verification.

### Step 1: What it represents

The cost approach asks: **what would it cost to create a substitute today?** A buyer will not pay more than the cost of buying a similar lot and building an equally useful building (substitution, L16-1). So the value is the land, plus the building as if new, minus the value the existing building has already lost.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Improvements** | The buildings and other structures on the land |
| **Land value** | Estimated separately, by sales comparison of similar lots (L16-2) |
| **Cost new** | What it would cost **today** to build the improvements new |
| **Reproduction cost** | Cost of an **exact replica**: same design, same materials |
| **Replacement cost** | Cost of a building with **equal utility** (usefulness), built with **current materials and standards** |
| **Accrued depreciation** | All the value the existing improvements have lost compared with new ones, from any cause |
| **Curable** | Worth fixing: the cure costs no more than the value it adds |
| **Incurable** | Not worth fixing (the cure costs more than it adds), or impossible for the owner to fix |

**The three types of depreciation:**

| Type | Cause | Where it comes from | Examples | Curable? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Physical deterioration** | Wear and tear | The property itself | Worn roof, peeling paint, cracked foundation | Curable **or** incurable |
| **Functional obsolescence** | Outdated design or features | The property itself | Four bedrooms but **one bathroom**; **no closets** | Curable **or** incurable |
| **External (economic) obsolescence** | Forces **outside the property line** | The neighbourhood or the market | A new **airport** nearby; a weak **economy** | **Always incurable** |

**Why is external obsolescence always incurable?** Its cause is outside the property. No amount of money spent on the house moves the airport or fixes the local economy.

**Land never depreciates** (manual). Land does not wear out and cannot go out of style; any change in its value shows up in the separate land estimate. Tax law also never depreciates land (V-77, verified).

### Step 2: Units

- **Building size:** square feet. Cost new is usually given **per square foot**: cost new = sq ft × cost per sq ft. *Metric aside only: a cost per m² ÷ 10.76 ≈ cost per sq ft.*
- **Depreciation:** given in dollars, as a **percentage of cost new**, or through years of **economic life** (the total number of years the building is expected to add value).
- **Land:** dollars, taken from sales of comparable lots.

### Step 3: Formula

**Value = land value + cost new of the improvements − accrued depreciation**

| The question gives… | Accrued depreciation = |
|---|---|
| A percentage | cost new × the percentage |
| Separate amounts | physical + functional + external |
| Age and total economic life | (cost new ÷ economic life) × age, the same amount each year (straight-line) |

Work in this order: cost new → minus depreciation → **then** add land. The depreciation applies to the **improvements only**.

### Step 4: When it applies

- **Best for:** **new construction** and **special-purpose properties** such as **schools, churches and fire stations** (manual). A new building has little depreciation to estimate. A special-purpose building has few comparable sales (so sales comparison is weak) and no rent (so the income approach does not work).
- **Weaker for old buildings:** the older the building, the more depreciation must be estimated, and the less reliable the result.
- **Reproduction or replacement?** The question says which cost it gives; you must recognise the definition. An exact copy of a historic building's original details is reproduction. A modern building that does the same job is replacement.
- **Not tax depreciation.** Appraisal depreciation is a **loss of market value**. Tax depreciation is an income-tax deduction: residential rental property over **27.5 years**, nonresidential over **39 years**, straight-line, land never (V-77, confirmed). Do not use these periods in a cost approach question unless the question itself gives them.

### Step 5: Worked example

**A. Percentage method: a church in Tallahassee.** Land: $240,000 (from sales of similar sites). Building: 12,000 sq ft; replacement cost $185 per sq ft. Accrued depreciation: 20%.

1. Cost new: 12,000 × $185 = **$2,220,000**.
2. Depreciation: $2,220,000 × 0.20 = **$444,000**.
3. Depreciated building: $2,220,000 − $444,000 = **$1,776,000**.
4. Add land: $1,776,000 + $240,000 = **$2,016,000**.

If you wrongly took 20% off the land as well, you would get $1,968,000: a wrong answer the exam may offer.

**B. Straight-line method: a fire station in Polk County.** Land: $180,000. Replacement cost new: $1,350,000. The building is 9 years old; its total economic life is 45 years.

1. Depreciation per year: $1,350,000 ÷ 45 = **$30,000**.
2. Accrued depreciation: $30,000 × 9 = **$270,000** (9 ÷ 45 = 20%).
3. Depreciated building: $1,350,000 − $270,000 = **$1,080,000**.
4. Add land: $1,080,000 + $180,000 = **$1,260,000**.

### Step 6: Reasonableness check

- The value is **at most** land + cost new. It equals that sum only for a building with no depreciation.
- The **land figure passes through unchanged**. If your answer changed it, you depreciated land.
- The depreciation percentage lies between 0 and 100%, and a new building should show little.
- Shortcut check for A: building value = 80% of cost new: 0.80 × $2,220,000 = $1,776,000 ✓.

### Step 7: Common errors

| Error | Correction |
|---|---|
| Depreciating the land | Depreciate the improvements only; land never depreciates |
| Forgetting to add the land | Value = land **+** depreciated improvements |
| Using the original (historical) construction cost | Cost new = today's cost |
| Calling external obsolescence curable | It is **always incurable** |
| Calling a poor floor plan "external" | Inside the property, outdated design → functional |
| Calling highway noise "functional" | Outside the property line → external |
| Mixing up the two costs | Reproduction = replica; replacement = equal utility, current materials |
| Using 27.5 or 39 years in an appraisal question | Those are tax periods (V-77); use the life the question gives |

### Step 8: Guided practice

**G1.** Land $75,000. A house of 1,600 sq ft would cost $150 per sq ft to build new. Accrued depreciation: 10%. What is the value by the cost approach? *Hint: depreciate the house, not the land.*

<details><summary>Check your answer</summary>

Cost new: 1,600 × $150 = $240,000. Depreciation: 10% = $24,000. House: $216,000. Plus land: $216,000 + $75,000 = **$291,000**.

</details>

**G2.** Classify each item: (a) worn carpet and peeling paint; (b) four bedrooms but one bathroom; (c) a landfill opens half a mile away; (d) a cracked foundation that would cost more to repair than the repair would add in value. *Hint: inside or outside the property line?*

<details><summary>Check your answer</summary>

(a) **Physical deterioration**, curable. (b) **Functional obsolescence**: curable if adding a bathroom adds at least what it costs, otherwise incurable. (c) **External obsolescence**, always incurable. (d) **Physical deterioration**, incurable.

</details>

**G3.** A historic coquina-stone building in St. Augustine would cost $3.2 million to rebuild exactly, with the same stone and hand-carved woodwork. A modern building with the same usefulness would cost $2.1 million. Name each cost. *Hint: replica or same utility?*

<details><summary>Check your answer</summary>

$3.2 million = **reproduction cost** (exact replica). $2.1 million = **replacement cost** (equal utility, current materials).

</details>

### Step 9: Independent practice

**I1.** Land $110,000. Replacement cost new $420,000. The building is 12 years old, with a total economic life of 60 years (straight-line). Find the value.

<details><summary>Check your answer</summary>

$420,000 ÷ 60 = $7,000 per year. × 12 = $84,000 depreciation. Building: $420,000 − $84,000 = $336,000. Value: $336,000 + $110,000 = **$446,000**.

</details>

**I2.** A house would cost $510,000 new. Accrued depreciation: physical $18,000, functional $22,000, external $35,000. Land: $140,000. Find the value. Which item can the owner never cure?

<details><summary>Check your answer</summary>

Depreciation: $18,000 + $22,000 + $35,000 = $75,000. Building: $510,000 − $75,000 = $435,000. Value: $435,000 + $140,000 = **$575,000**. The **external** $35,000 is always incurable.

</details>

**I3.** A property's value by the cost approach is $612,000. Land is $150,000 and cost new is $540,000. How much accrued depreciation was taken, and what percentage of cost new is that?

<details><summary>Check your answer</summary>

Depreciated building: $612,000 − $150,000 = $462,000. Depreciation: $540,000 − $462,000 = **$78,000**. $78,000 ÷ $540,000 = 0.1444, about **14.4%**.

</details>

### Step 10: Exam application

| If the question mentions… | Think | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| A new building, church, school or fire station: "best approach?" | Little depreciation; few sales; no rent | Cost approach |
| An airport, highway, landfill or the economy | Outside the property line | External obsolescence, always incurable |
| One bathroom, no closets, outdated layout | Design | Functional obsolescence |
| Worn roof, peeling paint | Wear | Physical deterioration |
| "Exact replica" | | Reproduction cost |
| "Same utility, modern materials" | | Replacement cost |
| A dollar answer | land + (cost new − depreciation) | Never depreciate the land |

## 5. Practical example

**Setting.** Daniel, a buyer you represent in Ocala, compares two homes on the same street:

- **The 1970s home:** 1,900 sq ft on a lot worth about $70,000.
- **The new builder home next door:** 2,000 sq ft on a lot worth about $72,000.

He asks why the older home is so much cheaper when the sizes are similar. You sketch the cost approach, using a local replacement cost of $165 per sq ft.

| | 1970s home | New home |
|---|---|---|
| Cost new | 1,900 × $165 = $313,500 | 2,000 × $165 = $330,000 |
| Physical: worn roof (curable: a new roof adds at least what it costs) | −$16,000 | — |
| Physical: aged structure (incurable) | −$22,000 | — |
| Functional: four bedrooms, one bathroom | −$18,000 | — |
| External: four-lane highway built behind the backyard (incurable) | −$20,000 | — |
| Depreciated improvements | $237,500 | $330,000 |
| Land (not depreciated) | +$70,000 | +$72,000 |
| **Cost approach indication** | **$307,500** | **$402,000** |

Your explanation to Daniel: the roof is curable, so it can be priced as a repair. A second bathroom might be curable if it adds at least what it costs. The highway is external obsolescence: no owner can cure it, so it will stay in the price when Daniel sells. You add that the cost approach is reliable for the new home, which has almost no depreciation, but weak for a house about 50 years old, so recent comparable sales (L16-2) matter more there. If Daniel's loan is a federally related transaction, the lender's appraisal must come from a state-licensed or certified appraiser, not from you (manual; not yet verified; L16-4).

## 6. Portugal comparison

**The familiar principle.** In your own brokerage work you have probably seen buyers discount an old building that needs work, and heard the question "what would it cost to build this today?". That is the logic of cost new minus depreciation: market common sense, not a rule of Portuguese or Florida law.

**The Florida rule.** The exam uses a fixed formula (land + cost new − accrued depreciation), the reproduction/replacement distinction and three named types of depreciation, with external obsolescence always incurable.

**The precise difference.** This course has no verified Portugal–Florida comparison entry on the cost approach. Any Portuguese construction-cost reference figure, or Portuguese rule for insurance or tax valuation, **requires verification** and plays no part on this exam. Units differ as well: Florida costs are quoted per square foot (a cost per m² ÷ 10.76 ≈ cost per sq ft).

**Exam implication.** Use only the US categories and the formula. Never let a Portuguese rule answer a Florida question.

## 7. Legal and numerical requirements

**Memorize exactly**

*Appraisal method: manual; not yet verified (no verification entry covers it).*

- **Value = land (by sales comparison) + cost new − accrued depreciation.**
- Best for **new construction** and **special-purpose** properties (**schools, churches, fire stations**).
- **Reproduction** = exact replica. **Replacement** = equal utility with current materials and standards.
- **Physical deterioration** (wear): curable or incurable.
- **Functional obsolescence** (outdated design: one bathroom, no closets): curable or incurable.
- **External (economic) obsolescence** (outside the property line: an airport, the economy): **always incurable**.
- **Land never depreciates.**

*Verified:*

- Appraisal = **8%** of the exam (V-02).
- Tax depreciation, a different concept: residential rental **27.5 years**, nonresidential **39 years**, straight-line; land is never depreciated (V-77).

**Understand**

- Why external obsolescence cannot be cured by the owner.
- Why special-purpose buildings point to the cost approach.
- Curable or incurable is a question of economics: does the cure add at least what it costs?
- Appraisal depreciation (loss of market value) is not tax depreciation (a deduction).

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"Apply the depreciation percentage to the whole property."** No. Only the improvements depreciate; land never does.
- **"External obsolescence can be cured if the owner spends enough."** No. Its cause is outside the property line, so it is always incurable.
- **"Functional obsolescence is always incurable."** No. It can be curable or incurable.
- **"Reproduction cost means a modern equivalent."** No. That is replacement cost. Reproduction is an exact replica.
- **"Appraisal depreciation uses 27.5 years."** No. That is a tax rule (V-77). Use the figures the question gives.
- **"The cost approach is best for an older home."** No. It is best for new construction and special-purpose properties.
