## 2. Why it matters

**In practice.** Florida builds a lot of new housing. Buyers of new homes often ask why an appraisal differs from the builder's price. Owners of special-purpose buildings, such as churches, private schools or a former fire station, discover that there are almost no comparable sales. The cost approach is the tool for both situations. Its vocabulary also explains everyday pricing problems: why a 1970s four-bedroom house with one bathroom sells at a discount, and why homes beside a new airport runway lose value that no renovation can recover.

**On the exam.** Appraisal is **8%** of the exam (V-02, confirmed), about 8 of the 100 questions (V-01). Expect three kinds of cost approach question: a calculation (land + cost new − depreciation), naming the type of depreciation from a short scenario, and reproduction versus replacement cost. Two phrases decide many answers: **external obsolescence is always incurable**, and **land never depreciates**.

## 3. Simple explanation

*Source status: the method and definitions in this lesson come from the study manual. No official-source verification entry covers appraisal methods, so they are taught as (manual; not yet verified). Where the manual uses a term without defining it (curable, incurable), the lesson gives its plain standard meaning. The tax depreciation figures marked V-77 are verified.*

### Step 1: What it represents

The **cost approach** asks: *what would it cost to create this property today?* You would buy a similar lot and build a similar building. An existing building is not new, though, so you subtract the value it has lost. The logic is **substitution** (L16-1): an informed buyer will not pay more for an existing property than the cost of buying land and building an equally useful substitute.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Land value** | The site's value as if vacant, found by **sales comparison** of land sales (L16-2) |
| **Cost new** | What it would cost today to build the improvements (the buildings) |
| **Reproduction cost** | Cost of an **exact replica**: same design, same materials, same features |
| **Replacement cost** | Cost of a building with **equal utility** using **current materials and standards** |
| **Accrued depreciation** | The total loss in value of the improvements from all causes, measured from cost new |
| **Curable** | Fixing it makes economic sense: the cure adds at least as much value as it costs |
| **Incurable** | It cannot be fixed, or fixing it would cost more than the value it adds |

**The three types of depreciation**

| Type | Cause | Examples | Curable? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Physical deterioration** | Wear and tear, damage, age | Worn roof, peeling paint, an old air-conditioning unit | Curable **or** incurable |
| **Functional obsolescence** | Outdated design or features **inside** the property | One bathroom in a large house, bedrooms with no closets | Curable **or** incurable |
| **External (economic) obsolescence** | Forces **outside the property line** | A new airport, the general economy, a nearby landfill | **Always incurable** |

**Why external obsolescence is always incurable.** The cause is outside the owner's land. No amount of money spent on the property can move the airport or fix the economy.

**Why land never depreciates.** Land does not wear out and is not built, so it cannot become obsolete in design. It is valued separately at today's market value, and depreciation is charged only against the improvements.

**Best for:** **new construction** and **special-purpose properties** (schools, churches, fire stations). A new building has little or no depreciation, so its cost new is a reliable guide to value. Special-purpose buildings rarely sell and rarely rent, so the other two approaches have little evidence to work with.

### Step 2: Units

- **Cost new** is usually given as area × cost per square foot: 2,000 sq ft × $175/sq ft = $350,000. *(Metric aside: 1 m² ≈ 10.76 sq ft, so $175 per sq ft ≈ $1,884 per m².)*
- **Depreciation** comes either in **dollars** or as a **percentage of the building's cost new**. A percentage is never applied to the land.
- Land value, cost new and depreciation are all in dollars, so the final answer is in dollars.

### Step 3: Formula

**Value = Land value + Cost new of the improvements − Accrued depreciation**

When depreciation is a percentage:

**Value = Land value + Cost new × (1 − depreciation %)**

Example: 20% depreciated means the building keeps 80% of its cost new (1 − 0.20 = 0.80).

### Step 4: When it applies

- **Reproduction or replacement?** The question says which one it uses. The formula is the same; only the cost figure changes. An exact replica copies outdated features too. Replacement builds the same usefulness with today's materials.
- **Depreciation given by type:** add the three types together, then subtract the total once.
- **Depreciation per year:** the manual gives no depreciation method, so treat this as plain arithmetic on the question's numbers. 2% a year for 8 years = 16% in total. If a question gives an age and a total useful life, the percentage is age ÷ life (10 years ÷ 50 years = 20%).
- **Curable or incurable** changes the label, not the arithmetic: all accrued depreciation is subtracted.
- **Land:** take it exactly as given, never depreciated.

### Step 5: Worked example

**A. Depreciation by type (Gainesville).** A 12-year-old home sits on a lot worth $120,000 (from vacant-lot sales). The house has 2,000 sq ft and a replacement cost of $175/sq ft. Accrued depreciation: physical $30,000 (worn roof, old air-conditioning unit), functional $10,000 (two bedrooms with no closets), external $15,000 (noise from a new highway).

1. Cost new: 2,000 × $175 = $350,000.
2. Accrued depreciation: $30,000 + $10,000 + $15,000 = $55,000.
3. Depreciated building: $350,000 − $55,000 = $295,000.
4. Value: $120,000 + $295,000 = **$415,000**.

**B. Depreciation as a percentage.** Building cost new $480,000, accrued depreciation 20%, land $150,000.

1. Depreciation: $480,000 × 0.20 = $96,000.
2. Building: $480,000 − $96,000 = $384,000 (check: $480,000 × 0.80 = $384,000).
3. Value: $150,000 + $384,000 = **$534,000**.
4. **The trap:** 20% of land plus building ($630,000 × 0.20 = $126,000) gives $504,000. That is $30,000 too low, exactly 20% of the land, because the land was wrongly depreciated.

### Step 6: Reasonableness check

- The depreciated building is **less than or equal to** its cost new.
- The final value is **more than** the land value.
- The land figure in your final sum is the **same** as the land figure given.
- A brand-new building has little or no depreciation, so its value ≈ land + cost new.

### Step 7: Common errors

| Error | What it looks like | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Depreciating the land | 20% of land + building | Apply the percentage to the building's cost new only |
| Forgetting the land | Answer = cost new − depreciation | Add the land back |
| Adding depreciation | Land + cost new + depreciation | Depreciation is always **subtracted** |
| Calling external obsolescence curable | "Soundproof windows cure the airport" | External = outside the property = always incurable |
| Swapping the cost definitions | "Replacement = exact replica" | Reproduction = exact replica; replacement = equal utility, current materials |
| Mixing appraisal and tax depreciation | Using 27.5 years to measure a house's loss in value | Tax depreciation (V-77) is an income-tax deduction, a different concept |

### Step 9: Independent practice

**I1.** A new office building in Lakeland: 3,200 sq ft at $185/sq ft; accrued depreciation 10%; land $140,000. Value?

<details><summary>Check your answer</summary>

Cost new: 3,200 × $185 = $592,000. Depreciation: $59,200. Building: $532,800. Value: $140,000 + $532,800 = <b>$672,800</b>.

</details>

**I2.** Building cost new $310,000. Physical deterioration $22,000, functional obsolescence $8,500, external obsolescence $12,000. Land $95,000. Value?

<details><summary>Check your answer</summary>

Depreciation: $22,000 + $8,500 + $12,000 = $42,500. Building: $310,000 − $42,500 = $267,500. Value: $95,000 + $267,500 = <b>$362,500</b>.

</details>

**I3.** Building cost new $400,000; the question says it has lost 2% a year for 8 years. Land $100,000. Value?

<details><summary>Check your answer</summary>

2% × 8 = 16%. $400,000 × 0.16 = $64,000. Building: $336,000. Value: $100,000 + $336,000 = <b>$436,000</b>.

</details>

**I4.** The cost approach gives $500,000. Land is $125,000 and the building's cost new is $450,000. How much accrued depreciation was taken, in dollars and as a percentage?

<details><summary>Check your answer</summary>

Building after depreciation: $500,000 − $125,000 = $375,000. Depreciation: $450,000 − $375,000 = <b>$75,000</b>. Percentage: $75,000 ÷ $450,000 = <b>16⅔% (about 16.67%)</b>.

</details>

**I5.** (a) For a 1920s Coral Gables house, an appraiser prices an exact copy with the same hand-made tiles and plaster work. Which cost is that? (b) A county opens a landfill half a mile from a Polk County subdivision. Which type of depreciation is it, and can new landscaping cure it?

<details><summary>Check your answer</summary>

(a) <b>Reproduction cost</b> (exact replica). (b) <b>External obsolescence</b>. The cause is outside the property line, so it is <b>always incurable</b>; landscaping does not cure it.

</details>

### Step 10: Exam application

1. Find the three inputs: land, cost new, depreciation.
2. If cost new is per square foot, multiply by the area first.
3. If depreciation is a percentage, apply it to the **building only**.
4. Value = land + (cost new − depreciation).
5. Classification questions: outside the property line → external, always incurable. Wear → physical. Outdated design → functional.
6. "Exact replica" → reproduction. "Same utility, modern materials" → replacement.

## 5. Practical example

**A charter school in Ocala.** Your buyer client, a nonprofit, wants to buy a 6-year-old charter school building. The lender orders an appraisal from an appraiser (who may appraise is covered in L16-4). Schools rarely sell and are rarely rented, so the appraiser relies mainly on the cost approach. You help the client read the report:

| Item | Appraiser's figure | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Land | $310,000 | From sales of vacant commercial land nearby |
| Replacement cost new | 14,000 sq ft × $215 = $3,010,000 | Equal utility with current materials and building standards |
| Physical deterioration | −$160,000 | Worn roof and aging air-conditioning equipment |
| Functional obsolescence | −$90,000 | Outdated layout: too few restrooms for the classrooms |
| External obsolescence | −$120,000 | A new truck distribution center next door brings traffic and noise |
| **Indicated value** | **$2,950,000** | $310,000 + ($3,010,000 − $370,000) |

The client asks: "If we plant a tree screen, does the $120,000 come back?" You explain that external obsolescence comes from outside the property line and is **always incurable**. The roof, by contrast, can be replaced. The client also asks why the land line has no depreciation: land never depreciates.

## 6. Portugal comparison

**The familiar principle.** Estimating what it would cost to build a property again, less an allowance for its age and condition, is general building and valuation logic. You may have used it when pricing old buildings or rehabilitation projects.

**The Florida rule.** The exam uses a fixed formula and fixed labels: land + cost new − accrued depreciation; reproduction versus replacement cost; physical, functional and external depreciation, with external always incurable and land never depreciated (manual; not yet verified).

**The precise difference.** This course has no verified Portugal–Florida comparison entry on cost-based valuation. Any Portuguese construction cost figure, depreciation scale or official valuation formula **requires verification** and must never be used in a Florida answer. Florida costs are quoted per square foot, not per square metre.

**Exam implication.** Use the three exam labels exactly. Translating them loosely ("old age", "bad location") leads to the wrong answer choice.

## 7. Legal and numerical requirements

**Memorize exactly**

*Appraisal method: manual; not yet verified (no verification entry covers it).*

- **Value = land (by sales comparison) + cost new − accrued depreciation.**
- Best for **new construction** and **special-purpose properties** (schools, churches, fire stations).
- **Reproduction** = exact replica. **Replacement** = equal utility, current materials and standards.
- **Physical deterioration** (wear): curable or incurable.
- **Functional obsolescence** (outdated design, such as one bathroom or no closets): curable or incurable.
- **External (economic) obsolescence** (outside the property line, such as an airport or the economy): **always incurable**.
- **Land never depreciates.**

*Verified figures:*

- Appraisal = **8%** of the exam (V-02).
- Tax depreciation, a different concept (V-77): residential rental **27.5 years**, nonresidential **39 years**, straight-line; land is never depreciated.

**Understand**

- Why the cost approach suits new and special-purpose properties.
- Why external obsolescence can never be cured by the owner.
- Why a depreciation percentage applies to the building, not the land.
- Appraisal depreciation measures lost market value; tax depreciation is a deduction on a set schedule.

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"Land depreciates along with the house."** No. Land never depreciates; apply the percentage to the building only.
- **"Reproduction and replacement cost mean the same thing."** No. Reproduction = exact replica; replacement = equal utility with current materials and standards.
- **"Enough money can cure any depreciation."** No. External obsolescence is always incurable because its cause is outside the property.
- **"Functional obsolescence is always incurable."** No. Physical and functional can each be curable or incurable.
- **"Accrued depreciation is the tax deduction over 27.5 years."** No. That is tax depreciation (V-77). Accrued depreciation is the appraiser's estimate of lost value.
- **"The cost approach is best for an ordinary resale home."** No. It is best for new construction and special-purpose properties; homes are usually valued by sales comparison.
