## 2. Why it matters

In Florida practice these loan names come up in real conversations. A seller offers to "carry the paper", that is, to lend the buyer part of the price herself. A developer says each lot will be "released" at closing. A buyer wants one loan for a furnished beach condo and its furniture. A retired client asks about a reverse mortgage. If you can name the loan, you can explain what it does and spot a structure that will not work, such as a wraparound over a loan with a due-on-sale clause.

For the exam, Section 13 carries about 4% of the questions (V-02). Expect vocabulary matching: a described loan to name, or a named loan to explain. The ARM formula (**index + margin**) is a classic.

## 3. Simple explanation

> **Source status.** The loan definitions come from the study manual (manual; not yet verified). The reverse-mortgage age of **62** is listed under V-26 as not yet verified. The right of rescission on home equity loans is verified against federal rules (V-51).

### Two questions about every loan

Every loan can be described by answering two separate questions:

1. **How is it repaid?** This is the loan's *shape*: amortized, balloon, interest-only or adjustable.
2. **What is it for, and what does it cover?** This is the loan's *purpose*: package, blanket, wraparound, purchase-money, home equity, reverse or construction.

One loan can carry a label from each list. A seller-financed (purchase-money) loan, for example, can also be partially amortized with a balloon.

### Repayment shapes

The key fact behind all four shapes is that **interest is charged on the unpaid balance**. Whatever the payment does to the balance decides the shape.

**1. Fully amortized loan.** The payments are **level** (the same every month), and each payment covers that month's interest plus some principal. The last payment brings the balance to zero. Because the balance falls, **the interest part falls and the principal part rises**, even though the payment stays the same.

*Example:* a $200,000 loan at 6% for 30 years has a level principal-and-interest payment of $1,199.10.
- Month 1: interest = $200,000 × 6% ÷ 12 = **$1,000.00**; principal = $1,199.10 − $1,000.00 = **$199.10**; new balance $199,800.90.
- Month 2: interest = $199,800.90 × 6% ÷ 12 = **$999.00**; principal = **$200.10**.

**2. Partially amortized loan with a balloon.** The payments reduce the principal, but not to zero by the end of the term. The remaining balance is due as one large final payment, the **balloon payment**. This structure keeps the monthly payments lower, but the borrower must then pay off, refinance or sell. Balloon abuse is one of the predatory practices you met in L12-3.

**3. Interest-only (term) loan.** The payments cover **interest only**, so the principal never goes down. The **whole principal** is due at the end of the term.

**4. Adjustable-rate mortgage (ARM).** The interest rate changes during the loan:

- **Rate = index + margin.**
- The **index** is an outside market rate. It **moves**.
- The **margin** is the lender's fixed add-on, written in the loan. It **does not move**.
- **Caps** limit the changes. A **periodic cap** limits how much the rate can change at each adjustment. A **lifetime cap** limits how much it can change over the whole life of the loan.
- Some ARMs start with a **teaser rate**: a low introductory rate that later gives way to index + margin, within the caps.

*Example (illustrative contract terms, not legal limits):* start rate 6.75% (index 4.25% + margin 2.50%), periodic cap 2%, lifetime cap 5%. At the first adjustment the index rises to 7.00%. Index + margin is now 9.50%, but the periodic cap allows at most 6.75% + 2% = **8.75%**. The rate can never exceed 6.75% + 5% = **11.75%**.

### Purpose types

| Loan | What it covers or does |
|---|---|
| **Package mortgage** | **Real and personal property** in one loan (for example, a furnished condo) |
| **Blanket mortgage** | **Several parcels** under one mortgage, with a **partial release clause** that frees each parcel as it is paid down (a developer's loan) |
| **Wraparound mortgage** | A **new junior loan that includes an existing loan**. The buyer pays the wrap lender (often the seller), who keeps paying the old loan. **The existing loan must have no due-on-sale clause** |
| **Purchase-money mortgage** | **Seller financing**: the seller becomes the lender for part of the price |
| **Home equity loan / HELOC** | Borrowing against equity the owner has already built; a HELOC (home equity line of credit) is drawn as needed |
| **Reverse mortgage** | For owners **62 or older** (manual; not yet verified, V-26). The **lender pays the owner**; the loan is **repaid on sale, move-out or death** |
| **Construction loan** | Money released in stages (**draws**) as building progresses, then replaced by permanent **"take-out"** financing |

**Why does a wraparound need "no due-on-sale clause"?** A due-on-sale (alienation) clause lets the lender demand the whole balance when the property is conveyed (L12-1). The wrap only works if the old loan can stay in place after the sale, so a due-on-sale clause would defeat it.

**Why does a reverse mortgage balance grow?** Money flows to the owner and nothing is repaid until sale, move-out or death, so the debt rises over time: the reverse of an amortized loan.

**Home equity loans and rescission (verified, V-51).** Under the federal Truth in Lending rules, credit secured by the borrower's principal dwelling, such as a home equity loan or a refinance with a new lender, comes with a **3-business-day right of rescission**. Saturdays count as business days. The right does **not** apply to a loan used to buy or build the home.

## 4. Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Amortized loan | Level payments that cover interest and principal until the loan is fully repaid |
| Balloon payment | The large final payment on a partially amortized loan |
| Interest-only (term) loan | Payments cover interest only; all principal is due at the end |
| ARM | Adjustable-rate mortgage: rate = index + margin, limited by caps |
| Index | The outside market rate that moves an ARM's rate |
| Margin | The lender's fixed add-on to the index |
| Periodic / lifetime cap | Limit on the rate change at each adjustment / over the life of the loan |
| Junior loan | A loan with lower lien priority than an existing loan |
| Package mortgage | Covers real and personal property |
| Blanket mortgage | Covers several parcels; a release clause frees parcels as they are paid |
| Wraparound mortgage | A new junior loan that includes an existing loan |
| Purchase-money mortgage | Seller financing: the seller lends part of the price |
| HELOC | Home equity line of credit |
| Reverse mortgage | The lender pays an owner aged 62+; repaid on sale, move-out or death |
| Draws / take-out loan | Staged payouts of a construction loan / the permanent loan that replaces it |

## 5. Practical example

**A developer in Lakeland.** Palmetto Ridge LLC finances 20 home sites with **one blanket mortgage** that has a **partial release clause**. When a lot sells, part of the proceeds pays down the blanket loan and the lender **releases** that lot, so the buyer receives it free of the developer's mortgage. Each house is built with a **construction loan** paid out in **draws** (foundation, framing, roof, finish); when it is finished, the buyer's permanent loan **takes out** the construction loan. The furnished model home sells "turnkey", and its buyer finances the house **and** the furniture with one **package mortgage**.

**A seller in Sarasota.** Rosa sells her house for $400,000. She still owes $150,000 on a loan that has **no due-on-sale clause**. The buyer pays $40,000 down, and Rosa carries a **$360,000 wraparound** note:

- $400,000 − $40,000 = **$360,000** wrap loan.
- It includes the $150,000 old loan, so Rosa's new money lent is $360,000 − $150,000 = **$210,000**.
- The buyer pays Rosa on $360,000. Rosa keeps paying her own lender on the $150,000.

Rosa is the lender, so this is also **purchase-money** (seller) financing. If her old loan had contained a due-on-sale clause, the sale would let her lender demand the full $150,000, and the wrap would fail.

**A retiree in Naples.** Mrs. Costa, aged 70, has substantial equity and wants income without moving. A **reverse mortgage** fits her age (62 or older; manual; not yet verified, V-26): the lender pays her, and the loan is repaid when she sells, moves out or dies.

## 7. Legal and numerical requirements

**Memorize exactly**

All loan definitions in this list: manual; not yet verified. The rescission item is the only verified one (V-51).

- **Amortized**: level payments; interest falls, principal rises; fully repaid.
- **Partially amortized** → **balloon** final payment. **Interest-only (term)** → whole principal due at the end.
- **ARM rate = index (moves) + margin (fixed)**; periodic and lifetime **caps**; sometimes a teaser rate.
- **Package** = real + personal property. **Blanket** = several parcels + partial **release clauses**.
- **Wraparound** = new junior loan that includes an existing loan; the existing loan **must have no due-on-sale clause**.
- **Purchase-money** = seller financing. **Home equity / HELOC** = borrowing against existing equity.
- **Reverse mortgage**: owners **62+** (V-26); lender pays the owner; repaid on **sale, move-out or death**.
- **Construction loan**: **draws**; replaced by permanent **take-out** financing.
- Home equity loan or refinance with a new lender on the principal dwelling: **3 business days** to rescind, Saturdays count; not for loans to buy or build the home (V-51, verified).

**Understand**

- Interest is charged on the unpaid balance. That single fact explains every repayment shape.
- Caps limit **how much** the rate can change; they do not freeze it.
- The purpose label and the shape label are independent: one loan can carry both.
- A wrap leaves the old loan in place; it does not pay it off.

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"In an ARM, the margin moves with the market."** No. The **index** moves; the margin is fixed.
- **"The ARM rate is set by the Fed's discount rate."** No. The rate is **index + margin** under the loan contract.
- **"Caps mean the rate cannot rise."** No. Caps limit the **size** of changes, per adjustment and over the life of the loan.
- **"On an amortized loan the payment falls over time."** No. The payment is level; only the interest/principal mix changes.
- **"A package mortgage covers several parcels."** No, that is a **blanket** mortgage. A package covers real **and personal** property.
- **"With a blanket mortgage, nothing can be sold until the whole loan is repaid."** No. The **release clause** frees each parcel as it is paid.
- **"A wraparound pays off the old loan."** No. The old loan stays in place inside the wrap, which is why it must have no due-on-sale clause.
- **"In a reverse mortgage the owner makes monthly payments to the lender."** No. The **lender pays the owner**.
- **"The 3-business-day rescission right applies to the loan used to buy the home."** No. It applies to credit secured by the principal dwelling, such as a home equity loan, but not to loans to buy or build it (V-51).

## 10. Memory sheet

- Interest is charged on the **unpaid balance**: amortized (to zero) · balloon (lump at the end) · interest-only (all principal at the end).
- **ARM = index (moves) + margin (fixed)**, within periodic and lifetime **caps**; teaser = low start rate.
- **Package** = real + personal. **Blanket** = many parcels + release. **Wrap** = junior loan around an old loan with no due-on-sale clause.
- **Purchase-money** = seller lends. **HELOC** = borrow against existing equity; if secured by the principal dwelling, 3-business-day rescission, Saturdays count (V-51).
- **Reverse** = **62+** (V-26, not yet verified); lender pays the owner; repaid on sale, move-out or death.
- **Construction** = draws → take-out loan.
