## 2. Why it matters

Florida licensees lease and manage property for owners. Renting and leasing for others for compensation is licensed activity (the R and L in A BAR SALE, L02-1). Commercial deals are negotiated in terms such as **triple net** and **percentage rent**, and when a building is sold you must know which leases the buyer inherits.

On the exam (Section VIII, Property Rights, 8%, V-02) these are mostly recognition questions: a tenancy or lease is described and you name it. The favourite traps are the **estate for years**, which can be shorter than a year, and the **assignment** that looks like a sublease because it covers only part of the original term.

## 3. Simple explanation

### A leasehold is possession without ownership

In L08-1 you saw that a freehold is ownership. A **leasehold (nonfreehold) estate** is the tenant's right to **possess** property for a time **without owning it**. The owner (the **landlord**, or **lessor**) gives possession to the **tenant** (the **lessee**) and keeps ownership, so the landlord can still sell.

### The four leasehold estates

What separates them is **how the tenancy ends**.

| Estate | How it works | How it ends |
|---|---|---|
| **Estate for years** | Any **fixed term** with a definite start and end: one week, 6 months, 3 years | **Automatically** on the end date; **no notice** needed |
| **Periodic tenancy** | Renews automatically **period to period**: week to week, month to month, quarter to quarter, year to year | Only when a party gives **notice** |
| **Tenancy at will** | No fixed duration | By **proper notice** |
| **Tenancy at sufferance** | A **holdover** tenant: the right to possess has ended, and the tenant stays **without the landlord's consent** | Nothing is left to end by notice: the right to stay has already ended, and the tenant remains without consent |

**Why does an estate for years need no notice?** Both parties signed knowing the end date, so the calendar ends it. The name misleads on purpose: "for years" does not mean "at least a year".

**Why does a periodic tenancy need notice?** It renews itself every period, so someone must stop the renewal. For Florida **residential** tenancies without a specific term, s. 83.57 (current text, verified V-04) requires **written notice before the end of a period**:

| Period | Minimum notice |
|---|---|
| Year to year | 60 days |
| Quarter to quarter | 30 days |
| Month to month | 30 days |
| Week to week | 7 days |

The study manual gives **15 days** for month to month and **45 days** for quarter to quarter. Fifteen days for month to month is the **outdated** pre-change rule that older books and some practice questions still use; the manual's 45 days for quarter to quarter does not match the verified statute. Use **60 / 30 / 30 / 7** (more in L07-4). If a question uses 15 days, it reflects the old law: answer with current law unless the question says otherwise, and note the edition of the question source (V-04).

### The lease as a document

A **lease** is two things at once:

- a **contract**: promises about rent, use and repairs; and
- a **conveyance**: it transfers a leasehold estate (possession) to the tenant.

Because the lease conveys an estate, the tenant's right does not disappear when the landlord sells. **A lease survives the sale**, and the buyer takes the property **subject to existing leases**.

**Elements of a lease:** parties, description of the property, term, rent, signatures.

**Writing and witnesses:**

- A lease for **more than one year** must be **in writing** (statute of frauds; verified V-05). Because the writing rule covers only leases over one year, a lease of one year or less may be oral.
- **No witnesses are required for a lease.** Current s. 689.01 still requires **two subscribing witnesses** on deeds, but it now states that no subscribing witnesses are required for a lease of real property (verified V-05). The manual's rule that leases over one year need two witnesses is **outdated**.

### Lease types: who pays the property's expenses?

| Lease type | How rent works | Where you see it |
|---|---|---|
| **Gross** | Tenant pays a **fixed rent**; the **landlord pays** the operating expenses | Typical for apartments |
| **Net** | Tenant pays rent **plus some or all** of the property expenses | Common in commercial property |
| **Triple net** | Tenant pays rent plus **taxes, insurance and maintenance** (all three) | Common in commercial property |
| **Percentage** | **Base rent plus a percentage of the tenant's gross sales**, usually only on sales above a **breakpoint** | Retail stores |
| **Ground** | A **long-term lease of land**; the **tenant builds** on it | Land for a tenant's building |
| **Graduated (step-up)** | Rent rises on a **schedule written in the lease** | Increases known in advance |
| **Index** | Rent moves with an **index**, such as the **CPI** (Consumer Price Index, a measure of inflation) | Increases follow inflation, not a schedule |

**Percentage rent, worked through (illustrative numbers).** A boutique in an Orlando shopping center pays base rent of **$4,000 a month** plus **5%** of annual gross sales above a **$600,000** breakpoint. This year's gross sales are **$750,000**.

- Base rent: $4,000 × 12 = **$48,000**
- Sales above the breakpoint: $750,000 − $600,000 = $150,000
- Percentage rent: $150,000 × 0.05 = **$7,500**
- Total rent for the year: $48,000 + $7,500 = **$55,500**

### Assignment vs sublease

A tenant who wants to leave early, or to share the space, can transfer the leasehold in one of two ways. **The lease itself can restrict either one.**

| | **Assignment** | **Sublease** |
|---|---|---|
| What is transferred | The **entire remaining term**: all the time left and all the space | **Part** of the remaining term **or** part of the space |
| New occupant | **Assignee** | **Subtenant** |
| Who pays the landlord | The **assignee pays the landlord** directly | The **subtenant pays the original tenant**, who pays the landlord |
| Original tenant's liability | **Secondarily liable** (a backup if the assignee does not pay), **unless the landlord releases** her | Stays **primarily liable** to the landlord |

**Measure against what remains, not against the original term.** Suppose a tenant has 20 months left on a 5-year lease. If she transfers all 20 months, that is an **assignment**, even though 20 months is a fraction of the original 60. If she transfers 12 of the 20 months, or half the space, it is a **sublease**.

A **release** ends the original tenant's backup liability. One way to get it is a **novation**: a new agreement with the landlord that puts the new tenant in place of the original one and releases her.

## 4. Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Leasehold (nonfreehold) estate | Right of possession under a lease, without ownership |
| Landlord (lessor) / tenant (lessee) | The owner who leases / the person who receives possession |
| Estate for years | Lease for a fixed term of any length; ends without notice |
| Periodic tenancy | Tenancy that renews period to period until notice |
| Tenancy at will (also "estate at will") | Tenancy with no fixed duration, ended by proper notice |
| Tenancy at sufferance (also "estate at sufferance") | Holdover tenant who stays without the landlord's consent |
| Statute of frauds | Rule requiring certain contracts, including leases over one year, to be in writing |
| Net / triple net lease | Tenant also pays some / all of taxes, insurance and maintenance |
| Percentage lease / breakpoint | Base rent plus a percentage of gross sales / the sales level above which the percentage applies |
| Assignment / assignee | Transfer of the entire remaining term / the person who receives it |
| Sublease / subtenant | Transfer of part of the term or space / the person who receives it |
| Primarily / secondarily liable | Must pay first / must pay only if the primary payer does not |
| Novation | Agreement with the landlord that substitutes a new tenant and releases the original one |

## 5. Practical example

**Bayshore Plaza** is a small mixed-use building in Tampa. Its owner, **Mr. Ferreira**, is selling it, and you are preparing the list of tenants for buyers.

| Space | Facts | Classification |
|---|---|---|
| Café | 5-year lease with a fixed end date; pays rent plus property taxes, insurance and maintenance | Estate for years; **triple net** lease |
| Shoe store | 3-year lease; base rent plus 6% of gross sales above $400,000 a year | Estate for years; **percentage** lease |
| Apartment upstairs | Written agreement with no end date; rent is due monthly, and it renews month to month | **Periodic tenancy** (month to month) |

Three things happen before closing:

1. **The café owner sells her business** with 20 months left on the lease and transfers all 20 months to the buyer of the business, who will pay Mr. Ferreira directly. This is an **assignment**. The café owner stays **secondarily liable** unless Mr. Ferreira releases her.
2. **The shoe store rents half its space** to a phone-repair kiosk. This is a **sublease** (part of the space). The kiosk pays the shoe store, and the shoe store stays **primarily liable** to Mr. Ferreira.
3. **Mr. Ferreira wants the apartment empty** for the new owner. He must give **written notice at least 30 days before the end of a monthly period** (s. 83.57, V-04).

**The sale itself:** the buyer of Bayshore Plaza takes the building **subject to** the café's and the shoe store's leases. The new owner cannot cancel them just because the building changed hands.

## 6. Portugal comparison

**Familiar principle.** You already know residential and commercial leasing in practice. In Portugal, urban leases are governed by the Civil Code and the NRAU; their written-form and registration requirements require verification.

**Florida rule.** A lease of one year or less may be oral. A longer lease must be in writing (statute of frauds) but needs **no witnesses** (s. 689.01, V-05). Residential tenancies without a specific term end with **60 / 30 / 30 / 7** days' written notice (s. 83.57, V-04). The exam also uses US leasing vocabulary: gross, net, triple net, percentage, ground, graduated, index, assignment and sublease.

**Precise difference.** Florida's day counts and categories are what is tested. Portuguese notice periods must not be transferred. Whether Portuguese law draws the same assignment and sublease liability lines requires verification.

**Exam implication.** For any notice question, use the current s. 83.57 figures. For any classification question, apply the Florida definitions literally.

## 7. Legal and numerical requirements

**Memorize exactly**

*Source status: items marked "verified" rest on the course's verification log; items marked "(manual; not yet verified)" rest only on the study manual.*

- **Estate for years:** any fixed term, even one week; ends **automatically without notice** (manual; not yet verified).
- **Periodic residential tenancy, notice to end** (s. 83.57, current; verified V-04): **year 60 · quarter 30 · month 30 · week 7 days**, in writing, before the end of a period. **Outdated:** 15 days for month to month (pre-change law; answer with current law unless the question says otherwise). The manual's 45 days for quarter to quarter does not match the verified statute.
- **Tenancy at will:** no fixed duration; ends with proper notice. **Tenancy at sufferance:** holdover without consent (manual; not yet verified).
- A lease over **one year** must be **in writing** (statute of frauds) (verified V-05).
- **No subscribing witnesses** are required for leases (s. 689.01, verified V-05). **Outdated:** "two witnesses for leases over one year". Deeds still need two witnesses (verified V-05).
- **Triple net** = taxes + insurance + maintenance (manual; not yet verified).
- **Assignment** = the entire remaining term; the assignee pays the landlord; the original tenant is **secondarily** liable unless released (manual; not yet verified).
- **Sublease** = part of the term or space; the subtenant pays the tenant; the tenant stays **primarily** liable (manual; not yet verified).
- A lease **survives the sale**; the buyer takes subject to existing leases (manual; not yet verified).

**Understand**

- Source status: the leasehold categories, lease elements, lease types, assignment/sublease rules and the rule that a lease survives a sale come from the study manual. They are standard course definitions with no entry in the verification log (manual; not yet verified). The s. 83.57 notice periods (V-04) and the witness rule (V-05) are verified corrections of the manual; the writing requirement for leases over one year is part of the verified V-05 entry.
- Liability follows the chain of payment. An assignment takes the original tenant out of the payment chain, but she stays a backup until released. A sublease keeps her in the middle of the chain.
- Related disclosure (L07-4): a **residential lease of 1 year or longer** requires the landlord's **flood disclosure** at or before signing (since October 1, 2025; verified V-07). Shorter leases are not covered. Older books will not mention it.

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"An estate for years must last a year and needs notice to end."** It is any fixed term, even one week, and the end date ends it automatically.
- **"Transferring the last 14 months of a 3-year lease is a sublease."** It is an assignment. Measure against what remains.
- **"After an assignment, the original tenant is free."** She stays secondarily liable unless the landlord releases her.
- **"A subtenant pays the landlord."** The subtenant pays the original tenant, who stays primarily liable.
- **"Month to month needs 15 days' notice."** That is outdated. Current s. 83.57 requires 30 days.
- **"Florida leases over one year need two witnesses."** That is outdated. No witnesses are required, although a lease over one year must still be in writing.
- **"A gross lease is based on gross sales."** A gross lease has fixed rent, and the landlord pays expenses. The lease based on sales is the percentage lease.
- **"When the building is sold, the leases end."** The buyer takes subject to existing leases.

## 10. Memory sheet

- Fixed term = **estate for years** (no notice). Renewing = **periodic** (60 / 30 / 30 / 7). No fixed duration = **at will**. Holdover without consent = **sufferance**.
- Lease = contract + conveyance, and it **survives the sale**.
- Over 1 year → in writing. Witnesses → **none** now (deeds still need two).
- Gross: landlord pays expenses. Net: tenant pays some or all. Triple net: taxes + insurance + maintenance. Percentage: base + % of sales above the breakpoint. Ground: land, tenant builds. Graduated: steps. Index: CPI.
- **All that remains = assignment** (assignee → landlord; tenant secondary). **Part = sublease** (subtenant → tenant; tenant primary).
