## 2. Why it matters

**In practice.** Investors and homeowners measure success in percentages: *"I made 18% on that townhouse"*, *"our condo has gone up 15% since we bought it"*. When a seller says "I sold at a 15% profit for $276,000", you should be able to say at once what they paid. The answer is not $234,600.

**On the exam.** Profit on cost is pattern 4 of the study manual's twelve math patterns, and percentage of change is one of its two extra patterns. Both sit in Section 14, which carries **6%** of the exam (V-02). The manual calls percentages taken of **different bases** the single most-missed idea in exam math. The trap answer is usually waiting among the choices.

## 3. Simple explanation

### Step 1: What it represents

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Cost** | What the owner paid for the property (the *base* of every profit or loss percentage) |
| **Sale price** | What the property sold for |
| **Profit** | Sale price − cost, when the sale price is higher |
| **Loss** | Cost − sale price, when the sale price is lower |
| **Appreciation** | An increase in value over time, measured on the original value |
| **Depreciation (in value)** | A decrease in market value over time, measured on the original value |

Profit and appreciation are the same calculation as the percentage change in L14-1. The base is always the **starting** amount: what was paid, or the original value.

### Step 2: Units

The amounts are in dollars. The rates are percentages **of cost**. Two multipliers do most of the work:

| Situation | Multiplier | At 15% |
|---|---|---|
| Profit or appreciation | 1 + rate | 1.15 |
| Loss or depreciation | 1 − rate | 0.85 |

The multiplier turns the cost into the sale price in one step: a 15% profit means the sale price is 115% of the cost.

### Step 3: Formula

- **Profit (or loss) in dollars** = Sale − Cost
- **Profit or loss percentage = (Sale − Cost) ÷ Cost** (positive = profit, negative = loss)
- **Sale = Cost × (1 + profit%)**, or Sale = Cost × (1 − loss%)
- **Cost = Sale ÷ (1 + profit%)**, or **Cost = Sale ÷ (1 − loss%)**

For appreciation, replace "cost" with "original value". If a value changes by a percentage **each year**, apply the multiplier once per year, because each year's percentage is taken of that year's starting value.

### Step 4: When it applies

- **"Profit on cost", "on what she paid", "since purchase"** → the cost (original value) is the base. This is the normal case. If a question explicitly says the percentage is based on the sale price, follow the question.
- **Is the cost known?** Then multiply (to find the sale price) or divide the difference by the cost (to find the percentage).
- **Is the cost the unknown?** Then the percentage is "of" the unknown, so **divide** the sale price by (1 + profit%) or (1 − loss%). Divide, do not multiply.
- **Improvements.** If the question tells you to include money spent on improvements, add it to the cost before calculating.

### Step 5: Worked example

**A. Recover the cost (profit).** An investor sold a property for $276,000 at a 15% profit. What did she pay?

1. Sale = Cost × 1.15, so Cost = $276,000 ÷ 1.15 = **$240,000**.
2. Check: $240,000 × 1.15 = $276,000 ✓. Profit $36,000 ÷ $240,000 = 15% ✓.
3. **Never** $276,000 × 0.85 = $234,600. That takes 15% of the **sale price**, the wrong base.

**B. Loss percentage.** A Daytona Beach condo bought for $325,000 sells for $286,000.

1. Loss: $325,000 − $286,000 = $39,000.
2. $39,000 ÷ $325,000 (the cost) = 0.12 = **12% loss**.

**C. Recover the cost (loss).** A lot sold for $255,000, a 15% loss on what the owner paid.

1. Sale = Cost × 0.85, so Cost = $255,000 ÷ 0.85 = **$300,000**.
2. Check: $300,000 × 0.85 = $255,000 ✓.

**D. Appreciation over two years.** A $300,000 home appreciates 4% a year for 2 years.

1. Year 1: $300,000 × 1.04 = $312,000.
2. Year 2: $312,000 × 1.04 = **$324,480**.
3. Total gain: $24,480 ÷ $300,000 = **8.16%**, not 8%. The second year's 4% was taken of a larger base.

### Step 6: Reasonableness check

- **Profit:** the cost must be **less** than the sale price. **Loss:** the cost must be **more** than the sale price.
- Multiply back: cost × (1 ± rate) must give the sale price exactly.
- The correct cost and the trap answer are close. In Example A, $240,000 and $234,600 differ by only $5,400. Only the multiply-back check separates them reliably.

### Step 7: Common errors

| Error | Example | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Taking the percent of the sale price | $276,000 × 0.85 = $234,600 | $276,000 ÷ 1.15 = $240,000 |
| Adding the percent to the sale price | $276,000 × 1.15 = $317,400 | That moves in the wrong direction entirely |
| Using the sale price as the base for the % | $36,000 ÷ $276,000 ≈ 13% | Divide by the **cost**: $36,000 ÷ $240,000 = 15% |
| Dividing by (1 + %) for a loss | $255,000 ÷ 1.15 | Loss → divide by (1 − %) = 0.85 |
| Adding yearly percentages | 4% + 4% = 8% | Apply 1.04 twice = 8.16% |
| Confusing a fall in market value with tax depreciation | Treating a market loss like straight-line depreciation | Tax depreciation (residential rental 27.5 years, nonresidential 39 years, straight-line, never land; V-77) is a different topic |

### Step 10: Exam application

1. **Find the cost (or original value).** Is it given, or is it the unknown?
2. **Known cost:** percentage = (sale − cost) ÷ cost, or sale = cost × (1 ± rate).
3. **Unknown cost:** cost = sale ÷ (1 + profit%) or sale ÷ (1 − loss%).
4. **Multiply back** to confirm.

The answer choices usually include the trap figures. For "sold for $276,000 at a 15% profit", expect to see $234,600 (× 0.85) and $317,400 (× 1.15) next to the correct **$240,000**. When a percentage is "of" the unknown, divide.

## 4. Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Cost | The amount paid for the property; the base for profit or loss |
| Sale price | The amount received on the sale |
| Profit | Sale price − cost (sale above cost) |
| Loss | Cost − sale price (sale below cost) |
| Percentage profit (loss) on cost | (Sale − cost) ÷ cost |
| Appreciation | An increase in value over time, measured on the original value |
| Depreciation (market) | A decrease in value over time; not the same as tax depreciation |
| Multiplier | 1 + rate (gain) or 1 − rate (loss); converts the cost into the sale price |
| Compounding | Applying a yearly percentage to each year's new starting value |

## 5. Practical example

**Two clients in Orlando.**

**Carlos** tells you he sold a townhouse last year for **$295,000** "at an 18% profit". How much did he pay?

- Cost = $295,000 ÷ 1.18 = **$250,000**. Check: $250,000 × 1.18 = $295,000 ✓. Profit: $45,000.
- If you had computed $295,000 × 0.82 = $241,900, you would have overstated his profit by $8,100.

**Marta** bought a condo for **$380,000** and a recent valuation suggests **$437,000**. How much has it appreciated?

- Gain: $437,000 − $380,000 = $57,000. $57,000 ÷ $380,000 (the original value) = **15%**.
- Marta then asks: "If I sell at $437,000, what percentage of that price is my gain?" $57,000 ÷ $437,000 ≈ 13%. That is a percentage of the **sale price**, a different question with a different base. In exam language, her profit is 15% **on cost** (before any selling costs, such as the commission in L14-3).

## 6. Portugal comparison

**Familiar principle.** Profit, loss and appreciation percentages are pure arithmetic, so there is no meaningful difference between Portugal and Florida here. The course's comparison entries contain nothing specific to this lesson.

**Florida rule (exam).** Profit or loss is a percentage **of cost**: (sale − cost) ÷ cost. The cost is recovered by dividing.

**Precise difference.** None in the math. How a gain is **taxed** is a separate topic in each country. The US federal rules are taught in the tax lesson (for example, the home-sale exclusion, V-78). Portuguese tax rules on gains are not in the course's comparison entries and **require verification**.

**Exam implication.** Treat "profit" questions as arithmetic, not tax questions, unless the question mentions tax.

## 7. Legal and numerical requirements

**Memorize exactly**

- **Profit or loss % = (Sale − Cost) ÷ Cost** (measured on cost).
- **Cost = Sale ÷ (1 + profit%)**; for a loss, **Cost = Sale ÷ (1 − loss%)**.
- $276,000 at a 15% profit → **$240,000**, never $276,000 × 0.85 = $234,600.

**Understand**

- No statute is involved: this is the percent triangle with the cost as the total.
- When a percentage is "of" the unknown, divide.
- A gain followed by an equal percentage loss does not bring you back to the start, and yearly percentages compound.
- A fall in market value is not the same thing as tax depreciation (V-77).

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"A 15% profit means the cost was 85% of the sale price."** No. The **sale** is 115% of the cost, so divide by 1.15.
- **"Profit percentage is measured on the sale price."** No. It is measured **on cost** unless the question says otherwise.
- **"Up 10% then down 10% is break-even."** No. $250,000 → $275,000 → $247,500, a 1% loss.
- **"4% a year for two years is 8%."** No. Compounded, it is 8.16%.
- **"For a loss, divide by 1.15 too."** No. A 15% loss means dividing by **0.85**.

## 10. Memory sheet

- **Profit is on cost:** (Sale − Cost) ÷ Cost.
- **Cost known → multiply:** Sale = Cost × (1 ± rate).
- **Cost unknown → divide:** Cost = Sale ÷ (1 + profit%) or ÷ (1 − loss%).
- **$276,000 ÷ 1.15 = $240,000**, never × 0.85 ($234,600).
- Yearly percentages compound; equal % up then down ≠ break-even.
- When a percent is "of" the unknown, divide. Always multiply back.
