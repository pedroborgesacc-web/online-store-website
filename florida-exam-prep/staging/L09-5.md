## 2. Why it matters

**In practice.** "Does the chandelier stay?" is a classic fight between buyer and seller. The answer depends on whether the item is a **fixture** (part of the real property, sold with the house) or **personal property** (the seller's to take). In commercial leasing you will meet business tenants' equipment bolted to the floor. And Florida has rivers, large lakes and two coastlines, so waterfront buyers ask what they own and what happens when water or a hurricane moves the shoreline.

**On the exam.** Section 9, "Title, Deeds and Ownership Restrictions", carries about 7% of the exam (V-02). Expect a fact pattern where you apply the fixture tests (intent wins), the trade-fixture trap (equipment bolted down by a business tenant), riparian versus littoral, and accretion versus avulsion after a storm.

## 3. Simple explanation

**Source status.** No verification entry covers this lesson. Every rule below comes from the study manual *(manual; not yet verified)*. It is standard exam vocabulary, but it has not been checked against an official Florida source in this course.

### The foundation: real property or personal property?

**Real property** is the land and what is permanently attached to it; **personal property** is movable. The deed conveys the real property. A **fixture** is personal property that becomes real property when it is attached: a ceiling fan in a box at the hardware store is personal property; once the owner wires it into the ceiling, it is part of the house. Because a fixture is real property, it goes to the buyer with the land.

### The four fixture tests (intent wins)

When it is unclear whether an item became a fixture, courts apply four tests *(manual; not yet verified)*:

| Test | Question | Points toward a fixture | Points away |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Intent** (dominant) | Did the person who attached it mean it to stay permanently? | Owner installs a kitchen island to improve the home | Seller states in writing that the antique chandelier is excluded |
| **Method of annexation** | How is it attached? | Bolted, wired in, plumbed, cemented | Resting on the floor, plugged into a socket |
| **Adaptation to the realty** | Was it made or fitted for this property? | Hurricane shutters custom-made for these windows, on permanent tracks | A standard item that fits anywhere |
| **Relationship of the parties** | Who installed it, in what role? | Owner improving their own home | Tenant installing equipment for its business |

**Why intent dominates.** The other three tests are really *evidence* of intent. Bolts, custom fitting and the installer's role are clues; intent is the conclusion. In practice, the clearest evidence of intent is what the parties write: list in the contract what stays and what goes.

### Trade fixtures: the business tenant's exception

**Trade fixtures** are items a business tenant installs for its trade (restaurant ovens, salon chairs, store shelving). They **remain the tenant's personal property**, even if bolted down, and the tenant **must remove them by the end of the lease**. If the tenant leaves them behind, they become the landlord's by **accession** (ownership gained because something was added to, or left on, your property) *(manual; not yet verified)*.

Why? Relationship and intent: a business tenant installs equipment to run its business, not to give it to the landlord. That is why the trade-fixture rule beats the method of attachment.

### Emblements: crops as personal property

**Emblements** are annual crops produced by labour, such as a tenant farmer's tomatoes. They are **personal property**, and a tenant farmer may harvest crops planted before the tenancy ended *(manual; not yet verified)*.

### Water rights: which water, which word

*(manual; not yet verified)*

| Term | Type of water | Florida example |
|---|---|---|
| **Riparian rights** | Rivers and streams | A home on the St. Johns River |
| **Littoral rights** | Oceans, seas and large lakes | A condo on the Atlantic; a home on Lake Okeechobee |

Memory aid: **ri**parian = **ri**ver; *littoral* sounds like "litoral", the coast.

On the coast, **the State of Florida owns the land seaward of the mean high-water line** (the average high-tide line). The beach seaward of that line is state land, not part of the private lot.

### When the water moves the land

| Event | Speed | What happens | Boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Accretion** | Gradual | Water slowly deposits soil or sand; the owner gains the new land | Moves |
| **Erosion** | Gradual | Water slowly washes land away; the owner loses it | Moves |
| **Avulsion** | Sudden | A hurricane rips the shoreline or a river suddenly changes channel | **Does not move** |

One way to understand it: gradual change cannot be seen from day to day, so the boundary follows the water. A sudden change leaves a clear "before", so the old boundary can still be identified and stays where it was.

| Term | Meaning |

| Personal property | Movable property, not part of the land |
| Fixture | Personal property attached to real property so that it becomes part of it |
| Method of annexation | How an item is attached (bolted, wired, plumbed) |
| Adaptation to the realty | How far an item was made or fitted for this property |
| Trade fixture | Item a business tenant installs for its trade; remains the tenant's personal property |
| Accession | Gaining ownership of what is added to or left on your property |
| Emblements | Annual crops produced by labour; personal property |
| Riparian rights | Rights of owners along rivers and streams |
| Littoral rights | Rights of owners along oceans, seas and large lakes |
| Mean high-water line | Average high-tide line; Florida owns the land seaward of it |
| Accretion | Gradual addition of land by water deposits; owner gains |
| Erosion | Gradual loss of land to water |
| Avulsion | Sudden loss or change; the boundary does not move |

## 5. Practical example

**The house.** Rui, a sales associate in Jacksonville, lists Linda's home on the St. Johns River. Before writing the listing, he goes through the items with her:

| Item | Tests | Likely result |
|---|---|---|
| Ceiling fans wired into the ceilings | Method (wired in); owner installed them to improve the home | Fixture: stays |
| Hurricane shutters custom-made for the windows, on permanent tracks | Adaptation; method | Fixture: stays |
| Free-standing refrigerator, plugged in | Not attached | Personal property: goes with Linda unless the contract includes it |
| Grandmother's chandelier in the dining room | Attached, so it looks like a fixture; Linda intends to keep it | Make the intent clear: exclude it in writing in the contract, or replace it before showings |
| TV on a bolted wall bracket | Mixed signals | Uncertain under the tests: write it into the contract |

**The shop.** Linda also owns a small retail building leased to a hair salon. The salon bolted styling chairs to the floor and plumbed in shampoo sinks. The lease ends next month and Linda is selling the building. These are **trade fixtures**: they belong to the salon, not to Linda and not to the building buyer. The salon must remove them by the end of the lease; whatever it leaves behind becomes the landlord's by accession.

**The river.** Over 15 years, sand has slowly built up along Linda's riverbank: **accretion**, so the new strip is hers. A neighbour's bank has slowly washed away: **erosion**, so the neighbour lost land. Rui explains that if a hurricane suddenly cut a new channel across a lot, that would be **avulsion**: the boundary would stay where it was. Linda's rights here are **riparian** (a river).

**The beach.** The buyer is also looking at an oceanfront condo at Jacksonville Beach. Those are **littoral** rights (the Atlantic), and the beach seaward of the mean high-water line belongs to the State of Florida.

## 7. Legal and numerical requirements

**Memorize exactly**

There are no numbers in this lesson. All rules below rest on the study manual *(manual; not yet verified)*.

- Fixture = personal property that becomes real property when attached.
- Four tests: **intent** (dominant), method of annexation, adaptation to the realty, relationship of the parties.
- Trade fixtures: a business tenant's personal property; removed by the end of the lease; left behind → the landlord's by accession.
- Emblements: annual crops produced by labour = personal property; a tenant farmer may harvest crops planted before the tenancy ended.
- Riparian = rivers and streams. Littoral = oceans, seas, large lakes.
- Florida owns the land seaward of the mean high-water line.
- Accretion (gradual gain) and erosion (gradual loss) move the boundary; avulsion (sudden) does not.

**Understand**

- Method, adaptation and relationship are evidence; intent is the conclusion. Bolts alone never decide.
- The trade-fixture rule protects the business tenant, but only until the lease ends.
- In a sale, avoid fixture disputes by listing in the contract what stays and what goes.
- On the exam keep the textbook distinction: river = riparian, ocean = littoral. Outside the exam you may hear "riparian" used loosely for any waterfront (not verified in this course).
- In water questions, choices such as "prior appropriation" or "air rights" are distractors.

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"If it is bolted down, it is a fixture."** Not for a business tenant's trade fixtures, and intent, not the method of attachment, is the dominant test.
- **"Trade fixtures pass to the building buyer with the deed."** They remain the tenant's personal property.
- **"The tenant can come back for the equipment whenever it likes."** It must remove trade fixtures by the end of the lease; what it leaves becomes the landlord's by accession.
- **"Growing crops are always part of the land."** Emblements (annual crops produced by labour) are personal property, and a tenant farmer may harvest crops planted before the tenancy ended.
- **"An oceanfront owner has riparian rights."** On the exam, ocean, sea or large lake = littoral; river or stream = riparian.
- **"After a hurricane moves the shoreline, the boundary follows the new water line."** A sudden change is avulsion: the boundary does not move.
- **"Erosion and avulsion are the same thing."** Both lose land, but erosion is gradual (boundary moves) and avulsion is sudden (boundary stays).
- **"A beachfront owner owns down to the water."** Florida owns the land seaward of the mean high-water line.
