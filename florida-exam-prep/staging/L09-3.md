## 2. Why it matters

Buyers ask two questions you should be able to answer in plain words: "How do I know the seller really owns this?" and "What protects me if an old problem with the title appears after I buy?" In Florida the answers are the **public records** and **title insurance**. Recording also explains why a buyer is bound by an easement recorded decades ago, even if nobody mentioned it during the sale.

For the exam (Section 9 of the course outline, about 7% of the exam), expect questions on constructive versus actual notice, on whether recording is needed for a deed to be valid (it is not), on an abstract with an attorney's opinion versus title insurance, on the owner's versus the lender's policy, and on what title insurance does **not** cover: problems created after the policy date, and physical damage.

## 3. Simple explanation

### Why recording exists

From L09-2: a properly signed and witnessed deed is valid between grantor and grantee once it is delivered and accepted; recording is not required. But nobody else can see a deed kept in a drawer. **Recording** places the document in the **county public records**, where anyone can search it. Recording does not make a deed valid. It **warns the world**.

### Two kinds of notice

- **Constructive notice** is notice the law presumes from the **public records** and from **visible possession** of the property, whether or not the person actually looked. Once a document is recorded, everyone is treated as knowing it.
- **Actual notice** is what a person actually knows, because she was told, read a document or saw something herself.

Why does the law presume knowledge? The records are open to everyone, so a buyer who skips the search cannot rely on her own failure to look. **Visible possession** works the same way: if someone other than the seller is living on the property, a buyer is expected to find out on what basis, for example a tenant with a lease. ### Who wins when two people claim the same property

Florida's recording system protects a **good-faith purchaser who records without notice of prior claims** (manual; not yet verified). Follow the logic:

1. A seller signs and delivers a deed to Buyer A, who does **not** record it.
2. The seller dishonestly signs a second deed to Buyer B. B searches the records and finds nothing, has no other knowledge of A's deed, and records.
3. B is a good-faith purchaser without notice who recorded, so the rule protects B. A's deed was valid between A and the seller, but A carried the risk of not recording.

The protection depends on **no notice**. If B had actual notice of A's deed, B would not be protected. If A had already moved into the house, A's visible possession would give B constructive notice. The practical lesson: **record promptly**.

### Title evidence: proving marketable title

**Marketable title** is title a reasonable buyer would accept, free of serious defects and clouds (general definition, not from this course's sources; not yet verified). A Florida buyer evidences it in one of two ways:

| | Abstract of title + attorney's opinion | Title insurance |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | An **abstract** is a summary of all recorded documents affecting the title. An attorney examines it and gives an **opinion** on the title | A policy that protects against losses from title defects existing **before the policy date** |
| What it rests on | What the records show | The records, plus the insurer's promise covering defects that existed before the policy date but were **undiscovered** |
| Status | The traditional method | The **modern standard** |

(manual; not yet verified)

An abstract and an opinion can only describe what the records show. Title insurance adds protection against a hidden past defect, such as a forged deed in the property's history.

### Owner's policy and lender's policy

| | Owner's policy | Lender's policy |
|---|---|---|
| Protects | The owner (the buyer) | The lender |
| Amount | The **purchase price** | The **loan amount** |
| Over time | Protects the owner **as long as she owns** the property | **Declines** as the loan balance is paid down |
| Transfer | **Not transferable** to the next buyer | **Follows the loan** |

(manual; not yet verified)

### Title insurance looks backward

Title insurance covers defects existing **before** the policy date, even undiscovered ones. It **never** covers defects the owner creates later, such as a lien she allows to attach after closing. It does not cover later events such as a zoning change after closing, and it is not property insurance, so it does not pay for physical damage from a hurricane or flood. Why? The title work examines the property's history up to the policy date. Whatever arises after that date, whether the owner's own debts or outside changes, is outside what the policy insures. (manual; not yet verified)

**Related federal rule (V-53, confirmed).** Under Section 9 of RESPA (the federal Real Estate Settlement Procedures Act), which applies to federally related mortgage loans on 1–4 family residential property, a seller may not require the buyer to use a particular title insurer. A seller who does is liable for 3 times the title charges. RESPA is covered in a later chapter.

## 4. Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|

| Constructive notice | Notice the law presumes from the public records or visible possession, whether or not the person looked |
| Actual notice | What a person actually knows |
| Good-faith purchaser | A buyer acting honestly and without notice of prior claims |
| Marketable title | Title a reasonable buyer would accept, free of serious defects and clouds |

| Attorney's opinion | An attorney's conclusion on the title after examining the abstract |

## 5. Practical example

Sofia, a client from Lisbon, buys a house in Fort Myers (Lee County) in November 2026 for **$450,000**, with a **$360,000** mortgage loan.

| When | Event | Result | Rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| Before closing | The title search shows a utility easement recorded in 1998. Sofia never read it | She takes the property subject to the easement | Recording = constructive notice |
| Before closing | During a showing, Sofia sees that a man is living in the garage apartment | She is treated as knowing whatever rights he has (for example, a lease) and should find out before she buys. If he tells her about his lease, she also has actual notice | Visible possession = constructive notice |
| Closing | Two title policies are issued | Owner's policy: **$450,000** (the price). Lender's policy: **$360,000** (the loan) | Policy amounts |
| 2028 | A man claims that a 2015 deed in the property's history was forged | The owner's policy covers the loss | Defect existed before the policy date |
| 2029 | Sofia does not pay the contractor who built her pool, and a construction (mechanic's) lien attaches to the house | Not covered | She allowed it to attach after the policy date |
| 2030 | A hurricane damages the roof | Not a title insurance matter | Physical damage is not a title defect |
| 2030 | The loan balance is down to $330,000 | The lender's coverage has declined with the balance | Lender's policy declines |
| 2031 | Sofia sells to Marco | Her owner's policy does not pass to Marco; his protection must come from a new policy | Owner's policy is not transferable |

**A second file.** Sofia's neighbor bought his lot from a seller who, a week earlier, had sold the same lot to someone else by a deed that was never recorded. The neighbor searched the records, knew nothing of the earlier sale, saw nobody occupying the lot, and recorded his deed the same day. As a good-faith purchaser who recorded without notice, he is the one Florida's rule protects.

## 6. Portugal comparison

**Familiar principle.** In Portugal, ownership and charges are recorded in the land registry (*registo predial*). The degree of protection that registry gives a buyer requires verification (PT-11).

**Florida rule.** Documents are recorded in county public records, which give constructive notice. Buyers prove marketable title with an abstract plus an attorney's opinion or, usually, title insurance: an owner's policy for the price and a lender's policy for the loan.

**Precise difference.** Title insurance is a central product in Florida. In Florida, the public records give notice, and protection against a hidden past defect, such as a forged deed, comes from title insurance. The exam tests the owner's versus the lender's policy and what is covered: past defects, not future ones. Example (PT-11): a forged deed from 2015 surfaces after closing, and the owner's title policy covers the loss; a lien the new owner creates in 2027 is not covered. Never use a Portuguese registry rule to answer a Florida question.

**Exam implication.** Recording protects against third parties; it is not required for a deed to be valid. Memory aid: **record to warn the world; insure against the past.**

- Title insurance is not property insurance and does not cover later events.

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"I never saw the recorded easement, so it does not bind me."** Recording gives constructive notice to everyone, whether or not they read the records.
- **"Recording makes the deed valid."** Delivery and acceptance make it valid; recording warns the world.
- **"If it is not in the records, a buyer can ignore it."** Actual knowledge, and visible possession by someone else, also give notice.
- **"Title insurance covers anything that goes wrong with the title."** It covers defects existing before the policy date, never defects the owner creates later.
- **"Title insurance pays for hurricane or flood damage."** Physical damage is not a title defect.
- **"The owner's policy passes to the next buyer."** It is not transferable.
- **"The owner's policy is issued for the loan amount."** The owner's policy is for the purchase price; the lender's policy is for the loan amount.
- **"The lender's policy protects the buyer."** It protects the lender, declines with the balance and follows the loan.
- **"An abstract of title is the same as title insurance."** An abstract summarizes the records and an attorney gives an opinion on them; title insurance is a policy covering losses from pre-policy defects.
