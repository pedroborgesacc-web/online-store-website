## 2. Why it matters

Many Florida sales involve a loan, and in a financed sale the buyer signs two separate loan documents at closing. If you know which one does what, you can read a payoff statement, explain to a seller why the old loan must be formally "satisfied", and spot the clauses that limit what an owner can do. One example is whether a buyer can take over the seller's loan.

For the exam, Residential Mortgages is about 9% of the state exam (V-02). This lesson gives you the vocabulary those questions use: note vs mortgage, mortgagor vs mortgagee, lien theory, the 60-day satisfaction rule and the clause names.

## 3. Simple explanation

> **Source status.** The 60-day satisfaction rule has been checked against the statute (s. 701.04, Fla. Stat.; verified, V-71). Lien theory and the clause definitions come from the study manual (manual; not yet verified). They are standard exam vocabulary, and the course's practice questions test them.

### Two documents, two jobs

A lender wants two things from a borrower: a **promise to pay**, and **something it can take if that promise is broken**. US practice puts them in two separate documents.

| Document | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Promissory note** | The **debt**: the borrower's personal written promise to repay, stating the amount, the interest rate and the terms |
| **Mortgage** | The **security instrument**: it pledges the property as collateral for the note |

**Language warning.** In everyday American speech, "my mortgage" often means the loan. On the exam, the **mortgage is the document that pledges the property**, not the loan. The debt itself is the note.

**Why two documents?** The note makes the borrower **personally** liable, so the lender can pursue the person. The mortgage gives the lender a claim on the **property**, so it can pursue the house. Some clauses adjust one side. For example, the exculpatory clause removes the personal side.

### Mortgagor and mortgagee

The borrower **gives** the mortgage (pledges the property) to the lender:

- **Mortgagor** = **borrower** (gives)
- **Mortgagee** = **lender** (receives)

The rule "-or gives, -ee receives" works across the exam: grantor/grantee, lessor/lessee, optionor/optionee.

### Florida is a lien-theory state

In Florida the borrower keeps **legal title**, and the mortgage gives the lender a **lien**: a claim on the property as security for the debt (manual; not yet verified). In **title-theory** states, the lender holds title, or a trustee holds it under a **deed of trust**.

In practice, a buyer who finances in Florida receives the deed and owns the property, and the lender's protection is its lien. Because the lender holds only a lien, not title, an unpaid lender reaches the property through foreclosure (L12-2).

### Payoff: satisfaction within 60 days

Paying the last dollar ends the debt, but the mortgage stays in the public records until it is released. Florida law puts that duty on the lender. Within **60 days after receiving full payment**, the mortgagee (or the holder of the loan, or its servicer, the company that collects the payments) must:

1. sign a written **satisfaction** (or release);
2. have it acknowledged (the signer formally confirms the signature before an official such as a notary);
3. record it in the county official records;
4. send the recorded satisfaction to the borrower (mortgagor) or the record owner.

The rule is in s. 701.04, Fla. Stat. (verified, V-71). The current text also covers payment of the payoff amount in an **estoppel letter**, which is the lender's written statement of what it takes to pay the loan off.

### The clauses: each answers a "what if?"

| Clause | What it does | When it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Acceleration** | Makes the **entire** balance due on default | The borrower stops paying. The lender can then demand the whole balance at once, not just the missed payments. This is the clause that makes foreclosure practical |
| **Due-on-sale (alienation)** | Makes the balance due if the property is transferred | The owner sells. It blocks an **assumption** without the lender's consent, because the lender chose this borrower, not her buyer |
| **Prepayment** | Gives a right to pay early, or sets a penalty for doing so | Early payoff |
| **Defeasance** | The lien is defeated (cancelled) on full payment | The loan is paid in full |
| **Subordination** | A lien agrees to take a **junior** (lower-priority) position | Common when a land seller finances the land and a construction lender demands first place |
| **Release** | Frees individual parcels from a **blanket mortgage** (one mortgage on several parcels) | A developer sells lots one at a time |
| **Receivership** | A receiver collects the rents during foreclosure | Income property in default |
| **Exculpatory** | The borrower is **not personally liable**. The lender's only recourse is the property | A foreclosure sale falls short: **no deficiency** claim against the borrower |

**How the end of the loan fits together.** Defeasance is the promise in the mortgage that the lien ends on full payment. The **satisfaction** is the recorded proof, due within 60 days.

## 4. Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Promissory note | The borrower's written promise to repay the debt |
| Mortgage | Security instrument pledging property for a debt. In US usage, the document, not the loan |
| Mortgagor / mortgagee | Borrower (gives the mortgage) / lender (receives it) |
| Lien theory | The borrower keeps title and the lender holds a lien (Florida) |
| Title theory | The lender, or a trustee under a deed of trust, holds title |
| Satisfaction of mortgage | Recorded document showing the mortgage is paid and the lien released |
| Estoppel letter | The lender's written statement of the payoff amount |
| Acceleration clause | Lets the lender demand the entire balance on default |
| Due-on-sale (alienation) clause | Makes the balance due if the property is transferred |
| Defeasance clause | The mortgage is defeated (cancelled) when the debt is paid |
| Exculpatory clause | The borrower is not personally liable; the lender looks only to the property |

## 5. Practical example

**Camila buys a condo in Fort Lauderdale** for $380,000 and borrows $304,000. At closing she signs a **promissory note** for $304,000, which is her personal promise to repay at the stated rate and terms. She also signs a **mortgage** pledging the condo as security. She is the **mortgagor** and the bank is the **mortgagee**. Under lien theory, the seller's deed gives title to Camila and the bank holds a lien.

Her mortgage has an **acceleration** clause, so a default makes the whole balance due. It has a **due-on-sale** clause, so a future buyer cannot take over the loan without the bank's consent. Its **prepayment** clause says whether she may pay early without a penalty, and its **defeasance** clause ends the lien once the loan is paid.

**Twelve years later she sells.** The closing agent obtains an estoppel letter and pays the payoff amount from the sale proceeds. From the date the bank receives that payment, it has **60 days** to sign the satisfaction, have it acknowledged, record it in the county official records and send it to Camila. If it does not and she sues to enforce the duty, the **prevailing party** recovers attorney fees and costs (V-71).

**A developer's loan, for contrast.** Near Ocala, a landowner finances part of a developer's land purchase. The construction lender demands first place, so the landowner **subordinates** her mortgage. The construction loan is a **blanket mortgage** with a **release clause**, so each lot can be freed from it when that lot is sold.

## 7. Legal and numerical requirements

**Memorize exactly**

- **Note** = the debt (personal promise). **Mortgage** = the security instrument (pledge of the property).
- **Mortgagor** = borrower. **Mortgagee** = lender.
- Florida is **lien theory**: the borrower keeps legal title and the lender holds a lien. In title-theory states the lender, or a trustee under a deed of trust, holds title (manual; not yet verified).
- **Satisfaction: 60 days.** Within 60 days after receiving full payment (including payment of an estoppel-letter payoff amount), the mortgagee, holder or servicer must:
  - sign a written satisfaction or release;
  - have it acknowledged;
  - record it in the county official records;
  - send the recorded satisfaction to the mortgagor or record owner.

  (s. 701.04(2), Fla. Stat.; verified, V-71)
- In a lawsuit to enforce that duty, the **prevailing party** recovers attorney fees and costs (V-71).
- Parallel duty: satisfactions of other **liens and judgments** are also recorded within 60 days after full payment (s. 701.04(3); V-71).
- The clause meanings in section 3: acceleration, due-on-sale, prepayment, defeasance, subordination, release, receivership, exculpatory (manual; not yet verified).

**Understand**

- The note creates **personal** liability and the mortgage creates a claim on the **property**. An exculpatory clause removes the personal liability, so no deficiency can be claimed.
- Acceleration and due-on-sale both make the whole balance due. The trigger differs: **default** for acceleration, **transfer** for due-on-sale.
- Defeasance is the promise that the lien ends; the satisfaction is the recorded proof.
- A **release clause** frees single parcels from a blanket mortgage. The "satisfaction or release" recorded after payoff ends the whole mortgage.

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"The mortgage is the loan."** No. The **note** is the debt. The mortgage pledges the property.
- **"The mortgagor is the bank."** No. The mortgagor is the **borrower**; the lender is the mortgagee.
- **"In Florida the bank owns the house until payoff."** No. Under lien theory the borrower holds title and the bank holds a lien.
- **"The lien disappears by itself after the last payment."** No. The lender must record a satisfaction within **60 days** and send it to the borrower.
- **"Acceleration and due-on-sale are the same."** Both make the whole balance due, but acceleration is triggered by **default** and due-on-sale by **transfer**.
- **"With an exculpatory clause the borrower can walk away and nothing happens."** The lender can still take the **property**. It just cannot claim a deficiency from the borrower.

## 10. Memory sheet

- **Note = debt** (personal promise). **Mortgage = security** (pledge). "Mortgage" means the document, not the loan.
- **"-or gives, -ee receives":** mortgagor = borrower, mortgagee = lender.
- **Florida = lien theory**: the borrower keeps title (manual; not yet verified).
- **Satisfaction within 60 days** of full payment: signed, acknowledged, recorded and sent to the borrower. The prevailing party gets attorney fees (V-71).
- **Clauses:** acceleration (default), due-on-sale (transfer), prepayment (early payoff), defeasance (paid, so the lien ends), subordination (steps back), release (parcels from a blanket mortgage), receivership (rents during foreclosure), exculpatory (no deficiency).
