## 2. Why it matters

Mortgage money is not created at the closing table. It flows from savers and investors, through lenders, to your buyer, and the Federal Reserve turns the tap up or down. This affects your daily work in Florida. When credit tightens, rates rise, fewer buyers qualify and listings sit longer. When a buyer's loan is sold after closing, you can explain why a new company is now collecting the payments. When a loan offer reads "6.50% with 2 points", you can tell the buyer what those points cost in dollars.

For the exam, Section 13 carries about 4% of the questions (V-02). Three question types repeat: **sort the entity** (primary or secondary market?), **name the Fed tool**, and **compute points** (cost in dollars, or how many points a yield change needs).

## 3. Simple explanation

> **Source status.** The market players, the Fed's tools and the definition of discount points come from the study manual (manual; not yet verified). The two point figures, **1 point = 1% of the loan** and **about 1/8% of yield per point**, are listed under N-125 / V-26 as not yet verified, and the 1/8% figure is a **course rule of thumb**, not a law.

### Why there are two markets

A lender has a limited amount of money. If a bank kept every 30-year loan it made, it would soon have nothing left to lend. The fix is to **sell** existing loans, get the cash back and lend it again.

- **Primary market**: where loans are **originated** (made) and the borrower deals with the lender.
- **Secondary market**: where **existing** loans are bought and sold, so that primary lenders get fresh money.

**Consumers borrow in the primary market only.** No buyer ever applies for a mortgage at Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac.

### The primary market (lenders the borrower meets)

| Player | What it does |
|---|---|
| Commercial banks | Lend to borrowers |
| Credit unions | Lend to their members |
| Savings associations | Lend to borrowers |
| **Mortgage bankers** | **Lend** their own or borrowed funds and **often service** loans (collect the payments) |
| **Mortgage brokers** | **Arrange** loans between borrower and lender for a fee; **lend nothing** |

### The secondary market (buyers of existing loans)

| Player | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Fannie Mae** (Federal National Mortgage Association) | **Buys** loans from lenders (conventional, and also government loans) and issues **mortgage-backed securities (MBS)** |
| **Freddie Mac** (Federal Home Loan Mortgage Corporation) | Same role: **buys** loans and issues MBS |
| **Ginnie Mae** (Government National Mortgage Association) | **Guarantees** securities backed by **FHA and VA** loans |

A **mortgage-backed security** is an investment backed by a pool of mortgage loans. Investors buy it, and their money flows back to the lenders, who lend again.

### Intermediation and disintermediation

- **Intermediation**: banks and savings institutions pool savers' deposits and lend them out as mortgages.
- **Disintermediation**: savers **withdraw** their deposits to get better returns elsewhere. The lenders lose the money they would have lent, which **starves mortgage lending**.

### The Federal Reserve: three tools

The **Federal Reserve** (the Fed) is the central bank of the United States. It does **not** set mortgage rates. It changes how easy or tight credit is, and rates follow.

| Tool | What it is | To tighten credit | To loosen credit |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Open-market operations** | Buying and selling government securities. **The day-to-day tool** | The Fed **sells** securities: buyers pay the Fed, so money leaves the banks | The Fed **buys** securities: money flows into the banks |
| **Discount rate** | The rate member banks pay to borrow from the Fed | **Raise** it | **Lower** it |
| **Reserve requirements** | The share of deposits banks must hold instead of lending. **The bluntest tool** | **Raise** them: banks can lend less | **Lower** them |

**Tight money → higher rates → real estate slows.** Easy money works the other way.

### Discount points

**Discount points** are **prepaid interest** paid **to the lender at closing**.

- **1 point = 1% of the loan amount**, never of the price (manual; not yet verified, V-26).
- **Course rule of thumb:** each point raises the lender's **yield** (its real rate of return) by about **1/8%**, which is **0.125%** (manual; not yet verified, V-26).

**Why do points raise the yield?** Suppose a lender charges 2 points on a $315,000 loan. It hands over $315,000 but receives $6,300 back at closing, so it has really advanced only $308,700 while collecting payments on $315,000. Its return is therefore higher than the note rate. Seen from the borrower's side, paying points is how a borrower can get a lower note rate while the lender still earns its target yield.

**Three formulas**

1. **Cost of points** = loan amount × number of points × 1%
2. **Points needed** = yield increase wanted ÷ 0.125%
3. **Approximate yield** = note rate + (number of points × 0.125%)

**Eighths to decimals:** 1/8 = 0.125 · 2/8 = 1/4 = 0.25 · 3/8 = 0.375 · 4/8 = 1/2 = 0.50.

**Try it**

1. A buyer pays $480,000 for a Fort Lauderdale home with 20% down. The lender charges 1.5 points. What do the points cost?

## 5. Practical example

Daniel is buying a $350,000 condo in Orlando with 10% down ($35,000), so his loan is **$315,000**.

1. **Who takes his application?** Daniel works with a **mortgage broker**, who shops several lenders but lends nothing. The loan is funded by a **mortgage banker**, which also **services** it. Both are **primary market**. Because the loan is conventional and the LTV is 90%, he will also pay PMI (L13-1).
2. **The rate offer.** The lender quotes **6.75% with no points** or **6.50% with 2 points**. The cost of the points is $315,000 × 2% = **$6,300**, paid at closing. The offer matches the rule of thumb: 2 points × 0.125% = 0.25%, so 6.50% plus 2 points gives the lender roughly the same yield as 6.75% with no points.
3. **After closing.** The mortgage banker sells Daniel's loan to **Fannie Mae** (secondary market). Fannie Mae pools it with other loans into mortgage-backed securities. The mortgage banker gets its money back and lends it to the next Orlando buyer. The loan's terms were fixed at closing; the sale only moves the loan to a new owner.
4. **Six months later.** The Fed **sells** government securities in the open market. Money leaves the banks, credit tightens and mortgage rates rise. Fewer buyers qualify, and your Orlando listings take longer to sell.

## 7. Legal and numerical requirements

**Memorize exactly**

- **Primary market**: commercial banks, credit unions, savings associations, mortgage bankers (lend; often service), mortgage brokers (arrange; lend nothing) (manual; not yet verified).
- **Secondary market**: Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac **buy** loans and issue MBS; Ginnie Mae **guarantees** securities backed by FHA/VA loans (manual; not yet verified).
- **Consumers borrow in the primary market only.**
- **Disintermediation** = depositors withdraw for better returns → less mortgage money (manual; not yet verified).
- **Fed tools**: open-market operations (**day-to-day tool**), discount rate, reserve requirements (**bluntest**). Tight money → higher rates → slower real estate (manual; not yet verified).
- **Discount points**: prepaid interest paid to the lender at closing; **1 point = 1% of the loan amount**; each point ≈ **+1/8% (0.125%) yield**, a course rule of thumb (manual; not yet verified, V-26).

**Understand**

- The secondary market exists to **refill lenders' money** so they can lend again.
- The Fed changes credit conditions; it does not set mortgage rates or make loans.
- Selling securities, raising the discount rate and raising reserve requirements all **tighten** credit.
- Points are based on the **loan**, so the down payment matters: find the loan first.

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"A first-time buyer applied for a loan at Freddie Mac."** Impossible. Freddie Mac and Fannie Mae are secondary-market buyers of existing loans; consumers borrow in the primary market.
- **"Ginnie Mae buys conventional loans."** No. In this course Ginnie Mae **guarantees** securities backed by **FHA/VA** loans.
- **"A mortgage broker lends money."** No. A broker **arranges** loans and lends nothing; a **mortgage banker** lends.
- **"The Fed sets mortgage rates."** No. It influences credit through its three tools.
- **"Reserve requirements are the Fed's everyday tool."** No. **Open-market operations** are the day-to-day tool; reserve requirements are the bluntest.
- **"When the Fed sells securities, credit gets easier."** No. Selling securities takes money out of the banks and **tightens** credit.
- **"Disintermediation means more money for mortgages."** No. It means depositors pull money out, so there is **less**.
- **"Points are a percentage of the price."** No. **1 point = 1% of the loan.**
- **"1 point = 1/8%."** Do not mix the two numbers. One point **costs** 1% of the loan and **raises yield** by about 1/8%.

- **Secondary** = loans are bought: Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac **buy** + issue MBS; Ginnie Mae **guarantees** FHA/VA-backed MBS.
- **Disintermediation** = deposits leave → mortgage money dries up.
- **Fed**: open-market operations (everyday) · discount rate · reserve requirements (bluntest). Tight money → higher rates → slower sales.
- **1 point = 1% of the LOAN ≈ +1/8% (0.125%) yield** (course rule of thumb; V-26, not yet verified).
- Points needed = yield increase ÷ 0.125%.
