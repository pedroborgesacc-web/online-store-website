## 2. Why it matters

**In practice.** Income tax shapes real estate decisions. An investor asks whether to sell a fourplex outright or exchange it; a couple asks whether they will owe tax on the profit from their home. You are not a tax adviser and should send clients to a CPA or tax attorney for their own figures, but you must understand the vocabulary well enough to see what is at stake and when to refer.

**On the exam.** Section 18 (Taxes Affecting Real Estate) carries 3% of the exam (V-02). Expect quick conceptual questions: 27.5 versus 39 years, land never depreciated, the effect of boot in a 1031 exchange, the $250,000/$500,000 home-sale exclusion with its 2-of-5-years test, and a short adjusted-basis and gain calculation.

## 3. Simple explanation

*Labels: V-nn = checked against official sources on 2026-10-19/20. "(manual; not yet verified)" = taken only from the study manual. These are **US federal** income tax rules (Internal Revenue Code, IRS publications), not Florida statutes. Dollar figures in examples are made up for practice.*

### Building block 1: basis

**Basis** is, in general, what you paid for the property. **Adjusted basis** = cost + capital improvements − depreciation taken (manual; not yet verified). Basis is the yardstick: tax is paid only on the amount by which the sale proceeds exceed it.

- An **improvement** (a new roof, an added bedroom) increases basis, because the owner has put more money into the property.
- **Depreciation** decreases basis, because the owner has already deducted that part of the cost from taxable income.

### Building block 2: depreciation (cost recovery)

A building wears out, so the tax law lets an investor deduct its cost over a set number of years. This deduction is **depreciation**, also called **cost recovery** (V-77):

| Property | Recovery period | Method |
|---|---|---|
| **Residential rental** property (a building where 80% or more of the gross rental income comes from dwelling units) | **27.5 years** | Straight-line |
| **Nonresidential** real property (offices, stores, warehouses) | **39 years** | Straight-line |
| **Land** | **Never** depreciated | — |

**Why never land?** Land does not wear out, become obsolete or get used up. Only the building's cost goes into the depreciable basis, so you must split the purchase price between land and building first.

**Straight-line** means the same deduction every year: building cost ÷ recovery period. An Orlando rental house costs $385,000, of which $110,000 is land: $275,000 ÷ 27.5 = **$10,000 a year**. An office building's structure costs $1,170,000: ÷ 39 = **$30,000 a year**.

Finer points (V-77): these are the general (GDS/MACRS) periods; an alternative system (ADS) uses 30 and 40 years. Real tax returns apply the **mid-month convention**, which adjusts the first and last years; exam math normally uses full years. The 39-year period replaced **31.5 years** in 1993, so 31.5 is **outdated**. Depreciation is a tax deduction, not an operating expense, so it does not reduce NOI (L17-1; manual; not yet verified).

**Why investors like it:** depreciation costs no cash in the year it is deducted, yet it reduces taxable income. It shelters part of the cash flow from tax. The price comes later, at the sale.

### Building block 3: capital gain and recapture

**Capital gain** = amount realized − adjusted basis (manual; not yet verified). On the exam, the amount realized is the net sale price the question gives.

Since depreciation **lowers** the basis, it **raises** the gain. The depreciation taken over the years is **recaptured** (taxed) at the sale (manual; not yet verified; the recapture tax rate is not covered in this course).

Continue the Orlando example. After 5 years the owner has taken 5 × $10,000 = $50,000 of depreciation and made no improvements. Adjusted basis: $385,000 − $50,000 = $335,000. She sells for a net $450,000. Gain: $450,000 − $335,000 = **$115,000**. Without the depreciation it would have been $65,000. The extra $50,000 is the depreciation coming back.

### Building block 4: three ways the tax is reduced, postponed or spread

| Tool | For what property | Effect on the tax |
|---|---|---|
| **Principal residence exclusion** (26 U.S.C. 121; V-78) | The seller's main home | Gain up to the limit is **excluded** (not taxed) |
| **1031 like-kind exchange** (manual; not yet verified) | Investment or business real property | Tax is **deferred** (postponed), except on boot |
| **Installment sale** (manual; not yet verified) | A sale where the buyer pays over time | Tax is **spread** over the years payments arrive |

**Principal residence exclusion (V-78).** A seller can exclude up to **$250,000** of gain, or **$500,000** for married filing jointly, if they **owned and used** the home as their principal residence for **at least 2 of the 5 years** before the sale. That means 24 full months or 730 days, and the periods need not be continuous. Qualifications:

1. Generally not available if the seller excluded gain on another home sold within the **2 years** before this sale.
2. For the $500,000 joint amount: at least one spouse meets the ownership test, and both spouses meet the use test and the 2-year look-back rule. Otherwise each spouse is tested separately.
3. A **partial** exclusion may apply if the tests are failed for qualifying reasons (work, health, unforeseen circumstances).
4. Gain equal to depreciation claimed or allowable after May 6, 1997 cannot be excluded (for example, depreciation taken while part of the home was rented out).
5. A surviving spouse may use $500,000 if the sale is within 2 years of the spouse's death and other conditions are met.

Verification note: this rests on IRS Publication 523 (2025) and Tax Topic 701; the statute text and any 2026 change were not checked directly (V-78).

**1031 like-kind exchange (manual; not yet verified).** An investor who exchanges **investment or business real property** for **like-kind** property defers the tax on the gain. A family home is not investment property, so it uses the exclusion instead, never a 1031. **Boot** is any value received that is not like-kind: **cash, debt relief, personal property**. Boot is **taxable to the extent of gain**, but it does **not** disqualify the exchange. The like-kind part stays tax-deferred. Further technical requirements exist that this course does not cover.

**Installment sale (manual; not yet verified).** When the seller receives the price over several years (for example, with seller financing), the seller recognizes the gain proportionally as payments are received, which spreads the tax over those years.

## 4. Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Basis | In general, the cost of the property |
| Adjusted basis | Cost + improvements − depreciation |
| Amount realized | What the seller receives from the sale (on the exam, the net sale price given) |
| Capital gain | Amount realized − adjusted basis |
| Depreciation (cost recovery) | Yearly deduction of a building's cost: 27.5 years residential rental, 39 years nonresidential, never land |
| Straight-line | Equal deduction every year |
| Recapture | Taxing, at the sale, the depreciation taken earlier |
| 1031 exchange | Tax-deferred exchange of like-kind investment or business real property |
| Like-kind | In this course: investment or business real property exchanged for like-kind investment or business real property (manual; not yet verified) |
| Boot | Non-like-kind value received in an exchange (cash, debt relief, personal property); taxable to the extent of gain |
| Principal residence exclusion | Up to $250,000 single / $500,000 married filing jointly of home-sale gain is not taxed; 2-of-5-years test |
| Installment sale | Sale paid over time; gain reported as payments are received |

## 5. Practical example

**Rita, the investor.** Rita bought a fourplex in St. Petersburg for **$660,000**: $165,000 land and $495,000 building. Yearly depreciation: $495,000 ÷ 27.5 = **$18,000** (four dwelling units, so residential rental). She later put on a new roof for **$42,000**. Her records show total depreciation taken of **$115,000** (building and roof).

| Step | Calculation | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Adjusted basis | $660,000 + $42,000 − $115,000 | **$587,000** |
| Amount realized (net sale price) | given | $890,000 |
| Gain | $890,000 − $587,000 | **$303,000** |

Her three options, in concept:

- **Sell outright.** The $303,000 gain is taxable; the part that comes from the $115,000 of depreciation is recaptured (manual; not yet verified).
- **1031 exchange** into a 10-unit apartment building in Tampa, another investment property. She also receives **$30,000 cash**. The $30,000 is boot and taxable, since it is less than her gain. The rest of the gain stays deferred, and the exchange is still valid. The new building (not its land) is again residential rental property, depreciated over 27.5 years.
- **Installment sale.** She finances the buyer and receives the price over several years. The gain is taxed as the payments come in.

She asks you which is best. You explain the concepts and refer her to her CPA.

**Paulo and Joana, the homeowners.** A married couple filing jointly bought their Jacksonville home 4 years ago for **$310,000** and lived in it the whole time. They sell for a net **$720,000**: gain **$410,000** (no improvements).

- Both owned it and lived in it at least 2 of the last 5 years, and neither used the exclusion on another home in the previous 2 years: up to **$500,000** can be excluded, so the whole **$410,000** is excluded.
- If Paulo were single, the limit would be **$250,000**: $410,000 − $250,000 = **$160,000** would be taxable.
- A 1031 exchange is not available for this home: it is their principal residence, not investment property.

## 7. Legal and numerical requirements

**Memorize exactly**

- Depreciation: **27.5 years** residential rental (80%+ of gross rents from dwelling units); **39 years** nonresidential; straight-line; **land never** (26 U.S.C. 168; IRS Pub. 946; V-77). ADS: 30/40 years. 31.5 years = outdated (replaced in 1993).
- Exclusion: **$250,000** single / **$500,000** married filing jointly; owned and used as the principal residence **2 of the 5 years** before the sale (24 months or 730 days; need not be continuous); generally not if used on another home sold within the previous **2 years** (26 U.S.C. 121; IRS Pub. 523; V-78).
- Joint $500,000: one spouse meets ownership; both meet use and look-back. Partial exclusion for work, health or unforeseen circumstances. Depreciation after May 6, 1997 cannot be excluded. Surviving spouse: $500,000 if sold within 2 years of the death and other conditions are met (V-78).
- Adjusted basis = cost + improvements − depreciation. Gain = amount realized − adjusted basis. Depreciation is recaptured at sale (manual; not yet verified).
- 1031: investment or business real property, like-kind; tax **deferred**; boot (cash, debt relief, personal property) taxable to the extent of gain; boot does not disqualify (manual; not yet verified).
- Installment sale: gain recognized as payments are received (manual; not yet verified).

**Understand**

- Depreciation shelters income now but raises the gain later.
- Exclusion = gain not taxed (up to the limit); 1031 = tax postponed; installment sale = tax spread.
- The home takes the exclusion; the investment property takes the 1031. Do not cross them.

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"Land is depreciated with the building."** No. Land is never depreciated (V-77).
- **"An apartment building is depreciated over 39 years."** No. Residential rental is 27.5 years; 39 is nonresidential.
- **"Nonresidential is 31.5 years."** Outdated since 1993: 39 years.
- **"Depreciation lowers the gain at sale."** No. It lowers the basis, so the gain is larger.
- **"Receiving cash kills the 1031."** No. The cash is boot and is taxed; the exchange survives.
- **"A 1031 makes the gain tax-free."** No. It defers the tax.
- **"I can 1031 my family home."** No. The home uses the $250,000/$500,000 exclusion.
- **"The 2 years must be the last 2 years in a row."** No. 2 of the 5 years before the sale; need not be continuous.
- **"Every married couple gets $500,000."** Only if they file jointly and meet the tests: at least one spouse meets ownership, both meet use and the 2-year look-back rule.
- **"An installment sale avoids the tax."** No. It spreads it.
