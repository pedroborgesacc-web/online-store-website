## 2. Why it matters

At every closing, some bills cover a period that the seller and the buyer **share**. Property taxes cover the calendar year, rent covers the month, and association dues may cover the whole year. **Prorating** makes each party pay only for its own days. In practice, prorations decide a few thousand dollars on most closing statements, and buyers and sellers will ask you to explain them.

On the exam, proration questions test three skills: counting days correctly, applying the course convention for the closing day, and deciding **who is debited and who is credited**. That last decision is also asked on its own, without any arithmetic. These lines then reappear on the closing statement (L14-8).

## 3. Simple explanation

### Step 1: What it represents

A **proration** divides an expense or an income between seller and buyer as of the closing date. The question behind every proration is: *someone has paid, or will pay, a bill that covers both owners' days. How much does the other owner owe them?*

- **Paid in arrears** means the bill is paid at the **end** of the period it covers. Florida property taxes and mortgage interest work this way. At closing the bill is usually **unpaid**, so the seller owes the buyer for the seller's days.
- **Paid in advance** means the bill is paid at the **start** of the period. Rent, insurance premiums and association dues work this way. At closing someone has already paid for days that belong to the other party.

Why are property taxes in arrears? Florida assesses property as of **January 1**, and the tax bill for that year is **due November 1** (V-76). For most of the year, the current year's taxes are unpaid. The buyer, who owns the property in November, will pay the whole year's bill. So the seller must give the buyer the seller's share at closing.

### Step 2: Units

- **Days.** The **365-day method** uses the actual days in each month: Jan 31, Feb 28 (non-leap year), Mar 31, Apr 30, May 31, Jun 30, Jul 31, Aug 31, Sep 30, Oct 31, Nov 30, Dec 31.
- **Daily rate** for an annual item = annual amount ÷ 365. For a monthly item (rent, one month's interest), daily rate = that month's amount ÷ the actual days in **that** month.
- **Money:** dollars, rounded to the cent only at the **end**. Keep the daily rate unrounded.

### Step 3: Formula

| Quantity | Formula |
|---|---|
| Daily rate (annual item) | Annual amount ÷ 365 |
| Daily rate (monthly item) | Monthly amount ÷ days in that month |
| Seller's days | Jan 1 (or the 1st of the month) **through the day before closing** |
| Buyer's days | **Closing day** through Dec 31 (or the end of the month) |
| Seller's share | Daily rate × seller's days |
| Buyer's share | Daily rate × buyer's days, or total − seller's share |

The **course convention** (V-79, unresolved): a 365-day year and **the buyer owns the day of closing**, unless the question says otherwise. No statute or rule sets these. They come from the contract and the course. The manual says the FR/BAR contract (the Florida Realtors/Florida Bar residential sale contract, a privately published form, not a statute or rule) prorates through the day before closing. That form could not be checked against an official source, so this statement stays flagged (V-79, unresolved).

### Step 4: When it applies

Decide the **direction** first by asking two questions: *Has the bill been paid yet? Who paid it?*

| Item | Paid | Typical status at closing | Days to compute | Entry |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Property taxes, current year | In arrears | Unpaid | Seller's days | **Debit seller, credit buyer** |
| Property taxes already paid by the seller (late-year closing) | Already paid | Seller paid the full year | Buyer's days | Debit buyer, credit seller |
| Interest on a mortgage the buyer assumes | In arrears | Current month unpaid | Seller's days | **Debit seller, credit buyer** |
| Rent the seller collected | In advance | Seller holds the buyer's days | Buyer's days | **Credit buyer, debit seller** |
| Prepaid insurance or association dues | In advance | Seller paid beyond closing | Buyer's days | **Debit buyer, credit seller** |

The rule behind every row: **the party who is out of pocket is credited, and the other party is debited by the same amount.** A party is out of pocket when it paid (or will pay) an expense that covers the other party's days, or when the other party collected income that belongs to its days.

**Other methods.** The FREC syllabus expects both the **360-day** and the **365-day** methods (V-79). In a 360-day method every month counts as 30 days and the daily rate = annual ÷ 360. Example: taxes of $3,600, closing March 10. 365-day: 3,600 ÷ 365 × 68 days (31 + 28 + 9) = **$670.68**. 360-day: $10 × 69 days (30 + 30 + 9) = **$690**. Always use the method the question states. If it states none, use the course convention.

### Step 5: Worked example

**A. Unpaid taxes (manual example).** Closing May 20; annual taxes $5,475, unpaid.
Daily rate = 5,475 ÷ 365 = **$15**. Seller's days = Jan 31 + Feb 28 + Mar 31 + Apr 30 + May 19 = **139**. Seller's share = 139 × 15 = **$2,085** → **debit seller, credit buyer**.

**B. Rent collected in advance.** The seller collected June rent of $2,100 on June 1; closing is June 21.
Daily rate = 2,100 ÷ 30 = $70. Buyer's days = June 21–30 = **10**. Buyer's share = 10 × 70 = **$700** → **credit buyer, debit seller**.

**C. Interest on an assumed loan.** The buyer assumes a $252,000 loan at 5%; interest is paid in arrears. Closing is September 19.
September interest = 252,000 × 0.05 ÷ 12 = $1,050. Daily = 1,050 ÷ 30 (September's days) = $35. Seller's days = Sept 1–18 = **18**. Seller's share = 18 × 35 = **$630** → **debit seller, credit buyer**.

### Step 6: Reasonableness check

- **Seller's days + buyer's days = 365** (example A: 139 + 226 = 365 ✓). For a month: they add up to the days in that month (B: 20 + 10 = 30 ✓).
- **Seller's share + buyer's share = the total bill** (A: 2,085 + 3,390 = 5,475 ✓).
- A closing near mid-year gives about half the bill to each party. A May 20 closing gives the seller a bit under 40%, and $2,085 is about 38% of $5,475 ✓.
- Direction check: for unpaid taxes, the buyer will pay the whole bill, so the buyer must be **credited**.

### Step 7: Common errors

| Error | Effect | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Giving the closing day to the seller | Seller's days one too many | Seller: Jan 1 to the day **before** closing |
| Using 30-day months when the question says 365 | Wrong day count | Actual days per month |
| Reversing the entry for unpaid taxes | Buyer debited | Arrears → debit seller, credit buyer |
| Computing the buyer's share when asked for the seller's | Wrong figure | Read who the question asks about |
| Rounding the daily rate too early | Off by cents | Round only the final answer |
| Monthly item divided by 365 | Wrong daily rate | Month's amount ÷ days in that month |

Rounding example: May rent $1,850, closing May 25, buyer's days 7. Correct: 1,850 ÷ 31 × 7 = **$417.74**. Rounding the daily rate to $59.68 first gives $417.76.

### Step 10: Exam application

1. Identify the item: **arrears** (taxes, mortgage interest) or **advance** (rent, insurance, dues).
2. Ask: *paid or unpaid? by whom?* Then choose the direction from the Step 4 table.
3. Use the method the question states. If it states none: 365 days, buyer owns the closing day.
4. Count the days covered by the amount that changes hands: unpaid taxes or interest → seller's days; prepaid dues or insurance → buyer's days; rent the seller collected → buyer's days.
5. Multiply, round at the end, and check that the days and shares add up.

A question may ask only for the entry, for example "How do unpaid taxes appear?" The answer is **debit seller, credit buyer**.

## 4. Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Proration | Dividing an expense or income between buyer and seller as of closing |
| In arrears | Paid at the end of the period (Florida property taxes, mortgage interest) |
| In advance | Paid at the start of the period (rent, insurance premiums, association dues) |
| Accrued item | An expense that has built up but is not yet paid, such as unpaid taxes |
| Prepaid item | An expense paid ahead for days after closing |
| Daily rate | Annual amount ÷ 365, or monthly amount ÷ days in that month |
| 365-day method | Actual days in each month; course convention (V-79) |
| 360-day method | Twelve 30-day months; use only when the question says so |
| Debit | An amount a party owes at closing |
| Credit | An amount a party receives at closing |
| Double entry | The same amount debited to one party and credited to the other |

## 5. Practical example

**An investor buys a tenant-occupied house in Orlando.** Closing is **August 12** (non-leap year); the buyer owns the day of closing (course convention, V-79).

| Item | Facts | Work | Amount | Entry |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Property taxes | $6,205 for the year, unpaid | 6,205 ÷ 365 = $17/day × 223 seller's days (Jan 1 – Aug 11) | **$3,791** | Debit seller, credit buyer |
| August rent | $2,170 collected by the seller on Aug 1 | 2,170 ÷ 31 = $70/day × 20 buyer's days (Aug 12–31) | **$1,400** | Credit buyer, debit seller |
| Association dues | $2,555 for the year, prepaid by the seller in January | 2,555 ÷ 365 = $7/day × 142 buyer's days (Aug 12 – Dec 31) | **$994** | Debit buyer, credit seller |

Net effect for the buyer: credits $3,791 + $1,400 = $5,191; debit $994; net **$4,197** in the buyer's favor. The seller has the mirror image. Each proration is a double entry, so what one party gains, the other loses.

## 6. Portugal comparison

**Familiar principle:** sharing a year's charges fairly between seller and buyer is common sense in any market.

**Florida rule:** the course convention is 365 days with the buyer owning the closing day (V-79: contract and course practice, not law). Property taxes are paid in arrears, so unpaid taxes are a seller debit and buyer credit.

**Difference:** this course has no verified comparison entry for how Portuguese practice divides annual property taxes or condominium charges between the parties. Any such Portuguese rule **requires verification** and is not taught here.

**Exam implication:** answer only with the Florida method the question gives, or the course convention.

## 7. Legal and numerical requirements

**Memorize exactly**

- **365-day method:** actual days in each month; daily rate = annual ÷ 365.
- **The buyer owns the day of closing.** Seller's days = Jan 1 through the day before closing.
- **Flag (V-79, unresolved):** no statute or rule sets either convention. They come from the contract and the course. Follow the method the question gives; otherwise use the course convention. The FREC syllabus expects both the 360- and 365-day methods. The manual says the FR/BAR contract (a private form) prorates through the day before closing (manual statement; V-79 unresolved).
- **Arrears** (property taxes, mortgage interest), unpaid: **debit seller, credit buyer** for the seller's days.
- **Advance:** rent the seller collected → **credit buyer, debit seller** for the unused days. Prepaid insurance or association dues → **debit buyer, credit seller** for the days after closing.
- Property taxes: assessed as of January 1; due November 1 (V-76).

**Understand**

- The party who is out of pocket (it paid an expense covering the other's days, or the other collected income belonging to its days) is credited, and the other party is debited by the same amount.
- No verification entry covers the debit/credit directions. They follow from who has paid, as the manual and the course's practice questions teach.
- If a question says the seller has *already paid* the taxes, the direction reverses: debit buyer, credit seller for the buyer's days.

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"The seller owns the closing day."** Under the course convention, the buyer does, so the seller's days stop the day before closing (V-79).
- **"The 365-day method is Florida law."** It is a contract and course convention. Follow the question's method if it gives one.
- **"Unpaid taxes are the buyer's debit because the buyer pays them later."** No. Because the buyer will pay the whole bill, the buyer is **credited** for the seller's share.
- **"Rent collected in advance is a credit to the seller."** The seller holds the buyer's money, so the entry is **credit buyer, debit seller**.
- **"Prorations are single entries."** Prorations between buyer and seller are **double entries**.
