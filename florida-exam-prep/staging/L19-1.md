## 2. Why it matters

**In practice.** Zoning decides what a client can do with a property: build on a narrow lot, run a home daycare, keep a small shop open in a residential street. Permits and the certificate of occupancy show whether a building was built legally and may be used. You are not a land-use lawyer, but you must recognise these issues and send the client to the local planning or building department, or to an attorney when rights are at stake.

**On the exam.** Section 19 of the course outline, "Planning and Zoning", carries **1%** of the exam (V-02), about 1 of the 100 questions (V-01). This lesson covers the land-use half of that section; L19-2 covers environmental rules. The question is almost always a classification: police power or eminent domain? Variance or special exception? Building permit or certificate of occupancy? The manual calls variance versus special exception "a guaranteed question".

## 3. Simple explanation

**Source status.** No verification entry covers the land-use rules in this lesson. They come from the study manual and are marked *(manual; not yet verified)*. Only the exam weight is verified (V-01, V-02). Each Florida county and city adopts its own plan and codes, so the details differ from place to place; the exam tests the general concepts below.

### Foundation: regulating land versus taking it

Owning land never means unlimited freedom (L08-1). Government holds powers over all private property. You met taxation, eminent domain and escheat in L08-1 and L09-1. Land-use control adds a fourth: **police power**.

| Government power | What it does | Is the owner paid? |
|---|---|---|
| **Police power** | **Regulates** use to protect public health, safety and welfare: zoning, building codes, permits | **No** compensation |
| **Eminent domain** | **Takes** private property for public use; the legal process is called **condemnation** | **Yes: just compensation** |
| Taxation | Levies property taxes (Section 18) | — |
| Escheat | Property passes to the state when an owner dies with no will and no heirs (L09-1) | — |

*(manual; not yet verified)*

**Why is one paid and the other not?** Regulation places similar limits on many owners for the benefit of everyone, including those same owners: your home is safer and more pleasant when the neighbour cannot build a factory. The owner keeps the land. A taking is different: one owner gives up property so the public can have a road or a school, so the public pays that owner. "Police" here has nothing to do with the police force. It means the government's general power to make rules for public health, safety and welfare.

**Inverse condemnation.** In ordinary condemnation the government starts the case. When a regulation goes so far that it **destroys all the property's value**, the owner may sue the government, claiming that the regulation amounts to a **taking** that must be paid for. It is "inverse" (reversed) because the **owner** starts the lawsuit *(manual; not yet verified)*. This course cites no court decisions on exactly when a regulation becomes a taking. For the exam, know the label and the trigger.

### The comprehensive plan and zoning

- **Comprehensive plan.** Every Florida local government (county or city) maintains a comprehensive plan: its **long-range land-use plan** for the community *(manual; not yet verified)*.
- **Zoning.** Zoning **implements** the plan. A local law (an **ordinance**) divides land into **districts**: **residential, commercial, industrial, agricultural and special districts**. Each district states the uses allowed and dimensional rules, such as **setback** (the minimum distance between a building and a lot line or the street) and **height** *(manual; not yet verified)*.
- **Buffer zones** ease the transition between very different districts, for example an area of offices or a park between a shopping centre and single-family homes *(manual; not yet verified)*.

Why both? The plan states the goals; zoning turns them into rules for each parcel. Think of the plan as the strategy and zoning as the rule book.

Zoning is **public** law made by a local government. A **deed restriction** is a **private** limit recorded in a deed or plat (L09-4). Do not mix them up.

### When a rule does not fit: four tools

| Tool | What it is | Does the district change? | Florida example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Variance** | Permission to **deviate from a dimensional rule** (setback, height) because of a **hardship unique to the parcel** | No; the use stays the same | A lot is unusually narrow, so a house cannot meet the side setback; the owner asks to build 3 feet closer to the line |
| **Special exception** (also called **conditional use**) | A use the code **already permits** in the district **with case-by-case approval** | No | A school, church or daycare in a residential district |
| **Nonconforming use** | A use that was **lawful before the code changed** ("grandfathered") | No; the use simply no longer fits the current code | A corner store that opened before the area was rezoned residential |
| **Rezoning** | **Changing the district itself** | **Yes** | Farmland changed to a residential district for a new subdivision |

*(manual; not yet verified)*

**Why each tool exists**

- **Variance.** Dimensional rules are written for typical lots; strict application can be unfair to an unusually narrow or oddly shaped one. Hence the hardship must be **unique to the parcel**.
- **Special exception.** Some uses serve a neighbourhood but fit only in the right place, so the code lists them and officials approve each case.
- **Nonconforming use.** An owner who followed the old rules is not forced out. The use **may continue**, but the code tolerates rather than encourages it: it **usually may not expand**, and if **destroyed or abandoned**, it **may not resume** *(manual; not yet verified)*.
- **Rezoning.** When the wanted use is not allowed in the district at all, the district itself must change.

**The trap the manual flags:** **hardship + dimensions → variance; permitted-with-approval use → special exception. Neither is a rezoning** *(manual; not yet verified)*.

**PUD (planned unit development).** A development planned as one unit that **mixes uses** (for example homes, shops and offices) and **clusters density** (buildings placed closer together) **in exchange for open space** such as parks or preserved land *(manual; not yet verified)*.

### Construction control: permit, inspections, certificate of occupancy

1. **Building permit:** needed **before construction**.
2. **Inspections** during the work check it against the building codes.
3. **Certificate of occupancy (CO):** issued **after final inspection**. The building may not be **used or occupied** before it is issued.

*(manual; not yet verified)* Florida contractors are licensed by the **CILB** (Construction Industry Licensing Board), not by the FREC *(manual; not yet verified)*.

## 5. Practical example

*Names, dates and distances below are invented for practice. Real setbacks and permitted uses come from each local code.*

Ana, a sales associate in Orlando, works with Mr. and Mrs. Silva, who want a home with space for Mrs. Silva's home daycare. Over two weeks Ana meets six land-use questions. Each time she names the issue, sends the clients to the city or county planning or building department for the facts, and suggests an attorney where rights are at stake.

| What happens | What it is | What follows |
|---|---|---|
| A vacant lot they like is unusually narrow. The house they want cannot meet the side setback; they would need to build 3 feet (about 0.9 m) closer to the line. | **Variance**: dimensional rule + hardship unique to the parcel | They may apply for a variance. The residential use does not change. |
| The city's code lists daycare as allowed in that residential district with case-by-case approval. | **Special exception (conditional use)** | Mrs. Silva applies for approval. No rezoning is needed. |
| A small grocery on the corner opened before the area was rezoned residential. Its owner wants to double its size. | **Nonconforming use** | It may keep operating, but expansion is usually not allowed. If it burns down or is abandoned, it may not reopen. |
| A developer asks the county to change nearby farmland to a residential district. | **Rezoning** | The district itself changes. |
| The county widens the road and takes a 10-foot (about 3.05 m) strip from the front of another listing. The same month, the city adopts a lower height limit for the whole street. | Road strip: **eminent domain**. Height limit: **police power** | The strip: just compensation. The height limit: no compensation. |
| The house they finally choose has a family room added by the seller. | **Building permit and final inspection** | Ana suggests checking the permit history with the building department during the inspection period. |

## 7. Legal and numerical requirements

**Memorize exactly**

- Police power = **regulate**, **no compensation**. Eminent domain = **take** for public use through **condemnation**, with **just compensation** (manual; not yet verified).
- Inverse condemnation = the **owner** sues, claiming a regulation is a taking, as when it destroys all value (manual; not yet verified).
- Variance = **dimensional** rule (setback, height) + **hardship unique to the parcel** (manual; not yet verified).
- Special exception = conditional use = a use **permitted with case-by-case approval** (school, church, daycare) (manual; not yet verified).
- Nonconforming use = lawful **before** the code changed; **may continue**; usually **no expansion**; **destroyed or abandoned → may not resume** (manual; not yet verified).
- Rezoning = the **district** changes. Variance and special exception are **not** rezonings (manual; not yet verified).
- PUD = mixed uses + clustered density **traded for open space** (manual; not yet verified).
- Building permit **before** construction → inspections → certificate of occupancy **after final inspection, before use** (manual; not yet verified).
- Contractors are licensed by the **CILB** (manual; not yet verified).
- Section 19 = **1%** of the exam (V-02).

**Understand**

- Why regulation is unpaid and a taking is paid.
- The comprehensive plan comes first; zoning implements it; zoning districts and buffer zones.
- A licensee spots land-use issues and refers clients to the planning or building department or an attorney, rather than guessing what a code allows.

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"If zoning lowers my property's value, the county must pay me."** No. Zoning is police power; no compensation is owed. Only when a regulation destroys all value may the owner sue for inverse condemnation (manual; not yet verified).
- **"Eminent domain is voluntary because the owner is paid."** No. It is an involuntary transfer; just compensation is not consent (L09-1).
- **"A variance lets me use my lot for a different purpose."** No. A variance relaxes a dimensional rule such as a setback or height. A different use needs a special exception (if the code lists it) or a rezoning.
- **"A special exception is just a variance for uses."** The tests differ. A special exception is a use the code already lists for case-by-case approval; a variance rests on a hardship unique to the parcel.
- **"Grandfathered means protected forever."** A nonconforming use usually cannot expand, and if it is destroyed or abandoned it may not resume.
- **"The building permit lets the owner move in."** The permit allows construction. Occupancy needs the certificate of occupancy after final inspection.
- **"Deed restrictions are part of zoning."** Deed restrictions are private and recorded (L09-4). Zoning is local government police power.
