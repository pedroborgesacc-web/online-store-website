## 2. Why it matters

In Portugal you already work with promissory contracts, deposits and deeds. In Florida the same everyday questions have their own answers: Is this agreement a binding contract yet? Can one side walk away? Would a court enforce it? A buyer and a seller can both believe they "have a deal" when, legally, they have nothing a court will enforce. Knowing the elements of a contract and the writing requirement tells you when a deal is real.

On the exam, Section 11 "Real Estate Contracts" carries 12% of the questions (V-02), about 12 of the 100 (V-01), tied for the largest section. This lesson is the foundation for offers (L11-2), listings (L11-3) and the Florida forms (L11-4). Typical questions:

- Which is NOT an essential element of a valid contract? (Earnest money is the usual trap.)
- Is a contract signed by a minor void or voidable?
- How long does a party have to sue on a written contract in Florida? On an oral one?
- Is a handshake agreement to sell a house enforceable?

## 3. Simple explanation

**Source status.** The contract-law principles here come from the study manual and are marked *(manual; not yet verified)*. The statute of frauds is on the list of manual statements still awaiting verification (V-26). The statute of limitations is verified (V-70).

### Step 1: The four essential elements (C.O.L.C.)

A **contract** is an agreement the law will enforce. It is valid only when four elements are present. The manual's mnemonic is **C.O.L.C.** *(manual; not yet verified)*:

| | Element | Meaning | Real estate illustration |
|---|---|---|---|
| **C** | Competent parties | Each party has the legal capacity to contract | An adult owner selling her house. A minor lacks full capacity (Step 3) |
| **O** | Offer and acceptance | **Mutual assent**, a "meeting of the minds" on the same terms | The buyer's signed offer, accepted without changes |
| **L** | Legal purpose | What the contract requires must be lawful | Selling a house is lawful; an agreement to do something illegal is not |
| **C** | Consideration | Something of value given in exchange. **Mutual promises are enough** | The buyer promises to pay; the seller promises to deliver the deed |

**Why earnest money is not on the list.** Consideration only requires that each side gives something of value, and a binding promise is itself of value: the buyer's promise to pay is consideration for the seller's promise to convey. **Earnest money** (the buyer's good-faith deposit) is customary because it shows commitment and gives the seller security, but it is **not legally required** *(manual; not yet verified)*.

### Step 2: Classifying a contract (three pairs)

Each contract takes one label from each pair *(manual; not yet verified)*:

| Question | First label | Second label |
|---|---|---|
| Who has promised? | **Bilateral**: a promise for a promise (a sale contract) | **Unilateral**: a promise for an act (an option; a reward) |
| Is anything left to do? | **Executory**: obligations remain (a signed sale contract before closing) | **Executed**: fully performed (the same contract after closing) |
| How was it formed? | **Express**: terms stated in words, oral or written | **Implied**: agreement shown by the parties' conduct |

A signed sale contract that has not yet closed is **bilateral, executory and express** at the same time.

### Step 3: When something is wrong: void, voidable, unenforceable

*(manual; not yet verified)*

- **Void**: there was never a contract; nobody is bound. Causes: an **illegal purpose**, or **no genuine assent** (no real agreement at all).
- **Voidable**: a contract exists and binds both parties **until** the protected party chooses to cancel. Causes: a **minor**, or consent obtained by **fraud**, **duress** (threats or pressure) or **misrepresentation**. A minor's contract is voidable **by the minor**; the adult cannot use the minor's age to escape.
- **Unenforceable**: valid in substance, but the courts will not enforce it. Causes: a **required writing is missing** (Step 4), or the **time to sue has expired** (Step 5).

Why three categories? A void agreement either has an unlawful purpose or was never truly agreed, so nobody can rely on it. A voidable contract harms only one person, so the law lets that person decide. An unenforceable contract fails on form or timing, not substance.

### Step 4: The statute of frauds: which contracts must be written

A **statute** is a law passed by the legislature. The **statute of frauds** is meant to prevent false claims about important agreements that were never written down. It requires that *(manual; not yet verified, V-26)*:

- a contract for the **sale of real property**, and
- any contract that **cannot be performed within one year**

must be **in writing and signed by the party to be charged**, meaning the person against whom enforcement is sought. If the seller backs out, the buyer needs a writing signed by the seller.

**Exception:** a **lease of one year or less may be oral** *(manual; not yet verified, V-26)*. Leases need no witnesses, while deeds need two (V-05, verified); V-05 also notes that a lease over one year must still be in writing.

A payment does not replace the signed writing: a buyer's check shows that money changed hands, but it is not a writing signed by the seller *(manual; not yet verified)*.

### Step 5: The statute of limitations: how long you have to sue (verified, V-70)

"F.S." means Florida Statutes.

| Contract | Deadline | Statute | Qualifications |
|---|---|---|---|
| Founded on a **written instrument** | **5 years** | s. 95.11(2)(b), F.S. | Legal and equitable actions. Claims on payment bonds and deficiency judgments follow their own provisions |
| **Not** founded on a written instrument (oral) | **4 years** | s. 95.11(3), F.S. | Cite subsection (3) only: its paragraph letters changed after 2023 amendments |

After the period runs, the contract is **unenforceable** (time-barred). Memory aid: **written lasts longer, 5 > 4**.

Do not mix the two statutes. **Frauds** asks "Does it need to be written?" **Limitations** asks "Is it too late to sue?"

## 4. Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Consideration | Something of value exchanged; mutual promises suffice |
| Mutual assent | Offer and acceptance on the same terms; a "meeting of the minds" |
| Bilateral / unilateral | Promise for a promise / promise for an act |
| Executory / executed | Obligations still to perform / fully performed |
| Express / implied | Terms stated in words / agreement shown by conduct |
| Void | No contract at all (illegal purpose, no genuine assent) |
| Voidable | Binding until the protected party cancels (minor, fraud, duress, misrepresentation) |
| Unenforceable | Valid, but courts won't enforce it (missing writing, time-barred) |
| Statute of frauds | Law requiring certain contracts (sale of real estate; more than one year to perform) to be written and signed |
| Party to be charged | The party against whom enforcement is sought |
| Statute of limitations | Deadline to sue: Florida written contract 5 years, oral 4 years |

## 5. Practical example

**A house in Tampa.** Sofia, a sales associate, is helping Mr. Okafor buy Ms. Reyes's house.

| Step | What happens | Legal analysis |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | At an open house Ms. Reyes says, "$410,000 and it's yours." Mr. Okafor says "Deal" and they shake hands in front of Sofia and two visitors. Next day Ms. Reyes gets a higher offer and backs out. | Not enforceable. A sale of real property needs a writing signed by Ms. Reyes, the party to be charged; a handshake in front of witnesses is not one *(manual; not yet verified, V-26)*. |
| 2 | Mr. Okafor makes a written offer of $415,000. Ms. Reyes signs it without changes, and it is delivered back to him. No earnest money has been paid yet. | A valid contract: all four C.O.L.C. elements, with the mutual promises as consideration. Before closing it is bilateral, executory and express *(manual; not yet verified)*. |
| 3 | Variation: Ms. Reyes had told him the roof was new, knowing it was not. | Consent obtained by misrepresentation: the contract is **voidable by Mr. Okafor**, not by her *(manual; not yet verified)*. |
| 4 | Variation: Ms. Reyes refuses to close. | The contract is written, so Mr. Okafor must sue within **5 years** (s. 95.11(2)(b), V-70). |
| 5 | Ms. Reyes rents her garage apartment to a student on an oral 6-month lease. | May be oral: a lease of one year or less *(manual; not yet verified, V-26)*. |

## 6. Portugal comparison

**The familiar principle.** In Portugal a deposit paid on a contrato-promessa is presumed to be **sinal**: if the payer defaults, the other party keeps it; if the recipient defaults, the payer can demand double (Código Civil art. 442; PT-07). The final sale is formalized by escritura pública or documento particular autenticado and registered (PT-08; details require verification). So the deposit and the formal deed feel like the moments that "make it real".

**The Florida rule.** A contract exists when the C.O.L.C. elements are present; mutual promises are the consideration and earnest money is not an essential element *(manual; not yet verified)*. A contract to sell real property is enforceable only if there is a writing signed by the party to be charged *(manual; not yet verified, V-26)*. The signed sale contract is itself the binding purchase agreement; there is no second "promise" document (PT-08).

**The precise difference.** In Florida the deposit does not create the contract, and Florida exam content has no automatic "return double" rule (PT-07); the signed writing is what makes a land contract enforceable. Portuguese limitation periods and rules on minors and defective consent are not in the course sources (requires verification).

**Exam implication.** "Which is NOT an essential element?" → earnest money. Limitation periods: 5 years written, 4 years oral (V-70). Never answer a Florida question with a Portuguese rule.

## 7. Legal and numerical requirements

**Memorize exactly**

- C.O.L.C.: competent parties, offer and acceptance, legal purpose, consideration. Earnest money is NOT required *(manual; not yet verified)*.
- Statute of frauds: sale of real property, and any contract not performable within 1 year → in writing, signed by the party to be charged. A lease of 1 year or less may be oral *(manual; not yet verified, V-26)*.
- Statute of limitations: written contract **5 years** (s. 95.11(2)(b)); oral **4 years** (s. 95.11(3)) (V-70, verified).
- Void = illegal purpose or no genuine assent. Voidable = minor, fraud, duress, misrepresentation. Unenforceable = missing writing or time-barred *(manual; not yet verified)*.

**Understand**

- A binding promise has value, so mutual promises are consideration.
- A minor's contract is voidable only at the minor's option.
- The statute of frauds controls form; the statute of limitations controls timing. Failing either makes a contract unenforceable, not void.
- Witnesses do not satisfy the statute of frauds; it needs a writing signed by the party to be charged *(manual; not yet verified, V-26)*.
- The 5-year rule has exceptions (payment bonds, deficiency judgments) with their own provisions (V-70). The rule to know for the exam is simply 5 years written, 4 years oral.

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"No earnest money, no contract."** Wrong. Mutual promises are consideration; earnest money is customary, not required *(manual; not yet verified)*.
- **"Voidable means void."** No. A voidable contract binds until the protected party cancels it.
- **"Either party can cancel a minor's contract."** Only the minor can.
- **"Unenforceable means void."** No. It is valid in substance; only the courts' help is missing.
- **"A check, a handshake or two witnesses make an oral sale of land enforceable."** No. It needs a writing signed by the party to be charged *(manual; not yet verified, V-26)*.
- **"Every lease must be written."** A lease of one year or less may be oral *(manual; not yet verified, V-26)*.
- **"Oral contracts get 5 years."** Reversed: written 5, oral 4 (V-70).
- **"The statute of frauds sets the deadline to sue."** That is the statute of limitations.

## 10. Memory sheet

- **C.O.L.C.**: competent parties · offer and acceptance · legal purpose · consideration. No deposit needed *(manual; not yet verified)*.
- Bilateral = promise for promise · unilateral = promise for act · executory = still to do · executed = done.
- **Void** (illegal, no assent) ≠ **voidable** (minor, fraud, duress, misrepresentation; protected party chooses) ≠ **unenforceable** (no writing, time-barred).
- Sale of land, or more than 1 year to perform → written and signed by the party to be charged; lease of 1 year or less may be oral *(manual; not yet verified, V-26)*.
- Limitations: **written 5 · oral 4** (s. 95.11, V-70).

## 2. Why it matters

Florida negotiations usually happen on one written form that passes back and forth: offer, counteroffer, counter-counteroffer. At every moment you must know which offer is still alive, who can still accept, and whether a contract exists. A seller who signs an offer that died two counteroffers ago has signed nothing binding, and an agent who then tells the buyer "you have a deal" is wrong.

On the exam (Section 11, 12% of the questions, V-02), tracing questions are classic: a chain of offers, counteroffers and rejections, then "Is there a contract?" Expect also questions on options (who is bound), on what the buyer holds between contract and closing, and on matching a remedy to its description.

## 3. Simple explanation

**Source status.** The rules on offers, options, equitable title, remedies and assignment come from the study manual *(manual; not yet verified)*. The escrow deadlines are verified (V-36, V-37).

### Step 1: The life of an offer

The **offeror** makes the offer; the **offeree** receives it. A contract is formed when the offeree **accepts** and the acceptance is **communicated** to the offeror. Until then the offer can end in several ways:

| How an offer ends | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Revocation** | The offeror withdraws it any time **before acceptance is communicated**, even after promising to keep it open, unless that promise was paid for (an option) |
| **Rejection** | The offeree says no |
| **Counteroffer** | The offeree changes any term. This rejects and **terminates the original offer** and creates a new offer going the other way |
| **Lapse of time** | The stated deadline passes |
| **Death or insanity** | Of either party, before acceptance |
| **Destruction of the property** | The subject of the offer no longer exists |
| **Acceptance** | Not an ending but a transformation: the offer becomes a contract |

All rows: manual; not yet verified.

**Why can an offeror break a promise to keep the offer open?** Because nobody paid for that promise. A free offer can be withdrawn; a buyer who wants a guaranteed period must buy it with an option (Step 2).

**Why does a counteroffer kill the original?** Acceptance must **mirror** the offer exactly. Any change, however small (a different closing date, a different deposit), makes the reply a counteroffer: a "no" plus a new proposal. The original offer is dead **permanently**; it cannot be accepted later. With each counteroffer the roles switch: whoever countered is now the offeror.

### Step 2: Option contracts

An **option** is a contract in which the owner, in exchange for **consideration** (the option money), promises to sell on fixed terms during a set **option period** *(manual; not yet verified)*:

- The **optionor** is the **owner** and is **bound**: if the optionee chooses to buy during the period, the optionor must sell on the agreed terms.
- The **optionee** is the potential buyer and holds the **choice**: exercise the option (buy) or walk away, losing only the option money.
- The manual classifies an option as **unilateral** (only the owner has made a binding promise). When the optionee **exercises**, it becomes a **bilateral** sale contract.

Memory hook: option**OR** = **O**wne**R**, bound; option**EE** = the one who choos**E**s.

### Step 3: Equitable title

**Title** means ownership. A Florida sale separates two kinds *(manual; not yet verified)*:

- **Equitable title**: the buyer's interest once a sale contract is signed. It is an interest sufficient to insure, plus the right to receive legal title at closing. Until the deed passes legal title, risk and rights are allocated by the contract.
- **Legal title**: ownership itself. It passes at closing by **delivery and acceptance of the deed**, which is then recorded (PT-08).

### Step 4: Remedies for breach

A **breach** is a failure to perform what the contract requires. The non-breaching party's **remedies** *(manual; not yet verified)*:

| Remedy | What it does | Why or when |
|---|---|---|
| **Specific performance** | A court orders the breaching party to perform, for example to convey the property | Land is unique; money may not replace the house the buyer chose |
| **Liquidated damages** | An amount agreed in advance, often the deposit kept by the seller | Avoids proving the actual loss. Florida contracts often treat the deposit this way if the buyer defaults (PT-07) |
| **Rescission** | Undoes the contract and restores both parties to their original positions | The deal is unwound instead of performed |
| **Compensatory damages** | Money to compensate the loss caused by the breach | When money can make up for the loss |

An escrowed deposit is released only according to the contract, a written agreement of the parties, or a settlement procedure (PT-07; details in L05-2).

### Step 5: Assignment

To **assign** a contract is to transfer your rights under it to someone else. Contracts are **generally assignable unless the contract prohibits it**. **Personal-service contracts**, including listings, are **not**, because the other party chose that particular provider (L11-3) *(manual; not yet verified)*.

## 4. Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Offeror / offeree | The person who makes the offer / the person who receives it |
| Revocation | Withdrawal of an offer before acceptance is communicated |
| Counteroffer | A reply that changes the terms; it rejects and terminates the original offer |
| Optionor | The owner who grants the option and is bound by it |
| Optionee | The holder of the option, who may exercise it or let it go |
| Equitable title | The buyer's interest under a signed sale contract before closing |
| Legal title | Ownership itself; passes at closing by delivery and acceptance of the deed |
| Specific performance | Court order to perform the contract as agreed |
| Liquidated damages | Damages agreed in advance (often the deposit) |
| Rescission | Undoing the contract and restoring the parties' original positions |
| Assignment | Transfer of one's contract rights to another person |
| Escrow | Holding of the buyer's deposit by a broker, title company or attorney, released only as the contract, a written agreement of the parties or a settlement procedure allows |

## 5. Practical example

**Tracing offers on a Sarasota house.** Luís is buying; Karen is selling. No legal holidays fall in this period.

| When | Event | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Mon 10:00 | Luís offers $480,000 with a $20,000 deposit, open until Wednesday 6:00 pm. | Luís's offer is alive. |
| Mon 19:00 | Karen counters at $495,000. | Luís's offer is **terminated**. Karen's counteroffer is alive. |
| Tue 09:00 | Luís rejects the counteroffer in writing. | Karen's counteroffer is terminated. **Nothing is open.** |
| Tue 16:00 | Karen changes her mind and signs Luís's original $480,000 offer, "still before Wednesday's deadline". | **No contract.** The original offer died on Monday at 19:00; its deadline cannot revive it. |
| Wed 11:00 | Luís makes a new offer at $485,000. Karen signs it without changes, and her acceptance reaches Luís at 13:00. | **Contract formed** when acceptance is communicated. Luís holds **equitable title**. |
| Fri | Luís hands his $20,000 check to his sales associate; the contract names her brokerage as escrow agent. | She delivers it to her broker by the end of **Monday** (next business day, V-36); the broker deposits it by the end of **Wednesday** (third business day after receipt, V-37). |

Contract analysis: manual; not yet verified.

**Two endings.** If Karen takes a $520,000 cash offer and refuses to close, Luís can ask a court for **specific performance**. If Luís defaults instead, Karen's remedy depends on the contract, which often treats the deposit as **liquidated damages** (PT-07).

**An option, for contrast.** A developer pays a Polk County landowner $10,000 for a 6-month option to buy 40 acres at $1,200,000. The landowner (optionor) cannot withdraw during those 6 months, because the developer paid for that promise. The developer (optionee) may buy, or let the option expire and lose only the $10,000. If exercised, it becomes a bilateral sale contract *(manual; not yet verified)*. Luís's free Monday offer, by contrast, could have been revoked at any moment before acceptance was communicated.

## 6. Portugal comparison

**The familiar principle.** A Portuguese sale typically has two steps: a contrato-promessa de compra e venda (CPCV), then the final sale by escritura pública or documento particular autenticado, which is registered (PT-08; details require verification). A deposit paid on a contrato-promessa is presumed to be **sinal**: if the payer defaults, the other party keeps it; if the recipient defaults, the payer can demand **double** (Código Civil art. 442; PT-07, verified).

**The Florida rule.** Buyer and seller sign **one** contract for sale and purchase, usually the FR/BAR form. It is itself the binding purchase agreement and gives the buyer **equitable title**; at closing the seller delivers a deed, which is recorded (PT-08). Earnest money is normally held in **escrow** by a broker, a title company or an attorney, under strict clocks (V-36, V-37). Contracts often treat the deposit as liquidated damages if the buyer defaults (PT-07).

**The precise difference.** Florida has no second "promise" document and no automatic "return double" rule in exam content. A defaulting seller faces the contract remedies above, such as specific performance; a defaulting buyer's consequence depends on the contract (PT-07).

**Exam implication.** Never apply the double-deposit rule. Contract → equitable title; deed delivered at closing → legal title. Count escrow deadlines in **business** days. *Sinal doubles in Portugal; escrow clocks run in Florida.*

## 7. Legal and numerical requirements

**Memorize exactly**

- An offer may be revoked any time **before acceptance is communicated**, even if promised open, unless an option was paid for *(manual; not yet verified)*.
- Any change = counteroffer = original offer **terminated permanently**. Offers also end by rejection, lapse of time, death or insanity of a party before acceptance, and destruction of the property *(manual; not yet verified)*.
- Option: **optionor** (owner) bound; **optionee** may exercise or walk away, losing only the option money; exercise → bilateral sale contract *(manual; not yet verified)*.
- Signed sale contract → **equitable title**; legal title passes by deed at closing *(manual; not yet verified)*.
- Remedies: specific performance, liquidated damages, rescission, compensatory damages *(manual; not yet verified)*.
- Deposit: associate → broker by end of next business day (V-36); broker deposits by end of third business day (V-37). Weekends and legal holidays are not business days.

**Understand**

- A free promise to hold an offer open can be broken; an option cannot, because it was paid for.
- Specific performance exists because land is unique.
- Liquidated damages are fixed in advance by the contract; the deposit is the usual example.
- Contracts are generally assignable unless prohibited; personal-service contracts, including listings, are not *(manual; not yet verified)*.

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"I promised to keep my offer open until Friday, so I cannot withdraw it."** You can, before acceptance is communicated, unless the buyer paid for an option.
- **"After a counteroffer, the original offer is still on the table."** No. It is terminated permanently.
- **"Accepting with a small change is still an acceptance."** No. Any change makes it a counteroffer.
- **"The optionee is bound to buy."** No. The optionor is bound; the optionee chooses.
- **"A buyer under contract already owns the house."** The buyer holds equitable title; legal title passes by deed at closing.
- **"A defaulting seller must pay the buyer double the deposit."** That is the Portuguese sinal rule (PT-07), not Florida exam content.
- **"Specific performance means money."** No. It is a court order to do what the contract says.

- Offer dies by: revocation (before acceptance is communicated) · rejection · counteroffer · lapse · death or insanity · destruction.
- **Counteroffer kills the offer.** A dead offer cannot be signed back to life.
- **Optionor bound, optionee chooses.** Exercise → bilateral contract.
- **Contract → equitable title. Deed delivered → legal title.**
- Remedies: specific performance (land is unique) · liquidated damages (deposit) · rescission (undo) · compensatory damages (money).
- Assignable unless prohibited; personal service (listings) not.
- Contract rules: manual; not yet verified. Escrow: next business day / third business day (V-36, V-37).

## 2. Why it matters

The listing agreement gives your brokerage the right to market a property and, above all, the right to be paid. Its type decides whether a commission is owed when the seller finds the buyer alone. A missing expiration date, a self-renewing clause or a late copy for the seller can lead to discipline by the Florida Real Estate Commission (FREC), whatever the seller agreed to.

On the exam (Section 11, 12% of the questions, V-02), listing questions are predictable:

- The seller finds her own buyer: is a commission owed? The answer depends on one word, **agency** or **right**.
- Which clause is NOT allowed in a written listing? (Automatic renewal.)
- How soon must the seller receive a copy? (24 hours.)
- Which event does NOT end a listing? (The listing associate moving to another firm.)

## 3. Simple explanation

**Source status.** The written-listing requirements are verified (s. 475.25(1)(r), F.S.; V-49). The listing types, commission rules and termination rules come from the study manual *(manual; not yet verified)*.

### Step 1: A listing is an employment contract with the brokerage

A **listing agreement** is an employment (personal-service) contract: the seller (the **principal**) hires the **brokerage** to find a buyer *(manual; not yet verified)*. Three consequences follow:

- The listing belongs to the **brokerage**, not to the individual sales associate. If the associate moves to another firm, the listing stays with the brokerage.
- Because it is a personal-service contract, it is **not assignable** to another brokerage without the seller's consent.
- The money path is fixed: seller → brokerage → associate. A sales associate may be paid only by the employing broker (PT-06; manual; not yet verified).

### Step 2: The four listing types and when the commission is earned

| Type | Brokerages | If the seller finds a buyer alone | If anyone else produces the buyer |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Open listing** | Any number | **No commission** | Only the brokerage that is the **procuring cause** is paid; the others get nothing |
| **Exclusive agency** | One | **No commission**: the seller keeps the right to sell personally | The listing brokerage earns it |
| **Exclusive right of sale** | One | **Commission owed** to the listing brokerage | The listing brokerage earns it: it is paid **no matter who sells** |
| **Net listing** | — | — | The broker keeps whatever the sale brings **above the seller's set net price** |

All rows: manual; not yet verified.

**Procuring cause** means the broker whose efforts started an uninterrupted chain of events that led to the sale. It decides open listings, where several brokers compete and only one is paid. The manual calls exclusive right of sale the strongest and standard form: it protects the brokerage even against the seller's own efforts.

**Net listings.** The manual says a net listing is **legal in Florida but conflict-laden** *(manual; not yet verified)*. Why the conflict? If the broker advises the seller on the net price, the broker earns more the lower that net is set, so the broker's interest pulls against the client's.

### Step 3: Florida's rules for written listings (verified, V-49)

Under **s. 475.25(1)(r), F.S.**, FREC may discipline a licensee who fails to include the required terms in a written listing agreement or to deliver a copy on time. Every **written** listing agreement must contain:

1. a **definite expiration date**;
2. a **description of the property**;
3. the **price and terms**;
4. the **fee or commission**;
5. the **signature of the principal(s)**.

It **may not contain** any provision requiring the person signing to **notify the broker of an intention to cancel after the definite expiration date**. The statute never uses the words "automatic renewal", but in effect it bars self-renewing listings that need a cancellation notice; exam materials call this **"no automatic renewal"**. A **copy** must be given to the **person signing within 24 hours** of obtaining the written listing agreement.

**Why these rules?** A definite end date and the ban on cancel-by-notice clauses stop sellers from being locked in by fine print. The 24-hour copy lets the seller know exactly what was signed.

**Must every listing be written?** These requirements apply to **written** listings; the statute does not require every listing to be in writing (V-49). The manual states that a listing need not be written to be valid unless its term exceeds one year, so an oral open listing can be enforceable *(manual; not yet verified; it mirrors the one-year rule of the statute of frauds, also not yet verified, V-26)*. In practice, put every listing in writing.

### Step 4: How listings end

A listing terminates by *(manual; not yet verified)*:

| Way | Example |
|---|---|
| **Performance** | The brokerage finds the buyer and the sale is completed |
| **Expiration** | The definite expiration date passes |
| **Mutual agreement** | Seller and brokerage agree to cancel |
| **Abandonment** | One side abandons the contract |
| **Destruction of the property** | The house burns down |
| **Death or incapacity of either party** | The seller dies or becomes incapacitated |

What does **not** end it: the listing associate leaving the brokerage, because the contract is with the brokerage. Nor can it be passed to another brokerage without the seller's consent.

## 4. Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Listing agreement | Employment (personal-service) contract between seller and brokerage |
| Open listing | Listing given to several brokers; only the procuring broker is paid; owner may sell free |
| Exclusive agency listing | One brokerage; owner keeps the right to sell without commission |
| Exclusive right of sale | One brokerage earns the commission no matter who sells |
| Net listing | Broker keeps any amount above the seller's net price |
| Procuring cause | The broker whose efforts started an uninterrupted chain of events leading to the sale |
| Automatic renewal | Exam shorthand for a clause requiring notice to cancel after expiration; not allowed |

## 5. Practical example

**A listing in Fort Lauderdale.** On Tuesday at 10:00 Maria signs an exclusive-right-of-sale listing with Coastal Realty. Her sales associate is João.

| Situation | Answer |
|---|---|
| The draft says: "This agreement renews for successive 6-month terms unless the seller gives 30 days' written notice." | Not allowed. It requires notice to cancel after the expiration date (s. 475.25(1)(r), V-49). Replace it with a definite expiration date. |
| When must Maria receive her copy? | Within 24 hours of obtaining the signed listing: by **Wednesday at 10:00**. Hours, not business days (V-49). |
| Maria's cousin buys the house directly from her, with no help from Coastal. | **Commission owed** to Coastal: exclusive right of sale pays no matter who sells *(manual; not yet verified)*. |
| Same facts, but the listing was exclusive agency. | **No commission**: Maria kept the right to sell personally *(manual; not yet verified)*. |
| Instead, Maria gave open listings to three brokerages, and Broker B's showing started the chain of events that led to the sale. | Only Broker B, the procuring cause, is paid. Had Maria sold to her cousin herself, no brokerage would be paid *(manual; not yet verified)*. |
| João moves to another brokerage in month two. | The listing **stays with Coastal Realty** and does not end. It does not follow João: the contract is between Maria and Coastal, and a listing is not assignable to another brokerage without consent *(manual; not yet verified)*. |
| At closing, grateful Maria hands João a $500 gift card. | A violation unless it goes through his broker; an associate is paid only by the employing broker (PT-06; manual; not yet verified). |

If Coastal is to act as Maria's single agent, the single agent notice must be given before or at the time of the listing agreement, or before showing the property, whichever comes first (s. 475.278, V-48; see L04-3).

## 6. Portugal comparison

**The familiar principle.** In Portugal the **contrato de mediação imobiliária** must be in writing. Missing mandatory content makes it null, and the mediation company cannot invoke that nullity. Contract models with general clauses need IMPIC approval or must follow the official model (Portaria n.º 228/2018) (Lei 15/2013, art. 16; PT-05, verified). Remuneration is due on the conclusion and perfection of the deal the mediation aimed at; under exclusivity it remains due if the deal fails for reasons attributable to the client (art. 19), and courts require a causal link between the mediator's work and the deal (PT-06, verified).

**The Florida rule.** A listing is an employment contract between the seller and the **brokerage**. Written listings need a definite expiration date, the property description, price and terms, the fee and the principal's signature; no cancel-by-notice ("automatic renewal") clause; and a copy to the signer within 24 hours (V-49). The listing type decides when the commission is earned *(manual; not yet verified)*. Payment flows seller → brokerage → associate (PT-06).

**The precise difference.**

- Portugal requires the written form. Florida's statute regulates **written** listings but does not require every listing to be written (V-49).
- Florida's verified consequence for a missing term or a late copy is **discipline of the licensee** (V-49), not the Portuguese nullity rule.
- The Portuguese causal-link idea resembles Florida's **procuring cause** in open listings. But under a Florida exclusive right of sale the brokerage is paid even when the seller sells with no help at all. Whether a Portuguese exclusive contract pays when the owner sells alone requires verification.
- In Florida, a sales associate can never accept payment directly from a customer (PT-06; manual; not yet verified).

**Exam implication.** When the owner finds the buyer: exclusive right of sale → commission; exclusive agency → none; open → only the procuring broker. Memory aid: **RIGHT of sale = paid regardless; AGENCY = owner keeps the right to sell free.**

## 7. Legal and numerical requirements

**Memorize exactly**

- Written listing (s. 475.25(1)(r), V-49): definite expiration date · property description · price and terms · fee or commission · principal's signature · no clause requiring notice to cancel after expiration (no automatic renewal) · copy to the person signing within **24 hours**.
- Open → procuring cause only; owner sells free. Exclusive agency → owner sells free. Exclusive right of sale → paid no matter who sells *(manual; not yet verified)*.
- Net listing: broker keeps the excess over the seller's net; legal in Florida per the manual, but conflict-laden *(manual; not yet verified)*.
- Termination: performance, expiration, mutual agreement, abandonment, destruction of the property, death or incapacity of either party *(manual; not yet verified)*.

**Understand**

- The listing is with the brokerage: an associate's departure does not end it, and it is not assignable to another brokerage without consent *(manual; not yet verified)*.
- The statute regulates written listings but does not require writing; its sanction is license discipline (V-49). The manual's view: writing is needed only for a term over one year *(manual; not yet verified, V-26)*.
- Commission rates are negotiable; a question describing a "standard rate" among brokers suggests price fixing (PT-06; manual; not yet verified).

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"The listing is my contract, so it moves with me."** No. It is the brokerage's contract with the seller.
- **"Exclusive agency and exclusive right of sale are the same."** No. Under exclusive agency the owner can sell personally without paying a commission.
- **"An automatic renewal is fine if the seller initials it."** No. A written listing may contain no clause requiring notice to cancel after the expiration date (V-49).
- **"The seller's copy is due within 3 business days."** No. Within 24 hours (V-49).
- **"Every listing must be in writing by statute."** The listing statute, s. 475.25(1)(r), regulates written listings; it does not require writing (V-49). The manual says writing is needed only for terms over one year *(manual; not yet verified)*.
- **"Net listings are illegal in Florida."** The manual says they are legal, though conflict-laden *(manual; not yet verified)*.
- **"A seller may tip the associate directly."** No. Money flows through the broker (PT-06).

## 2. Why it matters

The standard Florida residential sale contract is the FR/BAR form, which has an AS IS version. The **effective date** starts every deadline, and "time is of the essence" makes those deadlines strict. Around the contract sits a set of required disclosures, several recent or renamed, so older textbooks are out of date. And a seller who thinks "AS IS" allows silence about a known hidden defect risks liability.

On the exam (Section 11, 12% of the questions, V-02), expect:

- What is the effective date? (Not the first offer, not closing.)
- Which buildings need the radon notice? (Any building, sale or rental.)
- Lead-based paint: housing built before which year? (1978.) Flood disclosure: when? (At or before contract.)
- An AS IS seller hid a known latent defect. Liable? (He may be: *Johnson v. Davis*.)

## 3. Simple explanation

**Source status.** The descriptions of the FR/BAR forms, the effective date, "time is of the essence" and contingencies come from the study manual *(manual; not yet verified)*. The case *Johnson v. Davis* is cited by the study manual and the course notes but has no verification entry, so its disclosure rule is also *(manual; not yet verified)*. The disclosures in Step 4 are verified against statutes and federal rules (V-ids given), except the customary seller's disclosure.

### Step 1: The FR/BAR contract and its AS IS version

**FR/BAR** stands for Florida Realtors / Florida Bar. It is the standard residential sale contract, and it has an **AS IS** version *(manual; not yet verified)*:

- The buyer takes the property in its **current condition**; the seller makes no repairs.
- The buyer keeps a **due-diligence right to inspect and cancel** within the inspection period.
- The seller's duty to disclose **known latent defects** (hidden defects) **survives** AS IS (*Johnson v. Davis*, Step 5).

Why both? AS IS gives the seller certainty about repairs; the inspection right keeps the buyer from buying blind.

### Step 2: The effective date and "time is of the essence"

The **effective date** is the date the **last party signs or initials the final offer or counteroffer** and it is delivered (communicated) to the other side. **Every contract deadline counts from it**: inspection, financing and the rest *(manual; not yet verified)*. Before that moment there is no contract, so no clock can start.

**"Time is of the essence"** is a clause that makes every deadline strictly enforceable: missing a deadline by a single day is a breach *(manual; not yet verified)*. Calendar every deadline on the effective date.

### Step 3: Contingencies

A **contingency** is a condition that must be met for the contract to go forward. Standard ones: **financing, appraisal, inspection, sale of the buyer's home**. If a contingency is not met, the protected party may cancel and the deposit is returned *(manual; not yet verified)*. For escrow: a buyer's good-faith failure to obtain financing is one of the statutory cases in which the broker may return the deposit without notifying FREC, the Florida Real Estate Commission (V-16; see L05-2).

### Step 4: Required disclosures around the contract

| Disclosure | Applies to | When | Content | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Radon** | Sale or rental of **any building** | On at least one document signed at or before the sale contract or rental agreement | Statutory radon notice | s. 404.056(5); V-73 |
| **Lead-based paint** | Housing built **before 1978**, sales and leases | If disclosure comes after an offer, before the seller or lessor accepts it | EPA-approved pamphlet (EPA: the federal Environmental Protection Agency); known lead paint and hazards; available records and reports; Lead Warning Statement and signed acknowledgments in the contract or lease | 40 CFR 745 Subpart F; V-60 |
| **Property tax disclosure summary** | Buyer of **residential** property | At or before contract | Do not rely on the seller's current taxes; a sale or improvements can raise the assessment | s. 689.261; V-75 |
| **Energy-efficiency rating information** | Buyer of a building | At or before signing the contract | Information on the **option** of an energy-efficiency rating (older books: "brochure") | s. 553.996; V-74 |
| **Flood disclosure** | Seller of **residential** real property | **At or before** contract execution | Prescribed form: flood insurance (homeowners' policies do not cover flood); past flood insurance claims; federal flood assistance received; known flood damage | s. 689.302; V-06 |
| **Condominium documents** | Condo buyers | Buyer's cancellation window | Developer sale: **15 days**. Resale: **7 days**, excluding Saturdays, Sundays and legal holidays, after signing and receiving the documents (since July 1, 2025). Both rights end at closing | s. 718.503; V-08 |
| **HOA (homeowners' association) disclosure summary** | Buyers in an HOA community | **Before** the contract is signed | If late, buyer may void within **3 days** after receiving it or before closing, whichever is first; not waivable; ends at closing | s. 720.401; V-65 |
| **Seller's property disclosure** | Customary | — | Honesty required regardless; *Johnson v. Davis* applies with or without a form | manual; not yet verified |

**Notes on the table**

- **Flood (V-06, gap-filled).** Added by CS/CS/HB 1049 (2024). Its effective date was not confirmed in the verification pass. The manual only mentions "flood-zone awareness"; older books miss it. The verified rule is not limited to mapped flood zones. Separately, a residential **lease of 1 year or longer** needs the landlord's flood disclosure at or before signing (since October 1, 2025); shorter leases are not covered (V-07).
- **Condo resale: outdated figure.** Older books, including the manual's appendix, say **3 business days**. Current law is 7 days as above (V-08).
- **Property tax: why it matters.** A long-time homestead owner's assessed value may have been held down by the Save Our Homes cap (increase of no more than 3% or the change in the CPI, the consumer price index, whichever is lower; V-67). The buyer's tax bill can be much higher than the seller's.
- **Lead-based paint details (V-60).** Excluded from "target housing": housing for the elderly or persons with disabilities, and 0-bedroom dwellings, unless a child under 6 lives or is expected to live there. Exempt: foreclosure sales; leases of 100 days or less with no renewal; leases of housing certified lead-free; renewals where disclosure was already made. **Buyers** get a **10-day** opportunity for a risk assessment or inspection before being bound, unless the parties agree in writing on a different period; the buyer may waive it in writing. **Tenants** get the disclosure but **not** the 10-day period. The seller also discloses to each agent, and agents must ensure compliance. HUD's identical rule (HUD: the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development) is 24 CFR Part 35, Subpart A. More in L19-2.

### Step 5: *Johnson v. Davis*: AS IS shifts repairs, not disclosure

Under *Johnson v. Davis*, a seller must disclose **known** facts that **materially affect the value** of the property and are **not readily observable**. The duty applies with or without a disclosure form, and **AS IS does not excuse it**. AS IS limits the seller's **repair** obligations; it never permits concealing a known latent defect *(manual; not yet verified)*.

The duty has limits. A fact the seller does not know, or one that is readily observable, is outside this rule (though the seller must still be honest). By statute, the fact that a homicide, suicide or death occurred on the property, or that an occupant had HIV/AIDS, is **not** a material fact that must be disclosed (s. 689.25; V-47, verified; see L04-4).

## 5. Practical example

**An AS IS sale in Jacksonville.** Mr. Hale sells his 1972 house on the AS IS FR/BAR contract. He knows the septic drain field backs up after heavy rain; it is now the dry season and nothing is visible.

| Step | What happens | Analysis |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Priya offers on Monday. Mr. Hale counters on Tuesday. Priya initials the counteroffer on Thursday evening, and it is delivered to Mr. Hale's agent the same evening. | **Effective date: Thursday.** All deadlines count from it *(manual; not yet verified)*. |
| 2 | At or before contract: radon notice, flood disclosure form, property tax summary, energy-rating information. | Required (V-73, V-06, V-75, V-74). |
| 3 | Built in 1972: target housing. | Lead pamphlet, disclosures and Lead Warning Statement. Priya gets a 10-day inspection opportunity unless a different period is agreed in writing or she waives it in writing (V-60). |
| 4 | Priya's inspector finds nothing in the dry weather. Variation: she sends a cancellation notice one day after the inspection period ends. | Too late: time is of the essence, so the deadline is strict *(manual; not yet verified)*. |
| 5 | After closing, the rains come and sewage backs up. Mr. Hale knew. | He **may be liable**: a known, material, not readily observable defect (*Johnson v. Davis*; manual, not yet verified). "AS IS" is no defense to concealment. |
| 6 | A previous owner died of natural causes in the house. | Not a material fact that must be disclosed (s. 689.25, V-47). |

## 6. Portugal comparison

**The familiar principle.** In Portugal a CPCV is typically signed first, and the sale is later formalized by escritura pública or documento particular autenticado and registered (PT-08; details require verification). An energy certificate (certificado energético) is required to sell or rent (PT-23; requires verification).

**The Florida rule.** One contract, usually the FR/BAR, is the binding purchase agreement. Once the last party initials the final counteroffer, the effective date starts the inspection and financing deadlines, and "time is of the essence" makes each strict (PT-08). For energy, the buyer must receive **information about the option** of an energy-efficiency rating at or before contract (s. 553.996; V-74; PT-23).

**The precise difference.** Florida has no separate promise contract: deadlines run from the effective date of the one contract. Florida's energy rule is information, not a certificate. The course sources have no Portuguese comparison for the radon, lead, flood or property-tax disclosures; any Portuguese equivalent requires verification.

**Exam implication.** A Florida seller does **not** have to obtain an energy rating before listing (PT-23). Memory aid: *Florida: information, not certificate.*

## 7. Legal and numerical requirements

**Memorize exactly**

- Effective date = last party signs or initials the final offer or counteroffer, and it is delivered; all deadlines run from it. Time is of the essence = deadlines strict *(manual; not yet verified)*.

**Understand**

- AS IS moves repair costs to the buyer, not the duty of honesty.
- There is no contract until the last signature is delivered, so no deadline can start before the effective date.
- Stigmas (death, homicide, suicide, HIV/AIDS) are not material facts that must be disclosed (V-47).

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"AS IS means the seller can stay silent."** No. AS IS shifts repairs, never the duty to disclose known hidden defects (*Johnson v. Davis*).
- **"The effective date is the date of the first offer, or of closing."** No. It is when the last party signs or initials the final offer or counteroffer and it is delivered.
- **"Radon notices are needed only for older homes or when radon is found."** No. Every sale or rental of any building (V-73).
- **"Tenants also get the 10-day lead inspection period."** No. Buyers only; tenants get the disclosure (V-60).
- **"The flood disclosure is only for flood zones, or on request."** No. Residential sellers give it at or before contract (V-06).
- **"Florida sellers must obtain an energy rating."** No. The buyer receives information about the option of a rating (V-74).
- **"Condo resale buyers have 3 business days."** Outdated. Now 7 days, excluding weekends and legal holidays (V-08).
