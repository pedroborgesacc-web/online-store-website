## 2. Why it matters

Most of your Florida buyers will bring a pre-approval letter that names a loan program: **FHA**, **VA** or **conventional**. The program tells you a lot before the offer is even written. It tells you how much cash the buyer may need, which insurance or fee the buyer will pay and, for a VA loan, which document sets the property's value (the **CRV**). If you misunderstand who stands behind the loan, you will also misunderstand the buyer's position. A common example is telling a client that "the government is lending you the money". It is not.

For the exam, Section 13 (Types of Mortgages and Sources of Financing) carries about 4% of the state exam (V-02). That is roughly 4 of the 100 questions (V-01). The favourite traps in this lesson are the verbs (FHA **insures**, VA **guarantees**) and the matching of each program to the right charge: **MIP**, **funding fee** or **PMI**.

## 3. Simple explanation

> **Source status.** All the loan-program content in this lesson comes from the study manual; only the exam-format figures in section 2 are verified (V-01, V-02). The program roles (insure, guarantee, no government backing) and the names of the charges are standard exam vocabulary (manual; not yet verified). The two figures, **FHA 3.5% minimum down payment** and **PMI above 80% LTV**, are listed under V-26 as still not independently verified. V-26 also warns that such figures are program rules that change over time. Learn them as the course states them.

### Start with the lender's problem

In L12-1 you saw that the **mortgage** gives the lender a lien on the property. A lien is only as good as the property's value, though. If the borrower stops paying and the home sells for less than the debt, the lender loses money.

The buyer's **down payment** is the lender's cushion. The down payment is the cash the buyer pays toward the price that is not borrowed. A big cushion means low risk. A small cushion means the lender wants someone else to share the risk of loss. The course teaches three ways to do that:

| Who carries the extra risk? | Program | Exam verb |
|---|---|---|
| A federal agency, the FHA | FHA loan | **Insures** |
| A federal agency, the VA | VA loan | **Guarantees** |
| A private insurance company | Conventional loan with PMI | (private insurance) |

In all three cases a **private lender** supplies the money. That lender may be a bank, a credit union, a savings association or a mortgage banker. **The government agencies in this chapter never hand money to buyers.**

### Measuring the cushion: LTV

**LTV (loan-to-value ratio)** = loan amount ÷ property value, written as a percentage.

- A $400,000 home with $40,000 down → loan $360,000 → 360,000 ÷ 400,000 = **90% LTV**.
- When the property's value equals the price and there is only one loan, the down payment percentage and the LTV add up to 100%: 10% down ↔ 90% LTV.

### FHA: the government insures

- The **FHA (Federal Housing Administration)** is part of **HUD**, the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development.
- The FHA **insures** loans made by **approved lenders**. It never lends.
- The borrower pays **MIP (mortgage insurance premium)**: an **up-front** premium and an **annual** premium.
- The minimum down payment is **3.5%**, **with qualifying credit** (manual; not yet verified, V-26). Do not drop the qualification: the 3.5% figure is tied to the borrower's credit.
- **Why it works:** the insurance protects the lender, so the lender can accept a small cushion.

### VA: the government guarantees

- The **VA (Department of Veterans Affairs)** **guarantees** loans to **eligible veterans**. Private lenders lend. Who counts as eligible is set by VA rules that this course does not cover.
- The guarantee can take the place of a down payment, so **0 down is possible**.
- The borrower pays a **funding fee**. This is not MIP and not PMI.
- The value is set by a **Certificate of Reasonable Value (CRV)**. The VA now calls this document a **Notice of Value**, but the course and the exam use **CRV**.

### Conventional: no government backing

- A **conventional loan** has **no government insurance or guarantee**.
- When the LTV is **above 80%**, the lender requires **PMI (private mortgage insurance)** (manual; not yet verified, V-26). The borrower pays it.
- PMI **can be cancelled as equity grows**, that is, as the borrower's ownership share of the home rises. The course gives no cancellation percentage, so do not invent one.
- "Above 80%" means **more than** 80%. A loan at exactly 80% LTV (20% down) does not trigger PMI under the course rule.

### Insure vs guarantee: one memory hook

Both protect the **lender**, not the borrower. The exam uses the verbs as labels, so pair each verb with its charge:

- **Insurance charges a premium**: FHA **insures** → **MIP** (mortgage insurance **premium**).
- **A guarantee charges a fee**: VA **guarantees** → **funding fee**.
- **Private insurance for conventional loans**: **PMI**, only above 80% LTV.

## 5. Practical example

You are showing a $300,000 townhouse in Tampa to three different buyers. Each one has a different pre-approval.

**Ana: FHA, first-time buyer with qualifying credit.**
- Minimum down payment: 3.5% × $300,000 = **$10,500**.
- Loan: $300,000 − $10,500 = **$289,500**, so the LTV is 289,500 ÷ 300,000 = **96.5%**.
- The money comes from an FHA-approved bank. The FHA insures the loan, and Ana pays **MIP**, up front and annually.
- Ana tells you she was "approved by the FHA". Correct the vocabulary politely: she was approved by an **FHA-approved lender**, and the FHA insures that lender's loan.

**Marcus: VA, eligible veteran.**
- **0 down is possible**, so the loan could be the full $300,000. The VA guarantee replaces the down payment.
- A mortgage banker lends the money and the VA guarantees the loan. Marcus pays a **funding fee**.
- The VA's value for the townhouse is set by the **CRV**. Marcus's lender may call it a "Notice of Value"; it is the same document.

**Priya: conventional loan.**
- With 10% down ($30,000), the loan is $270,000, which is **90% LTV**. That is above 80%, so the lender requires **PMI**.
- With 20% down ($60,000), the loan is $240,000, which is **80% LTV**. That is not *above* 80%, so PMI is not required under the course rule.
- If Priya does pay PMI, it can be cancelled later as her equity grows.

**Your takeaway as the agent.** All three buyers borrow from **private lenders**. The program only decides who shares the lender's risk and what the buyer pays for that protection.

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"The veteran got his loan from the VA."** No. He got it from a private lender, and the VA **guaranteed** it.
- **"The FHA lends money to first-time buyers."** No. The FHA **insures** loans made by approved lenders.
- **"FHA borrowers pay PMI."** No. FHA borrowers pay **MIP**. PMI belongs to **conventional** loans.
- **"VA borrowers pay MIP."** No. VA borrowers pay a **funding fee**.
- **"PMI protects the buyer."** No. It protects the **lender**, even though the borrower pays for it.
- **"PMI is required at 80% LTV."** No. It is required only **above** 80%.
- **"PMI lasts for the life of the loan."** No. It can be cancelled as equity grows.
- **"A Notice of Value is something different from a CRV."** No. It is the VA's current name for the same idea. The exam answer is **CRV**.
- **"VA loans always mean 0 down."** No. 0 down is **possible** because the guarantee can replace a down payment, but it is not compulsory.

## 2. Why it matters

In Florida practice these loan names come up in real conversations. A seller offers to "carry the paper", that is, to lend the buyer part of the price herself. A developer says each lot will be "released" at closing. A buyer wants one loan for a furnished beach condo and its furniture. A retired client asks about a reverse mortgage. If you can name the loan, you can explain what it does and spot a structure that will not work, such as a wraparound over a loan with a due-on-sale clause.

For the exam, Section 13 carries about 4% of the questions (V-02). Expect vocabulary matching: a described loan to name, or a named loan to explain. The ARM formula (**index + margin**) is a classic.

## 3. Simple explanation

> **Source status.** The loan definitions come from the study manual (manual; not yet verified). The reverse-mortgage age of **62** is listed under V-26 as not yet verified. The right of rescission on home equity loans is verified against federal rules (V-51).

### Two questions about every loan

Every loan can be described by answering two separate questions:

1. **How is it repaid?** This is the loan's *shape*: amortized, balloon, interest-only or adjustable.
2. **What is it for, and what does it cover?** This is the loan's *purpose*: package, blanket, wraparound, purchase-money, home equity, reverse or construction.

One loan can carry a label from each list. A seller-financed (purchase-money) loan, for example, can also be partially amortized with a balloon.

### Repayment shapes

The key fact behind all four shapes is that **interest is charged on the unpaid balance**. Whatever the payment does to the balance decides the shape.

**1. Fully amortized loan.** The payments are **level** (the same every month), and each payment covers that month's interest plus some principal. The last payment brings the balance to zero. Because the balance falls, **the interest part falls and the principal part rises**, even though the payment stays the same.

*Example:* a $200,000 loan at 6% for 30 years has a level principal-and-interest payment of $1,199.10.
- Month 1: interest = $200,000 × 6% ÷ 12 = **$1,000.00**; principal = $1,199.10 − $1,000.00 = **$199.10**; new balance $199,800.90.
- Month 2: interest = $199,800.90 × 6% ÷ 12 = **$999.00**; principal = **$200.10**.

**2. Partially amortized loan with a balloon.** The payments reduce the principal, but not to zero by the end of the term. The remaining balance is due as one large final payment, the **balloon payment**. This structure keeps the monthly payments lower, but the borrower must then pay off, refinance or sell. Balloon abuse is one of the predatory practices you met in L12-3.

**3. Interest-only (term) loan.** The payments cover **interest only**, so the principal never goes down. The **whole principal** is due at the end of the term.

**4. Adjustable-rate mortgage (ARM).** The interest rate changes during the loan:

- **Rate = index + margin.**
- The **index** is an outside market rate. It **moves**.
- The **margin** is the lender's fixed add-on, written in the loan. It **does not move**.
- **Caps** limit the changes. A **periodic cap** limits how much the rate can change at each adjustment. A **lifetime cap** limits how much it can change over the whole life of the loan.
- Some ARMs start with a **teaser rate**: a low introductory rate that later gives way to index + margin, within the caps.

*Example (illustrative contract terms, not legal limits):* start rate 6.75% (index 4.25% + margin 2.50%), periodic cap 2%, lifetime cap 5%. At the first adjustment the index rises to 7.00%. Index + margin is now 9.50%, but the periodic cap allows at most 6.75% + 2% = **8.75%**. The rate can never exceed 6.75% + 5% = **11.75%**.

### Purpose types

| Loan | What it covers or does |
|---|---|
| **Package mortgage** | **Real and personal property** in one loan (for example, a furnished condo) |
| **Blanket mortgage** | **Several parcels** under one mortgage, with a **partial release clause** that frees each parcel as it is paid down (a developer's loan) |
| **Wraparound mortgage** | A **new junior loan that includes an existing loan**. The buyer pays the wrap lender (often the seller), who keeps paying the old loan. **The existing loan must have no due-on-sale clause** |
| **Purchase-money mortgage** | **Seller financing**: the seller becomes the lender for part of the price |
| **Home equity loan / HELOC** | Borrowing against equity the owner has already built; a HELOC (home equity line of credit) is drawn as needed |
| **Reverse mortgage** | For owners **62 or older** (manual; not yet verified, V-26). The **lender pays the owner**; the loan is **repaid on sale, move-out or death** |
| **Construction loan** | Money released in stages (**draws**) as building progresses, then replaced by permanent **"take-out"** financing |

**Why does a wraparound need "no due-on-sale clause"?** A due-on-sale (alienation) clause lets the lender demand the whole balance when the property is conveyed (L12-1). The wrap only works if the old loan can stay in place after the sale, so a due-on-sale clause would defeat it.

**Why does a reverse mortgage balance grow?** Money flows to the owner and repayment is not due until sale, move-out or death, so the debt rises over time: the reverse of an amortized loan.

**Home equity loans and rescission (verified, V-51).** Under the federal Truth in Lending rules, credit secured by the borrower's principal dwelling, such as a home equity loan or a refinance with a new lender, comes with a **3-business-day right of rescission**. Saturdays count as business days. The right does **not** apply to a loan used to buy or build the home.

## 5. Practical example

**A developer in Lakeland.** Palmetto Ridge LLC finances 20 home sites with **one blanket mortgage** that has a **partial release clause**. When a lot sells, part of the proceeds pays down the blanket loan and the lender **releases** that lot, so the buyer receives it free of the developer's mortgage. Each house is built with a **construction loan** paid out in **draws** (foundation, framing, roof, finish); when it is finished, the buyer's permanent loan **takes out** the construction loan. The furnished model home sells "turnkey", and its buyer finances the house **and** the furniture with one **package mortgage**.

**A seller in Sarasota.** Rosa sells her house for $400,000. She still owes $150,000 on a loan that has **no due-on-sale clause**. The buyer pays $40,000 down, and Rosa carries a **$360,000 wraparound** note:

- $400,000 − $40,000 = **$360,000** wrap loan.
- It includes the $150,000 old loan, so the new credit Rosa herself extends is $360,000 − $150,000 = **$210,000**. She hands over no cash: this is part of her equity, which she collects over time instead of at closing.
- The buyer pays Rosa on $360,000. Rosa keeps paying her own lender on the $150,000.

Rosa is the lender, so this is also **purchase-money** (seller) financing. If her old loan had contained a due-on-sale clause, the sale would let her lender demand the full $150,000, and the wrap would fail.

**A retiree in Naples.** Mrs. Costa, aged 70, has substantial equity and wants income without moving. A **reverse mortgage** fits her age (62 or older; manual; not yet verified, V-26): the lender pays her, and the loan is repaid when she sells, moves out or dies.

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"In an ARM, the margin moves with the market."** No. The **index** moves; the margin is fixed.
- **"The ARM rate is set by the Fed's discount rate."** No. The rate is **index + margin** under the loan contract.
- **"Caps mean the rate cannot rise."** No. Caps limit the **size** of changes, per adjustment and over the life of the loan.
- **"On an amortized loan the payment falls over time."** No. The payment is level; only the interest/principal mix changes.
- **"A package mortgage covers several parcels."** No, that is a **blanket** mortgage. A package covers real **and personal** property.
- **"With a blanket mortgage, nothing can be sold until the whole loan is repaid."** No. The **release clause** frees each parcel as it is paid.
- **"A wraparound pays off the old loan."** No. The old loan stays in place inside the wrap, which is why it must have no due-on-sale clause.
- **"In a reverse mortgage the owner makes monthly payments to the lender."** No. The **lender pays the owner**.
- **"The 3-business-day rescission right applies to the loan used to buy the home."** No. It applies to credit secured by the principal dwelling, such as a home equity loan, but not to loans to buy or build it (V-51).

## 2. Why it matters

Mortgage money is not created at the closing table. It flows from savers and investors, through lenders, to your buyer, and the Federal Reserve turns the tap up or down. This affects your daily work in Florida. When credit tightens, rates rise, fewer buyers qualify and listings sit longer. When a buyer's loan is sold after closing, you can explain why a new company may now be collecting the payments. When a loan offer reads "6.50% with 2 points", you can tell the buyer what those points cost in dollars.

For the exam, Section 13 carries about 4% of the questions (V-02). Three question types repeat: **sort the entity** (primary or secondary market?), **name the Fed tool**, and **compute points** (cost in dollars, or how many points a yield change needs).

## 3. Simple explanation

> **Source status.** The market players, the Fed's tools and the definition of discount points come from the study manual (manual; not yet verified). The two point figures, **1 point = 1% of the loan** and **about 1/8% of yield per point**, are listed under N-125 / V-26 as not yet verified, and the 1/8% figure is a **course rule of thumb**, not a law.

### Why there are two markets

A lender has a limited amount of money. If a bank kept every 30-year loan it made, it would soon have nothing left to lend. The fix is to **sell** existing loans, get the cash back and lend it again.

- **Primary market**: where loans are **originated** (made) and the borrower deals with the lender.
- **Secondary market**: where **existing** loans are bought and sold, so that primary lenders get fresh money.

**Consumers borrow in the primary market only.** No buyer ever applies for a mortgage at Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac.

### The primary market (lenders the borrower meets)

| Player | What it does |
|---|---|
| Commercial banks | Lend to borrowers |
| Credit unions | Lend to their members |
| Savings associations | Lend to borrowers |
| **Mortgage bankers** | **Lend** their own or borrowed funds and **often service** loans (collect the payments) |
| **Mortgage brokers** | **Arrange** loans between borrower and lender for a fee; **lend nothing** |

### The secondary market (buyers of existing loans)

| Player | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Fannie Mae** (Federal National Mortgage Association) | **Buys** loans from lenders (conventional, and also government loans) and issues **mortgage-backed securities (MBS)** |
| **Freddie Mac** (Federal Home Loan Mortgage Corporation) | Same role: **buys** loans and issues MBS |
| **Ginnie Mae** (Government National Mortgage Association) | **Guarantees** securities backed by **FHA and VA** loans |

A **mortgage-backed security** is an investment backed by a pool of mortgage loans. Investors buy it, and their money flows back to the lenders, who lend again.

### Intermediation and disintermediation

- **Intermediation**: banks and savings institutions pool savers' deposits and lend them out as mortgages.
- **Disintermediation**: savers **withdraw** their deposits to get better returns elsewhere. The lenders lose the money they would have lent, which **starves mortgage lending**.

### The Federal Reserve: three tools

The **Federal Reserve** (the Fed) is the central bank of the United States. It does **not** set mortgage rates. It changes how easy or tight credit is, and rates follow.

| Tool | What it is | To tighten credit | To loosen credit |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Open-market operations** | Buying and selling government securities. **The day-to-day tool** | The Fed **sells** securities: buyers pay the Fed, so money leaves the banks | The Fed **buys** securities: money flows into the banks |
| **Discount rate** | The rate member banks pay to borrow from the Fed | **Raise** it | **Lower** it |
| **Reserve requirements** | The share of deposits banks must hold instead of lending. **The bluntest tool** | **Raise** them: banks can lend less | **Lower** them |

**Tight money → higher rates → real estate slows.** Easy money works the other way.

### Discount points

**Discount points** are **prepaid interest** paid **to the lender at closing**.

- **1 point = 1% of the loan amount**, never of the price (manual; not yet verified, V-26).
- **Course rule of thumb:** each point raises the lender's **yield** (its effective rate of return) by about **1/8%**, which is **0.125%** (manual; not yet verified, V-26).

**Why do points raise the yield?** Suppose a lender charges 2 points on a $315,000 loan. It hands over $315,000 but receives $6,300 back at closing, so it has really advanced only $308,700 while collecting payments on $315,000. Its return is therefore higher than the note rate. Seen from the borrower's side, paying points is how a borrower can get a lower note rate while the lender still earns its target yield.

**Three formulas**

1. **Cost of points** = loan amount × number of points × 1%
2. **Points needed** = yield increase wanted ÷ 0.125%
3. **Approximate yield** = note rate + (number of points × 0.125%)

**Eighths to decimals:** 1/8 = 0.125 · 2/8 = 1/4 = 0.25 · 3/8 = 0.375 · 4/8 = 1/2 = 0.50.

**Try it**

1. A buyer pays $480,000 for a Fort Lauderdale home with 20% down. The lender charges 1.5 points. What do the points cost?

## 5. Practical example

Daniel is buying a $350,000 condo in Orlando with 10% down ($35,000), so his loan is **$315,000**.

1. **Who takes his application?** Daniel works with a **mortgage broker**, who shops several lenders but lends nothing. The loan is funded by a **mortgage banker**, which also **services** it. Both are **primary market**. Because the loan is conventional and the LTV is 90%, he will also pay PMI (L13-1; PMI above 80% LTV: manual; not yet verified, V-26).
2. **The rate offer.** The lender quotes **6.75% with no points** or **6.50% with 2 points**. The cost of the points is $315,000 × 2% = **$6,300**, paid at closing. The offer matches the rule of thumb: 2 points × 0.125% = 0.25%, so 6.50% plus 2 points gives the lender roughly the same yield as 6.75% with no points.
3. **After closing.** The mortgage banker sells Daniel's loan to **Fannie Mae** (secondary market). Fannie Mae pools it with other loans into mortgage-backed securities. The mortgage banker gets its money back and lends it to the next Orlando buyer. The loan's terms were fixed at closing; the sale only moves the loan to a new owner.
4. **Six months later.** The Fed **sells** government securities in the open market. Money leaves the banks, credit tightens and mortgage rates rise. Fewer buyers qualify, and your Orlando listings take longer to sell.

- The secondary market exists to **refill lenders' money** so they can lend again.
- The Fed changes credit conditions; it does not set mortgage rates or make loans.

- Points are based on the **loan**, so the down payment matters: find the loan first.

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"A first-time buyer applied for a loan at Freddie Mac."** Impossible. Freddie Mac and Fannie Mae are secondary-market buyers of existing loans; consumers borrow in the primary market.
- **"Ginnie Mae buys conventional loans."** No. In this course Ginnie Mae **guarantees** securities backed by **FHA/VA** loans.
- **"A mortgage broker lends money."** No. A broker **arranges** loans and lends nothing; a **mortgage banker** lends.
- **"The Fed sets mortgage rates."** No. It influences credit through its three tools.
- **"Reserve requirements are the Fed's everyday tool."** No. **Open-market operations** are the day-to-day tool; reserve requirements are the bluntest.
- **"When the Fed sells securities, credit gets easier."** No. Selling securities takes money out of the banks and **tightens** credit.
- **"Disintermediation means more money for mortgages."** No. It means depositors pull money out, so there is **less**.
- **"Points are a percentage of the price."** No. **1 point = 1% of the loan.**
- **"1 point = 1/8%."** Do not mix the two numbers. One point **costs** 1% of the loan and **raises yield** by about 1/8%.

- **Secondary** = loans are bought: Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac **buy** + issue MBS; Ginnie Mae **guarantees** FHA/VA-backed MBS.
