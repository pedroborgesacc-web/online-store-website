## 2. Why it matters

Every Florida owner pays property tax every year, and buyers will keep asking you, "What will my taxes be?" To answer responsibly you need to know who sets the value, on what date, when the bill arrives, how the early-payment discount works and what happens when the tax is not paid. The calendar also matters in a sale: the January 1 value, the August TRIM notice and the November bill shape what a buyer can expect and what gets prorated at closing.

On the exam, Section 18 (Taxes Affecting Real Estate) carries 3% of the weight (V-02). Expect quick recall questions: the assessment date, the discount for paying in a given month, the delinquency date, what the TRIM notice is, who hears appeals, and the difference between an ad valorem tax and a special assessment.

## 3. Simple explanation

*Labels used here: V-nn = checked against official sources on 2026-10-19/20. "(manual; not yet verified)" = taken only from the study manual.*

### What is being taxed

*Ad valorem* means "according to value." A Florida real property tax is an ad valorem tax: the higher the taxable value, the higher the tax. Property taxes fund local government, and Florida has no state property tax (manual; not yet verified).

Three local players share the work:

| Who | Role |
|---|---|
| County **property appraiser** | Sets each parcel's **just value** (Florida's term for market value) as of January 1, certifies the values, mails the TRIM notice |
| Local **taxing authorities** (county, city, schools and others) | Each sets its own tax rate, called **millage** |
| County **tax collector** | Receives the certified tax roll, sends the bills, collects payment |

### The yearly cycle, step by step

**1. January 1: the snapshot.** Real property is assessed according to its status and value as of **January 1** each year (s. 192.042(1); V-76). Improvements that are not substantially completed on January 1 get no value for that year. Why one fixed date? Every parcel in the county is measured at the same moment, so values are comparable and the roll can be ready for the autumn bills.

**2. Summer: certification and the TRIM notice.** The appraiser certifies the values (usually July 1). Within **55 days after certification**, the appraiser must mail the Notice of Proposed Property Taxes, known as the **TRIM (Truth in Millage) notice** (s. 200.065(2)(b); V-76). With the usual July 1 certification, the Florida Department of Revenue (DOR) calendar sets the deadline at **August 24**, which is why the exam says "TRIM in August". A state of emergency allows a 30-day extension. The notice shows the proposed value and the proposed rates. It is information, **not a bill**. (The timing rule is in s. 200.065(2)(b); s. 200.069 sets what the notice contains. The manual cites s. 200.069 for the timing.)

**3. Disagreeing with the value.** An owner who believes the value is wrong can first talk informally with the property appraiser and can then petition the county **Value Adjustment Board (VAB)**, the board that hears assessment appeals (manual; not yet verified). This course has no verified VAB filing deadline, so do not guess one on the exam.

**4. November 1: the bill and the discounts.** Taxes are due and payable **November 1**, or as soon afterwards as the tax collector receives the certified roll (s. 197.333; V-76). Paying early earns a discount (s. 197.162; V-76):

| Paid in | Discount |
|---|---|
| November (or within 30 days after the original tax notice is sent) | 4% |
| December | 3% |
| January | 2% |
| February | 1% |
| March | 0% (full amount) |

If a discount period ends on a weekend or legal holiday, it extends to the next working day. The discount rewards owners who pay early, so local governments receive the money sooner.

**5. April 1: delinquency.** Unpaid taxes become **delinquent on April 1** of the year after the assessment year, **or 60 days after the original tax notice was mailed, whichever is later** (s. 197.333; V-76). The 60-day alternative protects owners when bills go out late.

**6. After delinquency: tax certificates and tax deeds.** Delinquent taxes are enforced through **tax certificates** and, eventually, a **tax deed sale** (manual; not yet verified). A tax certificate is a lien sold to an investor to collect the delinquent tax (manual; not yet verified). Its role: the local government collects the money from the investor instead of waiting for the owner, while the unpaid tax stays attached to the property as a lien. If the owner never pays, the process can end with the property itself being sold by tax deed. Homestead status does not stop this: Florida's constitutional homestead protection from creditors gives no protection against property taxes and assessments (V-66).

### Ad valorem tax vs special assessment

A **special assessment** is not an ad valorem tax (manual; not yet verified). It is a charge on the properties **specially benefited** by a public improvement, such as a new sewer line or street paving, and it is often calculated per **front foot** (each foot of the lot's frontage along the improvement; a foot is a little under a third of a metre). It is often a one-time charge, and it has lien priority like property taxes (all manual; not yet verified).

| | Ad valorem property tax | Special assessment |
|---|---|---|
| Based on | Taxable value | Benefit from a specific improvement (often front footage) |
| Who pays | Every taxable parcel | Only the benefited parcels |
| How often | Every year | Often one-time |
| Status | V-76 (calendar) | manual; not yet verified |

Both are claims that homestead protection does not defeat (V-66: no protection against property taxes and assessments).

### Watch item: a ballot measure that is not law (V-23)

A homestead property-tax amendment, **CS/HJR 1-F** (2026 Special Session F), is on the **November 3, 2026** ballot. It needs at least 60% of the vote; if approved, it takes effect January 1, 2027. Until then it is **not law** and it is not exam content. Exam questions usually lag law changes. (Its Save Our Homes effect is covered in L18-2.)

## 5. Practical example

**Carlos's house in Tampa (Hillsborough County), tax year 2026**

- **January 1, 2026.** Carlos's pool is half built, not substantially completed. The house is valued as it stood that day, and the pool gets **no value for 2026**. If it is substantially completed by January 1, 2027, it will count in the 2027 value.
- **August 2026.** His TRIM notice shows a proposed just value of $455,000. Two similar houses nearby sold for less, so he calls the property appraiser's office. If the informal talk does not resolve it, he can petition the VAB (manual; not yet verified). The TRIM notice is not a bill, so nothing is payable yet.
- **November 2026.** The final bill is $6,000. What he pays depends on when he pays:

| Pays in | Discount | Amount |
|---|---|---|
| November 2026 | 4% | $6,000 × 0.96 = **$5,760** |
| December 2026 | 3% | $6,000 × 0.97 = **$5,820** |
| January 2027 | 2% | $6,000 × 0.98 = **$5,880** |
| February 2027 | 1% | $6,000 × 0.99 = **$5,940** |
| March 2027 | 0% | **$6,000** |

- **April 1, 2027.** If he has not paid, the tax is delinquent. (His bill was mailed in November, and 60 days after that falls before April 1, so April 1 controls. If a bill were first mailed on February 15, 2027, delinquency would wait until 60 days after mailing, in mid-April.)
- **Same year, a special assessment.** The city extends sewer lines down his street and charges benefited lots $40 per front foot. His lot has 85 feet of frontage: 85 × $40 = **$3,400**. This charge is based on the benefit and frontage, not on his home's value (manual; not yet verified).
- **2027, Carlos sells.** His buyer must receive the property tax disclosure summary at or before the contract. It warns the buyer not to rely on the seller's current taxes, because a sale or improvements can raise the assessment (s. 689.261; V-75). As the licensee, never quote Carlos's bill as "the buyer's taxes".

## 6. Portugal comparison

**Familiar principle.** Portugal also has an annual local tax on property: **IMI** is an annual municipal tax on the registered taxable value (VPT) (PT-10; the course still marks the Portuguese side of this comparison "requires verification"). IMI rates, exemptions, payment dates and appeal procedures require verification.

**Florida rule.** Florida's property tax is ad valorem: the county property appraiser sets just value as of January 1 every year, the taxing authorities set millage, the TRIM notice announces proposed figures (usually August), bills go out from November 1 with 4/3/2/1% discounts, and unpaid taxes are delinquent April 1 (or 60 days after mailing, if later).

**Precise difference.** Florida's base is a just value set as of January 1 each year, and the tax is computed in mills on taxable value after exemptions; school and non-school taxable values can differ (L18-2). Florida's calendar (January 1, TRIM, November discounts, April 1) is set by Florida statutes. Any similarity to IMI's timing requires verification.

**Exam implication.** Answer every calendar question with the Florida dates above. Do not carry over IMI habits about when or how property tax is paid.

## 7. Legal and numerical requirements

**Memorize exactly**

- Assessment date: **January 1** (status and value). Improvements not substantially completed on January 1 get no value that year (s. 192.042(1); V-76).
- TRIM notice: mailed within **55 days after certification** (s. 200.065(2)(b)); usually **August** (DOR deadline August 24 with July 1 certification); +30 days in a state of emergency. Content: s. 200.069 (V-76).
- Appeal: informal conference with the appraiser, then petition to the **VAB** (manual; not yet verified).
- Due: **November 1**, or when the tax collector receives the certified roll (s. 197.333; V-76).
- Discounts: **4%** November (or within 30 days after the original notice is sent) · **3%** December · **2%** January · **1%** February · **0%** March; a period ending on a weekend or holiday runs to the next working day (s. 197.162; V-76).
- Delinquent: **April 1** of the following year, or **60 days** after the original notice was mailed, **whichever is later** (s. 197.333; V-76).
- Enforcement: tax certificates, then eventually a tax deed sale (manual; not yet verified). Homestead protection does not cover property taxes and assessments (V-66).
- Special assessment: not ad valorem; specially benefited properties; often per front foot; lien priority like property taxes (manual; not yet verified).
- No state property tax; property taxes fund local government (manual; not yet verified).
- CS/HJR 1-F (November 3, 2026 ballot; 60% needed; would take effect January 1, 2027): **not law** (V-23).

**Understand**

- January 1 fixes both the value and the status of the property for the whole year.
- The TRIM notice is a warning, not a bill: it is the owner's chance to react before the bills are final.
- The discount shrinks by one point per month, November to February, and disappears in March.
- An ad valorem tax follows value; a special assessment follows benefit.
- Tax certificates let the local government collect now; the lien stays on the property.

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"The property is valued on the day the bill is sent."** No. It is valued as of January 1.
- **"The TRIM notice is the tax bill."** No. It is a notice of proposed taxes, usually in August. Bills come from November 1.
- **"The 4% discount is for paying in October."** No. 4% applies in November, or within 30 days after the original notice is sent.
- **"Taxes are delinquent on January 1 (or March 1)."** No. They become delinquent on April 1, or 60 days after mailing if that is later.
- **"The TRIM deadline is in s. 200.069."** The timing is in s. 200.065(2)(b); s. 200.069 covers the notice's content.
- **"A special assessment is just another ad valorem tax."** No. It is based on the benefit from an improvement, often per front foot, not on value.
- **"A homestead cannot be lost for unpaid taxes."** Homestead protection gives no protection against property taxes and assessments (V-66).
- **"The November 2026 amendment has already changed the rules."** No. CS/HJR 1-F is pending law until approved, and it is not exam content (V-23).

## 2. Why it matters

**In practice.** Buyers ask "What will my taxes be?", and in Florida the honest answer is often "more than the seller pays": Save Our Homes may have held a long-time owner's assessed value far below market value. You must explain how a bill is built (value, exemptions, millage), make a rough estimate, and warn buyers not to rely on the seller's current taxes, as the property tax disclosure summary for residential buyers does (V-75).

**On the exam.** Section 18 is 3% of the exam (V-02), about 3 of 100 questions (V-01). This lesson supplies its math question and its traps: which taxes the second homestead exemption reduces, the current widow/blind/disabled amount, "3% or CPI, whichever is **less**", and a tax in mills with separate school and non-school values.

## 3. Simple explanation

*Labels: V-nn = checked against official sources on 2026-10-19/20. "(manual; not yet verified)" = from the study manual only. Values, millage rates and CPI changes in the examples are invented for practice.*

### Step 1: What it represents

A Florida property tax bill is built in layers:

| Layer | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Just value** | Market value, set by the county property appraiser as of January 1 (V-76; L18-1) |
| **Assessed value** | Where the tax starts. A homestead's can sit below just value, because Save Our Homes limits its growth (V-67) |
| **Exemptions** | Dollar amounts subtracted from assessed value, for owners who qualify |
| **Taxable value** | Assessed value − exemptions. A homestead has **two**: school and non-school |
| **Millage** | Each taxing authority's rate in mills (school board, county, city and others) |

**Homestead exemptions (s. 196.031; V-09).** The owner must own the property and occupy it as a permanent residence on **January 1** (N-140).

- The first **$25,000** of assessed value is exempt from **all** taxes, school taxes included.
- An **additional exemption of up to $25,000** applies to assessed value **above $50,000**, for **non-school taxes only**. Since **January 1, 2025** (Amendment 5, approved November 5, 2024) it is **indexed to CPI-U** (the US Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers, an inflation measure; see Step 2) when the change is positive: **$25,722 for 2025** (Florida DOR, 2.9% CPI). **Exam math usually uses the round $25,000.** Older books say "value between $50,000 and $75,000"; with indexing the top of that band moves ($75,722 for 2025).

**Why two taxable values?** The additional exemption does not apply to school levies, so school taxes are computed on a base that is normally $25,000 larger.

**Other exemptions**

- **$5,000** each for a widow or widower, a blind person, or a totally and permanently disabled person who is a Florida resident (s. 196.202; V-10). Raised from $500 by the 2022 Legislature, first for the 2023 tax roll: **$500 is outdated**, though older printings still show it.
- Disabled veterans (combat-disabled, or service-connected and totally disabled): larger or total exemptions; details not covered (manual; not yet verified).
- **Green Belt:** agricultural land is assessed on **use value** (its value as farmland), not market value (manual; not yet verified).

**Save Our Homes (SOH)** (Art. VII, s. 4(d), Fla. Const.; s. 193.155; V-67). After the January 1 reassessment, a homestead's assessed value may rise by no more than **3% of the prior year's assessed value or the percentage change in the CPI for the preceding calendar year, whichever is lower**. Why: an owner who stays should not be taxed out of the home by a hot market. Just value − assessed value = the **accrued SOH benefit**.

- **Reset:** on a change of ownership, assessed value resets to just value (N-142).
- **Portability:** an owner who sets up a new Florida homestead may transfer the benefit, **up to $500,000**, if the new homestead is established **within 3 years after January 1 of the year the old one was abandoned** and the owner had a homestead exemption on January 1 of any of the 3 preceding years; claimed on **Form DR-501T** with the homestead application (V-67). The 3-year window replaced 2 years from 2021: "2 years" is **outdated**.
- **Pending, not law (V-23; V-67):** CS/HJR 1-F (2026 Special Session F) is on the **November 3, 2026** ballot. If at least 60% of voters approve, from January 1, 2027 it would, among other things, let the **full** accrued benefit transfer for non-school levies (no $500,000 cap for those levies). Until then the 3%/CPI cap and the $500,000 cap remain current. A 2026 House analysis of HB 7031 mentions possible changes to which prior homestead can be ported from; enactment status not verified. Exam questions usually lag law changes.

### Step 2: Units

- **Dollars**, in US notation: $1,710.50 (comma for thousands, point for decimals).
- **Mill** = one-thousandth. **1 mill = $1 of tax per $1,000 of taxable value = 0.001** (manual; not yet verified; N-144). It works like per mille (‰), not like percent.

| Millage | Tax per $1,000 | Decimal | Percent |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 mill | $1.00 | 0.001 | 0.1% |
| 6 mills | $6.00 | 0.006 | 0.6% |
| 18.4 mills | $18.40 | 0.0184 | 1.84% |

- **Percent** for SOH (3% = 0.03). The CPI is the Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers, U.S. City Average (V-67).

### Step 3: Formula

1. **SOH maximum** = last year's assessed value × (1 + the **lower** of 3% or the CPI change).
2. **Additional exemption** = the smaller of $25,000 (or the indexed figure) and (assessed value − $50,000); zero at $50,000 or less.
3. **School taxable value** = assessed value − $25,000 (− any other exemption the question applies).
4. **Non-school taxable value** = assessed value − $25,000 − additional exemption (− any other exemption the question applies).
5. **Tax** = taxable value × mills ÷ 1,000, for each part; then add.

**Shortcut:** taxable value in thousands × mills = tax (285 × 6 = $1,710). With round figures and an assessed value of $75,000 or more: school taxable = assessed − $25,000; non-school taxable = assessed − $50,000.

### Step 4: When it applies

| Situation | What to do |
|---|---|
| Homestead | Two taxable values, as above |
| Not a homestead (rental, second home, vacant lot, store) | No homestead exemption, no SOH: assessed value × total millage (unless the question gives another exemption) |
| Widow/widower, blind, totally and permanently disabled | Also subtract $5,000. V-10 confirms the amount, not how each levy treats it: apply it as the question states |
| Last year's assessed value + market and CPI changes given | SOH first, then exemptions |
| Property sold | Reset to just value (N-142) at the next January 1 assessment (V-76); the buyer must qualify for homestead on their own (N-140) |
| Question gives $25,722 | Use it; otherwise $25,000 |

### Step 5: Worked example

**A. The manual's example.** A homestead is assessed at **$310,000**. School millage 6 mills; county plus city 9 mills.

| | School | Non-school |
|---|---|---|
| Assessed value | $310,000 | $310,000 |
| − first exemption | − $25,000 | − $25,000 |
| − additional exemption | does not apply | − $25,000 |
| **Taxable value** | **$285,000** | **$260,000** |
| × mills ÷ 1,000 | × 6 | × 9 |
| **Tax** | **$1,710** | **$2,340** |

Total **$4,050**. Second check with decimals: $285,000 × 0.006 = $1,710; $260,000 × 0.009 = $2,340 ✓.

With the indexed 2025 figure, non-school taxable is $310,000 − $25,000 − $25,722 = $259,278 → $2,333.50; total $4,043.50. Only $6.50 apart, so questions use $25,000 unless they give the indexed figure.

**B. The next year, with Save Our Homes.** Market value rose 8%; the CPI changed 2.4%. Cap = lower of 3% and 2.4% = **2.4%** (ignore the 8%). Maximum assessed value = $310,000 × 1.024 = **$317,440** (check: $310,000 × 0.024 = $7,440 ✓). Same millage: school $292,440 × 6 ÷ 1,000 = $1,754.64; non-school $267,440 × 9 ÷ 1,000 = $2,406.96; total **$4,161.60**.

### Step 6: Reasonableness check

- **School taxable ≥ non-school taxable**; at assessed values of $75,000 or more the gap is exactly $25,000 (round figures).
- **About $1 per $1,000 per mill:** $300,000 at 15 mills ≈ $4,500. Getting $45,000 means you divided by 100.
- **SOH adds at most $3 per $100** of last year's assessed value; more means you used the market rise or the higher rate.
- **An exemption saves exemption × mills ÷ 1,000:** $5,000 at 15 mills = $75.

### Step 7: Common errors

| Error | Correction |
|---|---|
| Taking $50,000 off school taxes | School gets only the first $25,000 |
| Full second exemption on a cheap home (assessed $62,000 → $25,000 off) | Only value above $50,000 counts: $12,000 |
| Dividing mills by 100 | Mills are per **1,000** |
| "Whichever is greater" (CPI 4% → 4%) | The **lower** one: 3% |
| Applying the cap to market value or to this year's value | Apply it to **last year's assessed value** |
| Subtracting $500 for a widow | $5,000 (V-10) |
| Estimating the buyer's tax on the seller's capped value | Resets to just value after a sale (N-142) |

### Step 10: Exam application

1. Homestead or not? SOH involved? Which exemption figures are given?
2. SOH first: the lower of 3% or CPI, on last year's assessed value.
3. Exemptions: $25,000 from both values; up to $25,000 more from non-school only; $5,000 if the question includes it.
4. Each taxable value × its mills ÷ 1,000; add. Round only at the end, to cents.

Wrong choices are built from the Step 7 errors. The annual tax is also what gets prorated at closing (L14-7): use the question's method, otherwise the course convention of a 365-day year with the buyer owning the closing day (V-79: unresolved; a course convention, not law).

## 5. Practical example

**The Moreira house in Sarasota.** Homestead since 2010. On January 1, 2026: just value **$540,000**, assessed value **$275,000**. Millage: school 5.9, non-school 9.6. In June 2026 Daniel buys it and moves in. Assume a January 1, 2027 just value of $530,000, unchanged millage, and his own homestead exemption. Use the round $25,000 exemption figures.

| | Moreira 2026 bill | Daniel's 2027 estimate |
|---|---|---|
| Assessed value | $275,000 | $530,000 (reset to just value) |
| School | $250,000 × 5.9 ÷ 1,000 = $1,475.00 | $505,000 × 5.9 ÷ 1,000 = $2,979.50 |
| Non-school | $225,000 × 9.6 ÷ 1,000 = $2,160.00 | $480,000 × 9.6 ÷ 1,000 = $4,608.00 |
| **Total** | **$3,635.00** ($3,489.60 if paid in November with the 4% discount) | **$7,587.50** |

- The 2026 bill reflects the Moreiras' January 1, 2026 status and value (V-76) and is prorated at closing (L14-7).
- From 2027 the assessed value resets (N-142): Daniel's bill could be **more than twice** theirs even with his own homestead exemption, which is what his property tax disclosure summary warns (V-75). As a rental, with no homestead exemption: 530 × 15.5 = **$8,215**.
- The Moreiras move to a smaller home in Venice. Their benefit, $540,000 − $275,000 = **$265,000**, is under the $500,000 cap. Abandoning the old homestead in 2026, they must establish the new one within 3 years after January 1, 2026 and file Form DR-501T (V-67).

## 6. Portugal comparison

**Familiar principle.** Portugal also taxes property every year: **IMI** is an annual municipal tax on the registered taxable value (VPT) (PT-10; the course still marks the Portuguese side of this comparison "requires verification"). IMI rates and exemptions require verification.

**Florida rule.** The property appraiser sets just value as of January 1; taxing authorities set millage. Homesteads get the exemptions ($25,000 + up to $25,000 non-school) and the Save Our Homes cap (3% or CPI).

**Precise difference.** Florida computes the tax **in mills on taxable value after exemptions**, and school and non-school values can differ: a homestead assessed at $300,000 has school taxable $275,000 and non-school taxable $250,000 (round figures), because the additional exemption applies only to non-school levies (PT-10).

**Exam implication.** 1 mill = $1 per $1,000; run school and non-school separately when exemptions differ. Never answer a Florida question from IMI habits.

## 7. Legal and numerical requirements

**Memorize exactly**

- Taxable value = assessed − exemptions. Tax = taxable value × mills ÷ 1,000. 1 mill = $1 per $1,000 = 0.001 (manual; not yet verified; N-144).
- Homestead: **$25,000** off **all** taxes + up to **$25,000** off **non-school** taxes on assessed value **above $50,000** (s. 196.031; V-09); CPI-indexed since 2025 (**$25,722** for 2025); exam math $25,000. Own and occupy as a permanent residence on January 1 (N-140).
- **$5,000** widow/widower, blind, totally and permanently disabled; Florida resident (s. 196.202; V-10). **$500 = outdated** (before the 2023 roll).
- Disabled veterans: larger or total exemptions. Green Belt: use value (manual; not yet verified).
- SOH: increase ≤ **3%** of prior assessed value or the **CPI** change, **whichever is lower** (Art. VII, s. 4(d); s. 193.155; V-67). Resets to just value on a change of ownership (N-142).
- Portability: up to **$500,000**; within **3 years** after January 1 of the year the old homestead was abandoned (**2 years = outdated**, before 2021); exemption held on January 1 of any of the 3 preceding years; Form **DR-501T** (V-67).
- CS/HJR 1-F (November 3, 2026 ballot; 60%; would apply from January 1, 2027): **not law** (V-23). HB 7031: status not verified.

**Understand**

- Exemptions reduce the value, never the tax directly.
- School taxable value is the larger one: the second exemption skips school levies.
- SOH caps assessed-value growth, not millage: a higher millage can still raise the bill.
- SOH protects the owner who stays; a sale resets it; portability lets the owner take part of it along.

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"Homestead exempts the first $50,000 from all taxes."** No. $25,000 from all taxes; the second exemption covers value above $50,000, non-school only. Value from $25,000 to $50,000 gets no homestead exemption.
- **"The widow's exemption is $500."** Outdated; it is $5,000 (V-10).
- **"SOH is 3% or CPI, whichever is greater."** Whichever is **less**.
- **"SOH caps the tax bill."** It caps assessed-value growth; millage can still rise.
- **"The buyer keeps the seller's low assessed value."** It resets to just value (N-142).
- **"Portability has no limit."** Up to $500,000, within 3 years. The November 2026 measure would change this for non-school levies only if approved (V-23).
- **"A rental gets Save Our Homes."** No. SOH and the homestead exemption are for homesteads.

## 2. Why it matters

**In practice.** Income tax drives many sale decisions. An investor wonders whether to sell a fourplex outright or exchange it; a couple asks whether the profit on their home is taxable. You are not a tax adviser, and a client's own figures belong with a CPA or tax attorney, but you must understand the vocabulary well enough to see what is at stake and when to refer.

**On the exam.** Section 18 (Taxes Affecting Real Estate) is 3% of the exam (V-02). Questions here are mostly conceptual: 27.5 versus 39 years, land never depreciated, the effect of boot in a 1031 exchange, the $250,000/$500,000 home-sale exclusion with its 2-of-5-years test, and a short adjusted-basis and gain calculation.

## 3. Simple explanation

*Labels: V-nn = checked against official sources on 2026-10-19/20. "(manual; not yet verified)" = from the study manual only. These are **US federal** income tax rules (Internal Revenue Code, IRS publications), not Florida statutes. Figures in the examples are invented for practice.*

### Building block 1: basis

**Basis** is, in general, what the owner paid for the property. **Adjusted basis** = cost + capital improvements − depreciation taken (manual; not yet verified). Basis is the yardstick the sale is measured against:

- An **improvement** (a new roof, an added bedroom) **raises** basis: the owner has put more money in.
- **Depreciation** **lowers** basis: the owner has already deducted that part of the cost from taxable income.

### Building block 2: depreciation (cost recovery)

Buildings wear out, so the tax law lets the owner of a rental or business building deduct the building's cost over a fixed period. This deduction is **depreciation**, also called **cost recovery** (26 U.S.C. 168; IRS Pub. 946; V-77):

| Property | Recovery period | Method |
|---|---|---|
| **Residential rental** property (a building where 80% or more of gross rental income comes from dwelling units) | **27.5 years** | Straight-line |
| **Nonresidential** real property (offices, stores, warehouses) | **39 years** | Straight-line |
| **Land** | **Never** depreciated | — |

**Why never land?** Land does not wear out, become obsolete or get used up (V-77). Only the building's cost goes into the depreciable basis, so the purchase price must first be split between land and building.

**Straight-line** means the same deduction every year: building cost ÷ recovery period. A Kissimmee rental house costs $360,000, of which $85,000 is land: $275,000 ÷ 27.5 = **$10,000 a year**. An office building's structure costs $780,000: $780,000 ÷ 39 = **$20,000 a year**.

Finer points (V-77): these are the general-system (GDS, under MACRS) periods; the alternative system (ADS) uses 30 and 40 years. Real tax returns apply the **mid-month convention**, which adjusts the first and last years; this course's calculations use full years. The 39-year period replaced **31.5 years** in 1993, so 31.5 is **outdated**.

**Why investors like it (tax shelter):** depreciation costs no cash in the year it is deducted, yet it reduces taxable income, so it shields part of the cash income from tax (manual; not yet verified). The bill comes later, at the sale.

### Building block 3: capital gain and recapture

**Capital gain** = amount realized − adjusted basis (manual; not yet verified). On the exam, the amount realized is the net sale price the question gives.

Because depreciation lowers the basis, it **raises** the gain. The depreciation taken over the years is **recaptured** (taxed) at the sale (manual; not yet verified; recapture rates are not covered).

Kissimmee, 6 years later: depreciation taken 6 × $10,000 = $60,000; no improvements. Adjusted basis $360,000 − $60,000 = $300,000. Net sale price $455,000 → gain **$155,000**. Without the depreciation the gain would be $95,000: the extra $60,000 is the depreciation coming back.

### Building block 4: exclude, defer or spread

| Tool | For what property | Effect on the tax |
|---|---|---|
| **Principal residence exclusion** (26 U.S.C. 121; V-78) | The seller's main home | Gain up to the limit is **excluded** (not taxed) |
| **1031 like-kind exchange** (manual; not yet verified) | Investment or business real property | Tax **deferred** (postponed), except on boot |
| **Installment sale** (manual; not yet verified) | A sale paid over time | Tax **spread** as payments arrive |

**Principal residence exclusion (V-78).** A seller can exclude up to **$250,000** of gain, or **$500,000** for married filing jointly (a married couple filing one joint federal tax return), if they **owned and used** the home as their principal residence for **at least 2 of the 5 years** before the sale: 24 full months or 730 days, and the periods need not be continuous. Qualifications:

1. Generally not available if the seller excluded gain on another home sold within the **2 years** before this sale.
2. For the $500,000 joint amount, at least one spouse meets the ownership test and **both** meet the use test and the 2-year look-back rule; otherwise each spouse is tested separately.
3. A **partial** exclusion may apply if the tests are failed for qualifying reasons (work, health, unforeseen circumstances).
4. Gain equal to depreciation claimed or allowable after May 6, 1997 cannot be excluded.
5. A surviving spouse may use $500,000 if the sale is within 2 years of the spouse's death and other conditions are met.

Source note: confirmed from IRS Publication 523 (2025) and Tax Topic 701; the statute text and any 2026 change were not checked directly (V-78).

**1031 like-kind exchange (manual; not yet verified).** An owner who exchanges **investment or business real property** for **like-kind** property **defers** the tax on the gain: postponed, not forgiven. A family home is not investment property, so it takes the exclusion, never a 1031. **Boot** is any value received that is not like-kind: **cash, debt relief, personal property**. Boot is **taxable to the extent of gain** (taxed, but never more than the gain), and it does **not** disqualify the exchange; the like-kind part stays deferred. Other technical requirements of an exchange are beyond this course.

**Installment sale (manual; not yet verified).** When the seller receives the price over several years (for example, through seller financing), the gain is recognized proportionally as payments are received, so the tax is spread over those years. It is not avoided.

## 4. Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Basis | In general, the cost of the property |
| Adjusted basis | Cost + improvements − depreciation |
| Amount realized | What the seller receives from the sale (on the exam, the net sale price given) |
| Capital gain | Amount realized − adjusted basis |
| Depreciation (cost recovery) | Yearly deduction of a building's cost: 27.5 years residential rental, 39 years nonresidential, never land |
| Straight-line | The same deduction every year |
| Recapture | Taxing, at the sale, the depreciation taken earlier |
| 1031 exchange | Tax-deferred exchange of like-kind investment or business real property |
| Boot | Non-like-kind value received in an exchange (cash, debt relief, personal property); taxable to the extent of gain |
| Principal residence exclusion | Up to $250,000 single / $500,000 married filing jointly of home-sale gain not taxed; 2-of-5-years test |
| Installment sale | Sale paid over time; gain reported as payments are received |

## 5. Practical example

**Rita, the investor.** Rita bought a fourplex in St. Petersburg for **$640,000**: $145,000 land and $495,000 building. All of its rent comes from its four dwelling units, so it is residential rental property (80% or more from dwelling units; V-77): $495,000 ÷ 27.5 = **$18,000 a year**. She later added a new roof for **$40,000**. Her records show **$120,000** of total depreciation taken (building and roof).

| Step | Calculation | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Adjusted basis | $640,000 + $40,000 − $120,000 | **$560,000** |
| Amount realized | net sale price | $860,000 |
| Gain | $860,000 − $560,000 | **$300,000** |

Her options, in concept:

- **Sell outright.** The $300,000 gain is taxable; $120,000 of it comes from the depreciation taken, which is recaptured (manual; not yet verified).
- **1031 exchange** into an apartment building in Tampa (also investment property), receiving **$25,000 cash** as well. The $25,000 is boot and is taxable, since it is less than her gain; the other $275,000 of gain stays deferred, and the exchange is still valid. (Had her gain been only $15,000, the $25,000 of boot would be taxable only up to $15,000.)
- **Installment sale.** She finances the buyer and is paid over several years; the gain is taxed as the payments come in.

She asks which is best. You explain the concepts and refer her to her CPA.

**Paulo and Joana, the homeowners.** A married couple filing jointly bought their Jacksonville home 3 years ago for **$305,000**, added a **$35,000** pool, and have lived there ever since. No depreciation was taken. They sell for a net **$735,000**.

- Adjusted basis $305,000 + $35,000 = $340,000; gain $735,000 − $340,000 = **$395,000**.
- Both owned and lived in it at least 2 of the last 5 years, and neither excluded gain on another home sold in the previous 2 years: up to **$500,000** can be excluded, so the whole **$395,000** is excluded.
- If Paulo were single, the limit would be **$250,000**: $395,000 − $250,000 = **$145,000** taxable.
- A 1031 exchange is not available: this is their principal residence, not investment property.

## 7. Legal and numerical requirements

**Memorize exactly**

- Depreciation: **27.5 years** residential rental (80% or more of gross rents from dwelling units); **39 years** nonresidential; straight-line; mid-month convention; **land never** (26 U.S.C. 168; IRS Pub. 946; V-77). ADS: 30/40 years. 31.5 years = outdated (replaced in 1993).
- Exclusion: **$250,000** single / **$500,000** married filing jointly; owned and used as the principal residence **2 of the 5 years** before the sale (24 months or 730 days; need not be continuous); generally not if used on another home sold within the previous **2 years** (26 U.S.C. 121; IRS Pub. 523; V-78).
- Joint $500,000: one spouse meets ownership; both meet use and look-back. Partial exclusion for work, health or unforeseen circumstances. Depreciation after May 6, 1997 cannot be excluded. Surviving spouse: $500,000 if sold within 2 years of the death and other conditions are met (V-78).
- Adjusted basis = cost + improvements − depreciation. Gain = amount realized − adjusted basis. Depreciation is recaptured at sale (manual; not yet verified).
- 1031: investment or business real property, like-kind; tax **deferred**; boot (cash, debt relief, personal property) taxable to the extent of gain; boot does not disqualify (manual; not yet verified).
- Installment sale: gain recognized as payments are received (manual; not yet verified).

**Understand**

- Depreciation shelters income now but raises the gain later.
- Exclusion = gain not taxed (up to the limit); 1031 = tax postponed; installment sale = tax spread.
- The home takes the exclusion; the investment property takes the 1031. Do not cross them.

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"Land is depreciated with the building."** No. Land is never depreciated (V-77).
- **"An apartment building is depreciated over 39 years."** No. Residential rental is 27.5 years; 39 is nonresidential.
- **"Nonresidential is 31.5 years."** Outdated since 1993: 39 years.
- **"Depreciation lowers the gain at sale."** No. It lowers the basis, so the gain is larger.
- **"Receiving cash kills the 1031."** No. The cash is boot and is taxed; the exchange survives.
- **"A 1031 makes the gain tax-free."** No. It defers the tax.
- **"I can 1031 my family home."** No. The home uses the $250,000/$500,000 exclusion.
- **"The 2 years must be the last 2 years in a row."** No. 2 of the 5 years before the sale; need not be continuous.
- **"Every married couple gets $500,000."** Only married filing jointly, with at least one spouse meeting ownership and both meeting use and the 2-year look-back rule.
- **"An installment sale avoids the tax."** No. It spreads it.
