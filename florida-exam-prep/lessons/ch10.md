## 2. Why it matters

In daily brokerage work you identify a property by its street address. In Florida that is not enough for the documents that move ownership. A valid deed must contain a **legal description**: words that identify one parcel of land so precisely that it cannot be confused with any other (L09-2; manual; not yet verified). A written listing agreement must also contain a description of the property (s. 475.25(1)(r); V-49, verified). Agents usually take the legal description from the seller's recorded deed or title documents. A wrong lot number or a missing fraction can describe the wrong land, so copy it exactly.

On the exam, Section 10 "Legal Descriptions" carries about 5% of the questions (V-02), which is about five of the 100 questions (V-01). This lesson covers the concepts. The next lesson (L10-2) covers the arithmetic. Typical concept questions:

- Which system must begin and end at the same point?
- Which system describes most homes in platted urban subdivisions?
- Where do Florida's principal meridian and base line intersect?
- Is a street address a legal description? (Never.)

## 3. Simple explanation

### What a legal description is, and why an address is not one

A **legal description** identifies a specific parcel so that a surveyor could find its boundaries on the ground. Florida exam materials use **three systems**: **metes and bounds**, **lot and block**, and the **government (rectangular) survey** (manual; not yet verified).

A **street address is never a legal description** (manual; not yet verified). The reason: an address tells a mail carrier which door to go to, but it says nothing about where the boundary lines are. Streets can be renamed and numbers can change. One address can cover several parcels, and vacant land often has no address at all.

### System 1: Metes and bounds

"Metes" means measurements and "bounds" means boundaries. The description "walks" the boundary of the parcel:

1. It starts at a **point of beginning (POB)**.
2. It follows **courses** (directions, such as "North" or "North 45 degrees East") and **distances** (in feet) from one corner to the next.
3. It **returns to the POB**.

Why must it return to the POB? A boundary line that does not close back on itself does not enclose any land. The return to the POB is what turns a list of lines into a parcel. Metes and bounds is **used for irregular parcels**, whose shape does not fit a grid or a subdivision map (manual; not yet verified).

### System 2: Lot and block

When land is divided into a subdivision, a **plat** is prepared: a map of the subdivision showing its blocks, lots and streets. The plat is then **recorded** in the public records. From that moment each lot can be described simply by its lot number, its block number, the subdivision name and a reference to the recorded plat. Lot and block is the **usual description in platted urban subdivisions**, so a typical subdivision home is described this way (manual; not yet verified).

The condition that matters is a **recorded plat**. Without a recorded plat, "Lot 14, Block C" points to nothing.

### System 3: Government (rectangular) survey

Imagine a giant grid laid over the land, measured from two reference lines:

- a **principal meridian**, a reference line running north–south;
- a **base line**, a reference line running east–west.

**Florida's principal meridian and base line intersect in Tallahassee** (N-105; manual; not yet verified, V-26). From these lines the land is divided into **townships** of 6 × 6 miles, and each township into **36 sections** of 1 square mile (640 acres) (N-100, N-101; manual; not yet verified, V-26). A government survey description names a fraction of a section, the section number and its township. L10-2 teaches the numbering and the arithmetic.

Real descriptions also say where the township lies relative to the two lines, for example "Township 12 South, Range 17 East": the township row counted south of the base line and the column (called a **range**) counted east of the principal meridian. *This township-and-range wording is not in the course sources (neither the study manual nor any verification entry): requires verification. None of this course's practice questions use it.*

### The three systems side by side

| | Metes and bounds | Lot and block | Government survey |
|---|---|---|---|
| Starting reference | A **point of beginning (POB)** | A **recorded plat** | A **principal meridian** and **base line** (Florida: Tallahassee) |
| How land is described | Courses and distances, **returning to the POB** | Lot number in a block on the recorded plat | Townships, sections and fractions of sections |
| Typical use | **Irregular parcels** | **Platted urban subdivisions** | Land described by sections (see L10-2) |
| Clue words in a question | "point of beginning", "thence North ... feet" | "Lot", "Block", "plat" | "Section", "Township", "NW¼" |

All rows: manual; not yet verified.

## 4. Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Legal description | Words that identify one specific parcel precisely enough to locate its boundaries. Required in a deed |
| Metes and bounds | Description using a point of beginning, courses (directions) and distances, returning to the POB |
| Point of beginning (POB) | The starting and ending point of a metes-and-bounds description |
| Course | A direction in a metes-and-bounds description, such as "North 45 degrees East" (face north, then turn 45 degrees toward east). Angles can include minutes: 36°52' means 36 degrees 52 minutes |
| Distance | The length of each boundary line, in feet |
| Thence | Old-fashioned English for "from that point". Metes-and-bounds descriptions use it to introduce the next course, as in "thence North 200 feet" |
| Lot and block | Description by lot and block numbers on a recorded plat |
| Plat | Recorded map of a subdivision showing its blocks, lots and streets |
| Recorded | Entered in the public records, so that anyone can find and rely on it |
| Government (rectangular) survey | System of townships and sections measured from principal meridians and base lines |
| Principal meridian | The north–south reference line of the government survey |
| Base line | The east–west reference line of the government survey |
| Township | A 6 × 6 mile square containing 36 sections |
| Section | 1 square mile, 640 acres |

## 5. Practical example

**Luís**, a buyer from Porto, asks you to help him buy three Florida properties. You look at the legal description in each seller's recorded deed.

| Property | Legal description (illustrative) | System |
|---|---|---|
| A house in an Orlando subdivision, "4127 Heron Pointe Drive" | "Lot 14, Block C, HERON POINTE, according to the plat thereof recorded in Plat Book 62, Page 115, of the Public Records of Orange County, Florida" | **Lot and block** |
| 40 acres of pasture in Levy County, no street address | "The SW¼ of the NE¼ of Section 22, Township 12 South, Range 17 East" | **Government survey** |
| An irregular lot on the St. Johns River | "Beginning at the point of beginning (POB) on the north line of River Road; thence North 200 feet; thence East 300 feet; thence South 36°52' East 250 feet along the river bank; thence West 450 feet along River Road back to the POB" | **Metes and bounds** |

The names, plat book and page numbers, and distances above are invented for illustration.

What you explain to Luís:

1. **The house** sits in a platted subdivision, so its description refers to the recorded plat. "4127 Heron Pointe Drive" is the mailing address only. It appears in marketing, but the deed must carry a legal description, and for this platted lot that is the lot-and-block description.
2. **The pasture** has no address at all. The government survey locates it within a section of a township measured from Florida's principal meridian and base line, which intersect in Tallahassee (N-105; manual; not yet verified, V-26). In L10-2 you will calculate that "the SW¼ of the NE¼" of a section is 40 acres.
3. **The riverfront lot** is irregular, so it is described by metes and bounds. Check that the description ends "back to the POB". If the last course does not return to the starting point, the boundary does not close and the parcel is not properly described. Ask the title company or a surveyor to review it.

## 6. Portugal comparison

**The familiar principle.** Any system that transfers land needs a way to identify exactly which land is transferred. How Portuguese documents identify a property is not covered by the course's comparison entries: *requires verification*. Do not rely on it for any Florida answer.

**The Florida rule.** The deed carries a legal description in one of three systems: metes and bounds, lot and block (recorded plat) or the government survey (Tallahassee origin). A street address never qualifies (manual; not yet verified).

**The precise difference that is verified for this course (PT-17).** Units. Portugal uses the metric system (m², hectares; 1 ha = 10,000 m²). Florida descriptions use US customary units: distances in **feet**, areas in **square feet** and **acres**, and **miles** for townships and sections. For intuition only: 1 acre ≈ 4,047 m² ≈ 0.405 ha, and 1 mile ≈ 1.61 km.

**Exam implication.** Answer with the three Florida system names and Florida's survey origin (Tallahassee). Read every distance in a metes-and-bounds description in feet. Exam math never uses metric (PT-17).

## 7. Legal and numerical requirements

**Memorize exactly**

- Three systems: **metes and bounds**, **lot and block**, **government (rectangular) survey** (manual; not yet verified).
- Metes and bounds: **POB → courses (directions) and distances → back to the POB**. Used for **irregular parcels** (manual; not yet verified).
- Lot and block: a lot number in a block on a **recorded plat**. The usual description in **platted urban subdivisions** (manual; not yet verified).
- Government survey: **townships and sections** measured from a **principal meridian** and **base line**. **Florida's intersect in Tallahassee** (N-105; manual; not yet verified, V-26).
- A **street address is never a legal description** (manual; not yet verified).
- A valid deed requires a legal description (L09-2; manual; not yet verified). A written listing agreement must include a property description (s. 475.25(1)(r); V-49, verified).

**Understand**

- Each system answers the same question, "which land, exactly?", from a different starting reference: a point (POB), a map (plat) or a grid (meridian and base line).
- Metes and bounds must close: without the return to the POB, no area is enclosed.
- Lot and block depends entirely on the recorded plat. The description is only as good as the map it points to.
- The government survey is a grid, so its parcels are described by section numbers and fractions. That is why it lends itself to arithmetic (L10-2).
- Source status: the exam format and weighting (V-01, V-02) and the listing-agreement rule (V-49) are verified; the metric figures are PT-17 arithmetic. ## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"The street address is enough for the deed."** No. A street address is never a legal description.
- **"Metes and bounds just has to list the boundary lines."** The lines must start at the POB and **return to the POB**. That is the classic exam answer to "which description begins and ends at the same point?"
- **"Lot and block works for any parcel."** Only for land shown on a **recorded plat**.
- **"Most subdivision homes are described by metes and bounds."** The usual description in platted urban subdivisions is **lot and block**. Metes and bounds is for irregular parcels.
- **"Florida's survey starts in Miami, Orlando or Jacksonville."** Florida's principal meridian and base line intersect in **Tallahassee**.
- **"I can read the distances in metres, as at home."** Florida descriptions use feet and acres (PT-17), and Portuguese practice never answers a Florida question.

## 2. Why it matters

Rural Florida land is often priced **per acre** and described by fractions of a section ("the NW¼ of the SE¼ of Section 22"). You must turn that description, or lot dimensions in feet, into acres and then into a price, all in US units, while your Portuguese clients think in hectares.

On the exam, Section 10 "Legal Descriptions" carries about 5% of the questions (V-02), and the study manual treats section-and-township arithmetic as near-certain exam material: "How many acres in ...?", "... AND ...", "What does it cost at $X per acre?", "Where is section 1?"

## 3. Simple explanation

### Step 1: What it represents

The government survey (L10-1) lays a grid over the land (all facts in this step: manual; not yet verified. The township and section figures are N-100 and N-101, listed under V-26):

- A **township** is **6 miles × 6 miles = 36 square miles**, divided into **36 sections** (N-100).
- A **section** is **1 mile × 1 mile = 1 square mile = 640 acres** (N-101).
- Sections are numbered in a **serpentine** pattern (back and forth, like a snake). **Section 1 is in the northeast corner**. Numbering runs **west** to section 6, then back **east** (7–12), west again (13–18), and so on, ending with **section 36 in the southeast** corner. **Section 16** was traditionally reserved for schools.

An **aliquot part** is a fraction of a section named by direction: halves (N½, S½, E½, W½) and quarters (NE¼, NW¼, SE¼, SW¼). Fractions can be nested: "the NW¼ **of** the SE¼" is a quarter of a quarter. One section divided into quarter-quarters:

| ← West | | | East → |
|:-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|
| NW¼ of NW¼ | NE¼ of NW¼ | NW¼ of NE¼ | NE¼ of NE¼ |
| SW¼ of NW¼ | SE¼ of NW¼ | SW¼ of NE¼ | SE¼ of NE¼ |
| NW¼ of SW¼ | NE¼ of SW¼ | **NW¼ of SE¼** | NE¼ of SE¼ |
| SW¼ of SW¼ | SE¼ of SW¼ | SW¼ of SE¼ | SE¼ of SE¼ |

<p class="caption">North is up. Each square is 1,320 ft × 1,320 ft = 40 acres. Each 2 × 2 corner block is a 160-acre quarter section.</p>

**To locate a parcel, read the fractions right to left.** "The NW¼ of the SE¼": first find the SE¼ of the section (the bottom-right block), then take its NW¼ (the highlighted square).

### Step 2: Units

| Unit | Equals | Measures | Memory aid |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 mile | **5,280 feet** | Length | "Five tomatoes": 5-2-80 |
| 1 acre | **43,560 square feet** | Area | 43-5-60 |
| 1 square yard | **9 square feet** (3 ft × 3 ft) | Area | 3 × 3, not 3 |
| 1 square mile | **640 acres** | Area | 640 in a section |
| 1 section | 1 square mile = 640 acres | Area | |
| 1 township | 36 sections = 36 square miles | Area | 6 × 6 |

(Manual; not yet verified, V-26. These are standard measurement definitions, but the course has not yet checked them against an official source.)

Feet and miles measure **lines**. Square feet, square yards, acres and square miles measure **areas**. An area is length × width, so a square unit is the side length multiplied by itself. Check: 1 square mile = 5,280 ft × 5,280 ft = 27,878,400 sq ft, and 640 acres × 43,560 = 27,878,400 sq ft. The same number, so the constants agree.

**Metric intuition (for understanding only):** 1 acre ≈ 4,047 m² ≈ 0.405 ha. 640 acres ≈ 259 ha. 1 mile ≈ 1.61 km (PT-17; arithmetic checked with 1 ft = 0.3048 m exactly). Exam math never uses metric.

### Step 3: Formula

| Task | Formula |
|---|---|
| Acreage of an aliquot part | **640 ÷ each denominator** (same as 640 ÷ the product of the denominators) |
| Description joined by **AND** | Compute each parcel separately, then **add** |
| Square feet → acres | sq ft **÷ 43,560** |
| Acres → square feet | acres **× 43,560** |
| Feet → miles / miles → feet | **÷ 5,280** / **× 5,280** |
| Square feet → square yards | sq ft **÷ 9** |
| Square miles → acres / acres → square miles | **× 640** / **÷ 640** |
| Rectangle in feet | length × width = sq ft |
| Price | acres × price per acre |

**The acreage shortcut in action:**

- NW¼ of the SE¼ = 640 ÷ 4 ÷ 4 = **40 acres**.
- S½ of the NE¼ = 640 ÷ 2 ÷ 4 = **80 acres**.
- "N½ of the NW¼ **AND** the SW¼" = 80 + 160 = **240 acres**.

**Why it works.** Each "of" takes a fraction of the piece before it: half of a quarter is ½ × ¼ = ⅛ of the section, and 640 ÷ 8 = 80. **AND** joins separate parcels. After AND you start again from the full 640-acre section.

### Step 4: When it applies

- **Fractions of a section:** start from 640. The order of the fractions does not change the acreage.
- **"Of" versus "AND":** "of" nests one piece inside another (divide). "AND" adds a separate parcel (add).
- **Where versus how big:** to *locate* a parcel, read right to left. For its *size*, just divide.
- **Dimensions in feet or a price per acre:** get square feet, then acres, then the price.

### Step 5: Worked example

**A. Nested fractions.** "The SW¼ of the NE¼ of the NW¼ of Section 22."

| Read right to left | Piece | Acres |
|---|---|---|
| NW¼ | The northwest quarter of the section | 640 ÷ 4 = 160 |
| NE¼ of that | Its northeast quarter | 160 ÷ 4 = 40 |
| SW¼ of that | Its southwest quarter | 40 ÷ 4 = **10** |

Check: 4 × 4 × 4 = 64, and 640 ÷ 64 = 10 acres.

**B. AND.** "The W½ of the SW¼ AND the SE¼ of the SE¼."

- Parcel 1: W½ of the SW¼ = 640 ÷ 2 ÷ 4 = 80 acres.
- Parcel 2: SE¼ of the SE¼ = 640 ÷ 4 ÷ 4 = 40 acres.
- Total = 80 + 40 = **120 acres**.

Trap: reading it as one long chain gives 640 ÷ (2 × 4 × 4 × 4) = 640 ÷ 128 = 5 acres. That is wrong, because AND starts a new parcel.

**C. Feet to acres to price.** A rectangular tract in Polk County measures 660 ft × 1,320 ft and sells for $12,500 per acre.

- Area: 660 × 1,320 = 871,200 sq ft.
- Acres: 871,200 ÷ 43,560 = **20 acres**.
- Price: 20 × $12,500 = **$250,000**.
- Cross-check: 660 ft is half of 1,320 ft, so this is half of a 40-acre quarter-quarter: 20 acres.

**D. Township in feet and acres.** Each side is 6 miles × 5,280 = **31,680 ft**. The area is 36 square miles × 640 = **23,040 acres**.

### Step 6: Reasonableness check

- Parcels within one section can never total more than **640 acres**.
- Every extra "of" makes the parcel **smaller**. Every "AND" makes the total **larger**.
- A single aliquot part is always one of 320, 160, 80, 40, 20, 10, 5, 2.5 ... acres. A result such as 37.5 acres for one aliquot part means a slip.
- Anchors: **half = 320**, **quarter = 160**, **quarter-quarter = 40** acres.
- The number of acres is far smaller than the number of square feet. The number of square yards is one-ninth of the number of square feet.

### Step 7: Common errors

| Error | Example | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Dividing by 3 instead of 9 for square yards | 43,560 ÷ 3 = 14,520 sq yd per acre (wrong) | 43,560 ÷ 9 = **4,840** sq yd per acre |
| Chaining or multiplying across AND | Example B read as one chain = 5 acres | Compute each parcel, then **add**: 120 acres |
| Multiplying square feet by 43,560 | 871,200 × 43,560 = a huge number | Square feet → acres: **divide** |
| Mixing the mile and the acre | "5,280 sq ft in an acre" | 5,280 = feet in a **mile**. 43,560 = sq ft in an **acre** |
| Reading left to right to locate | In "NW¼ of the SE¼", looking first at the section's NW¼ | Start with the **last** fraction (SE¼) |

### Step 10: Exam application

Exam routine for any section question:

1. Circle every **AND**: each one starts a new parcel.
2. For each parcel, divide 640 by its denominators.
3. Add the parcels.
4. Multiply by any price per acre. For a lot given in feet: length × width ÷ 43,560 first.
5. Run the Step 6 checks.

If the answer choices include both a "divided" result and an "added" result, look again for the word AND. Recall questions ("sections in a township?", "acres in a section?", "corner of section 1?") are answered by 36, 640 and northeast.

## 6. Portugal comparison

**The Florida rule.** US customary units: feet, square feet, acres (43,560 sq ft), miles (5,280 ft) and square yards (9 sq ft). Exam math never uses metric.

**Exam implication.** Work in feet and acres from start to finish. Converting to metric and back only adds rounding errors. When a lot is given as two dimensions, the **front feet** are the frontage dimension, the first number given (PT-17). Memory aids: acre = 43,560 ("43-5-60"); mile = 5,280 ("five tomatoes": 5-2-80).

## 7. Legal and numerical requirements

**Memorize exactly**

- **Township = 6 × 6 miles = 36 square miles = 36 sections** (N-100; manual; not yet verified, V-26).
- **Section = 1 mile × 1 mile = 1 square mile = 640 acres** (N-101; manual; not yet verified, V-26).
- **Section 1 in the northeast**, serpentine numbering (west to 6, back east 7–12, ...), **section 36 in the southeast**. Section 16 was traditionally reserved for schools (manual; not yet verified).
- **1 acre = 43,560 sq ft · 1 mile = 5,280 ft · 1 square yard = 9 sq ft · 1 square mile = 640 acres** (manual; not yet verified, V-26).
- Acreage: **640 ÷ each denominator**. **AND = compute each parcel and add.** Locate: **read right to left** (manual; not yet verified).
- Metric for intuition only: 1 acre ≈ 4,047 m² ≈ 0.405 ha. 640 acres ≈ 259 ha (PT-17).

**Understand**

- Each "of" takes a fraction of the previous piece, so the denominators multiply and the parcel shrinks.
- AND starts a new parcel from the full section, so the acreages add.
- Square units are side × side: that is why a square yard is 9 sq ft, not 3.
- The constants are consistent: 640 × 43,560 = 5,280 × 5,280 = 27,878,400 sq ft in a square mile.
- Source status: all survey facts and constants come from the study manual and are labelled "not yet verified" (V-26). They are standard definitions, but the course has not yet checked them against an official source. The metric conversions are arithmetic checks (PT-17), not law.

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"Divide 43,560 by 3 to get square yards."** A square yard is 3 ft × 3 ft = **9** sq ft. Divide by 9 (4,840 sq yd per acre).
- **"Multiply the acreages when the description says AND."** AND joins separate parcels: compute each and **add**.
- **"More fractions make a bigger parcel."** Each added "of" makes it **smaller**.
- **"A township is 640 acres."** A **section** is 640 acres. A township is 36 sections (36 square miles). Do not confuse N-100 with N-101.
- **"Section 1 is in the northwest, like reading a page."** Section 1 is in the **northeast**. Section 36 is in the southeast.
- **"To find where the parcel is, start with the first fraction."** Read **right to left**: the last fraction is the biggest piece.
- **"I'll do the math in hectares and convert back."** Exam math never uses metric. Work in feet and acres.
