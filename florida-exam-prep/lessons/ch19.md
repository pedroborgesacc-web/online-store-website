## 2. Why it matters

**In practice.** Zoning decides what a buyer may do with a property: build an addition near the lot line, run a small daycare from home, keep the shop the seller operates. Permit records and the certificate of occupancy show whether a building, or a later addition, was built legally and may be used. The county or city decides these questions, not you. Your job is to spot them and send the client to the local planning or building department, or to a Florida attorney when rights are at stake.

**On the exam.** Section 19, "Planning and Zoning", carries **1%** of the exam (V-02): about 1 of the 100 questions (V-01), shared with L19-2. Expect a classification: police power or eminent domain? Variance, special exception, nonconforming use or rezoning? Building permit or certificate of occupancy? The study manual calls variance versus special exception "a guaranteed question". With only about one question for the whole section, read that as "the most likely topic", not a promise.

## 3. Simple explanation

*Source status: no verification entry covers the land-use rules in this lesson. Apart from the exam weight (V-01, V-02), everything here comes from the study manual and is labelled (manual; not yet verified). Each Florida county and city adopts its own plan and codes, so local details differ; the exam tests the general concepts.*

### Foundation: regulating land versus taking it

Owning land never means unlimited freedom. Government can **limit how you use** land, or it can **take the land itself**.

| | **Police power** | **Eminent domain** |
|---|---|---|
| What government does | **Regulates** use for public health, safety and welfare (zoning, building codes, permits) | **Takes** private property for public use |
| Legal process | Ordinances, codes, permits | **Condemnation** |
| Owner keeps the property? | Yes | No |
| Owner paid? | **No compensation** | **Just compensation** (fair payment for what is taken) |

(manual; not yet verified)

**Why is one paid and the other not?** A regulation places similar limits on many owners, and those owners also benefit: your home is safer and more pleasant when the neighbor cannot build a factory. A taking forces one owner to give up property so the public can have a road or a school, so the public pays that owner. "Police power" has nothing to do with the police force; it is the general power to make rules for public health, safety and welfare.

**Inverse condemnation.** In a normal condemnation the government starts the case. When a regulation goes so far that it **destroys all the property's value**, the **owner** may sue, claiming the regulation amounts to a **taking** that must be paid for. "Inverse" means reversed: the owner starts the case (manual; not yet verified). A mere drop in value is not enough; the course's trigger example is a regulation that destroys all value. This course cites no court decisions on exactly where regulation becomes a taking; know the label and the trigger.

Taxation (Section 18) and escheat (L09-1) are also government powers over property, but not land-use controls.

### The comprehensive plan and zoning

- **Comprehensive plan.** Every Florida local government (county or city) maintains one: its **long-range land-use plan**.
- **Zoning** implements the plan. A local law (**ordinance**) divides land into **districts**: **residential, commercial, industrial, agricultural and special districts**. Each district lists the uses allowed and dimensional rules such as **setback** (minimum distance between a building and a lot line or the street) and **height**.
- **Buffer zones** ease the transition between very different districts, for example offices or a park between a shopping center and single-family homes.

(manual; not yet verified)

The plan is the strategy; zoning is the rule book that carries it out, parcel by parcel. Zoning is **public** law; a **deed restriction** is a **private** limit recorded in a deed or plat (L09-4).

### When a rule does not fit: four tools

| Tool | What it is | District changes? | Why it exists | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Variance** | Permission to **deviate from a dimensional rule** (setback, height) because of a **hardship unique to the parcel** | No; same use | Rules written for typical lots can be unfair to an unusually narrow or odd-shaped one | Narrow lot: house would sit 3 feet (about 0.9 m) inside the side setback |
| **Special exception** (also **conditional use**) | A use the code **already permits** in the district **with case-by-case approval** | No | Some uses serve a neighborhood but fit only on the right site | School, church or daycare in a residential district |
| **Nonconforming use** | A use **lawful before the code changed** ("grandfathered") | No | An owner who followed the old rules is not forced out | Corner store opened before the street was rezoned residential |
| **Rezoning** | **Changing the district itself** | **Yes** | The wanted use is not allowed in the district at all | Farmland changed to residential for a subdivision |

(manual; not yet verified)

**Limits of "grandfathered".** A nonconforming use **may continue**, but the code tolerates rather than encourages it: it **usually may not expand**, and if **destroyed or abandoned**, it **may not resume** (manual; not yet verified).

**The manual's exam trap:** hardship + dimensions → **variance**; permitted-with-approval use → **special exception**. **Neither is a rezoning** (manual; not yet verified).

**PUD (planned unit development).** A development planned as one unit that **mixes uses** (homes, shops, offices) and **clusters density** (buildings grouped closer together) **in exchange for open space**, such as parks or preserved land (manual; not yet verified).

### Construction control

1. **Building permit:** needed **before** construction starts.
2. **Inspections:** during the work, against the building codes.
3. **Certificate of occupancy (CO):** issued **after final inspection**; the building may not be used or occupied before it.

Florida **contractors** are licensed by the **CILB** (Construction Industry Licensing Board), not by the FREC. (manual; not yet verified)

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"If zoning lowers my property's value, the county must pay me."** No. Zoning is police power; a loss of value alone earns no compensation. Inverse condemnation is for a regulation that goes so far it amounts to a taking; the course's trigger example is a regulation that destroys all value.
- **"Eminent domain is voluntary because the owner is paid."** No. It is an involuntary transfer; just compensation is not consent (L09-1).
- **"A variance lets me use my lot for a different purpose."** No. A variance relaxes a dimensional rule. A different use needs a special exception (if the code lists it) or a rezoning.
- **"Any question that mentions a height limit is about a variance."** No. A height limit imposed on everyone is police power; a variance is one owner's request to deviate from it because of a hardship unique to the parcel.
- **"Grandfathered means protected forever."** A nonconforming use usually cannot expand and, if destroyed or abandoned, may not resume.
- **"The building permit lets the owner move in."** The permit allows construction; occupancy needs the CO after final inspection.
- **"Deed restrictions are part of zoning."** Deed restrictions are private and recorded; zoning is police power.

## 2. Why it matters

**In practice.** Selling or renting a home built before 1978 triggers federal lead-based paint paperwork (with some exclusions and exemptions, covered below), and the agents must make sure it is done. Selling or renting **any** building needs Florida's radon notice. A buyer of an old gas station or factory lot faces a bigger risk: under federal cleanup law, a new owner can pay for contamination someone else caused. You are not an environmental expert, but you must know which forms belong in the file and when to refer a client to an inspector, environmental consultant or attorney.

**On the exam.** Section 19, "Planning and Zoning", carries **1%** of the exam (V-02): about 1 of the 100 questions (V-01), shared with L19-1. Typical questions: the lead-paint year (**1978**), the buyer's inspection opportunity (**10 days**, unless waived or changed in writing), whether a new owner can be liable for contamination she did not cause (**yes**, under CERCLA, unless she has a defense), and intact asbestos (**encapsulation**).

## 3. Simple explanation

*Source status: lead-based paint (V-60, confirmed; single-checked) and the radon notice (V-73, confirmed) are verified against official sources. Asbestos, CERCLA, brownfields, wetlands, EIS and underground storage tanks come only from the study manual and are labelled (manual; not yet verified).*

### Foundation: federal rules and Florida rules

Two layers of law apply at once. **Federal** rules apply in every state; Florida statutes add state rules. Federal agency rules are published in the **CFR** (Code of Federal Regulations). The **EPA** is the US Environmental Protection Agency; **HUD** is the US Department of Housing and Urban Development. Lead paint and CERCLA are federal; the radon notice is Florida law.

### Lead-based paint (V-60)

**Which housing?** **Target housing** = housing built **before 1978** (40 CFR 745.103). The EPA rule is 40 CFR 745 Subpart F; HUD's identical rule is 24 CFR Part 35 Subpart A.

- **Excluded:** housing for the elderly or persons with disabilities, and **0-bedroom dwellings** (no separate bedroom, such as a studio), unless a **child under 6** lives or is expected to live there.
- **Exempt transactions** (745.101) include: **foreclosure sales**; **leases of 100 days or less with no renewal**; leases of housing **certified lead-free**; **renewals** where disclosure was already made.

**Duties of the seller or landlord (lessor)** (745.107, 745.110, 745.113):

| Duty | Sale | Lease |
|---|---|---|
| Give an EPA-approved pamphlet, such as *Protect Your Family From Lead in Your Home* | Yes | Yes |
| Disclose **known** lead-based paint and lead-based paint hazards | Yes | Yes |
| Provide **available** records and reports | Yes | Yes |
| Disclose the same to **each agent** | Yes | Yes |
| Include the **Lead Warning Statement** and **signed acknowledgments** in the contract or lease | Yes | Yes |
| Allow a **10-day** opportunity for a risk assessment or inspection **before the buyer is bound** | **Yes** | **No** |

**Timing.** If the disclosure comes after an offer, it must be made **before the seller or lessor accepts** it, so the buyer or tenant has the information while still free to walk away.

**The 10-day opportunity.** Before the buyer is bound by the contract, the seller must allow **10 days** for a lead risk assessment or inspection, **unless the parties agree in writing on a different period**. The buyer may also **waive** it **in writing**. The manual's short version, "may be waived", is correct but incomplete. **Tenants** get the disclosure but **not** the 10-day period.

**Your duty as a licensee.** Licensees share responsibility for compliance: agents must **ensure compliance** (745.115). Lead paperwork is not only the seller's or landlord's job.

**Year trap.** **1978** is the lead-paint year. **1988** is when fair housing law added familial status and handicap (V-56). The manual warns that both years may appear among the answer choices.

### Radon (V-73)

**Radon** is a naturally occurring radioactive gas. Florida requires the statutory **radon notice** on **at least one document signed at or before** a **sale contract or rental agreement**, for **any building** (s. 404.056(5); V-73).

- **Any building:** new or old, residential or commercial; **sale or rental**.
- The manual says the notice appears "in every sale and lease contract". That points the right way; the verified wording is more precise: at least one document signed at or before the contract or rental agreement.
- It is a **notice**. This course's sources describe no testing requirement.

Other Florida disclosures around the contract (flood, property tax summary, energy-efficiency rating information) are in L11-4.

### Asbestos (manual; not yet verified)

**Asbestos** is a fibrous mineral found in older building materials, especially **insulation** (pre-1980s). It is dangerous when **friable**: crumbling, so fibers can get into the air and be breathed in. When the material is intact, **encapsulation** (sealing or covering it so fibers cannot escape) is often **safer than removal**, because removal disturbs the material and can release fibers.

### CERCLA / Superfund (manual; not yet verified)

**CERCLA** (the Comprehensive Environmental Response, Compensation, and Liability Act), known as **Superfund**, is the federal law on cleaning up contaminated sites.

| Feature | Meaning | Consequence for an owner |
|---|---|---|
| **Strict** liability | Liability without fault | An owner can be liable **without having caused** the contamination |
| **Joint and several** liability | Each liable party can be made to pay the whole cleanup cost, not only its share | One owner may face the entire bill |

**Innocent-landowner defense.** A current owner can be liable **unless she qualifies for a defense**, such as the innocent-landowner defense. It requires **due diligence before purchase**, such as a **Phase I environmental audit** (a review of the site and its history before buying). Why so strict? The law gets contaminated land cleaned up and pushes buyers to investigate first; a buyer who skipped the investigation cannot meet the innocent-landowner defense.

### Other exam words (manual; not yet verified)

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Brownfield** | Abandoned or underused contaminated site being redeveloped |
| **Wetlands** | Land wet or flooded much of the time, such as a marsh or swamp; development there is subject to wetlands permitting |
| **EIS** | Environmental impact statement: a study of a project's environmental effects, prepared for major federal actions |
| **UST** | Underground storage tank, for example at a gas station; a leak can contaminate soil and groundwater |

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"The lead-paint year is 1988."** No: **1978**. 1988 is when fair housing added familial status and handicap (V-56).
- **"Tenants also get 10 days to inspect."** No. Tenants get the pamphlet, the disclosure, available reports and the Lead Warning Statement, but no 10-day period (V-60).
- **"The seller can disclose after accepting the offer."** No. A disclosure that follows an offer must come before acceptance (V-60).
- **"The radon notice is only for older homes, or only when radon is found, or only for sales."** It is for the sale or rental of any building (V-73).
- **"A new owner who did not cause the contamination cannot be liable."** Under CERCLA she can be, unless she qualifies for a defense such as innocent landowner (manual; not yet verified).
- **"Asbestos must always be removed at once."** Intact, non-friable asbestos is often better encapsulated (manual; not yet verified).
