## 2. Why it matters

**In practice.** Almost every number you discuss with a Florida client is a percentage of something: the commission on a sale price, a down payment, an offer "5% under list price", or the change in a home's value since it was bought. You already do these calculations in Portugal. Here you must do them quickly, in US number notation, and with the decimal point in the right place.

**On the exam.** Section 14, "Real Estate Computations and Closing of Transactions", carries **6%** of the state exam (V-02). The study manual estimates about ten pure-arithmetic questions across the whole exam, and its twelve math patterns include mortgage, appraisal and tax problems as well as the topics of this chapter (manual; not yet verified). Most of them rest on the one idea in this lesson: the **percent triangle**. Learn it once and the commission, net-to-seller and profit lessons become variations on it.

## 3. Simple explanation

### Step 1: What it represents

Most exam math is one idea: **a part is a rate (a percentage) of a total.**

| Word | Meaning | Real estate examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Total** (also called the *base* or the *whole*) | The amount the percentage is taken **of** | Sale price, purchase cost, original value |
| **Rate** | The percentage, written as a decimal when you calculate | Commission rate, down-payment percentage, profit percentage |
| **Part** | The piece that results | Commission in dollars, down payment in dollars, profit in dollars |

Take a **6% commission on a $300,000 sale**. The total is $300,000, the rate is 6% (0.06) and the part is the commission: $300,000 × 0.06 = **$18,000**.

### Step 2: Units

**Percent** means "per hundred": 6% = 6/100 = 0.06. Before you multiply or divide, turn every percent into a decimal.

| Conversion | How | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Percent → decimal | Divide by 100: move the decimal point **two places left** | 6% = 0.06 · 0.5% = 0.005 · 125% = 1.25 |
| Decimal → percent | Multiply by 100: move the point **two places right** | 0.375 = 37.5% · 0.08 = 8% |
| Fraction → decimal | Divide the top number (numerator) by the bottom number (denominator) | 3/8 = 3 ÷ 8 = 0.375 = 37.5% |
| Decimal → fraction | Write it over 10, 100 or 1,000, then simplify | 0.25 = 25/100 = 1/4 |
| Percent → fraction | Write it over 100, then simplify | 12.5% = 12.5/100 = 125/1,000 = 1/8 |

**Equivalents worth knowing by heart:** 1/2 = 0.5 = 50% · 1/4 = 0.25 = 25% · 3/4 = 0.75 = 75% · 1/8 = 0.125 = 12.5% · 1/3 ≈ 0.3333 = 33⅓% · 2/3 ≈ 0.6667 = 66⅔%.

**US notation.** The period is the decimal point and commas group the thousands: $1,250.50 is one thousand two hundred fifty dollars and fifty cents.

The part and the total always have the **same unit** (dollars with dollars). The rate has no unit.

### Step 3: Formula

**The percent triangle:** **Part = Total × Rate.** Rearranged:

- **Total = Part ÷ Rate**
- **Rate = Part ÷ Total**

Picture a triangle with **Part** at the top and **Total × Rate** at the bottom (Section 8). Cover the number you want. If the two numbers left are side by side, multiply them. If one sits above the other, divide the top one by the bottom one.

**Word clues:** "of" usually means multiply (6% *of* the price). "Is" or "was" means equals (the commission *was* $9,450). "What percent" asks for the rate.

**Percentage change** is a special case in which the total is the starting value:

**Percentage change = (new − old) ÷ old**

A positive result is an increase; a negative result is a decrease.

### Step 4: When it applies

1. Identify the **total the percentage is taken of**. This is the most important decision in all exam math.
2. If that total is known, **multiply** to get the part.
3. If the percentage is applied to the **unknown** (the total is what you are looking for), **divide**.
4. For a change over time, the base is always the **original** (old) value.

| The question says | Total (base) | Unknown | Operation |
|---|---|---|---|
| "a 6% commission on a $300,000 sale" | Sale price, known | Commission | Multiply: $300,000 × 0.06 |
| "the 6% commission was $9,450" | Sale price, unknown | Sale price | Divide: $9,450 ÷ 0.06 |
| "$41,250 down on a $275,000 home" | Price, known | Rate | Divide: $41,250 ÷ $275,000 |
| "value went from $80,000 to $92,000" | Old value, $80,000 | % change | Change ÷ old value |

### Step 5: Worked example

**A. Find the total.** A 6% commission was $9,450. What was the sale price?

1. Part = $9,450. Rate = 0.06. The total is unknown, so Total = Part ÷ Rate.
2. $9,450 ÷ 0.06 = **$157,500**.
3. Check: $157,500 × 0.06 = $9,450 ✓.

**B. Find the rate.** A buyer in Lakeland pays $41,250 down on a $275,000 home.

1. Rate = Part ÷ Total = $41,250 ÷ $275,000 = 0.15 = **15%**.
2. Check: $275,000 × 0.15 = $41,250 ✓.

**C. Percentage change.** A lot bought for $80,000 sells for $92,000.

1. Change = $92,000 − $80,000 = $12,000.
2. $12,000 ÷ $80,000 (the old value) = 0.15 = **+15%**.
3. Reverse the story: a fall from $92,000 to $80,000 is $12,000 ÷ $92,000 = 0.1304, about **−13%**.

The dollar change is the same, but the **base changes**, so the percentages differ.

### Step 6: Reasonableness check

- A commission is a small fraction of the price. If your "price" is smaller than the commission, you multiplied instead of divided. ($9,450 × 0.06 = $567, which cannot be the price of a house.)
- A rate below 100% always gives a part smaller than the total.
- Estimate with easy percentages: 10% means moving the point one place left, 1% two places. So 1% of $157,500 is $1,575, and 6% is about 6 × $1,575 = $9,450 ✓.
- The same dollar change is a bigger percentage of a smaller base.

### Step 7: Common errors

| Error | What it looks like | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Decimal placement | Using 0.6 for 6% (ten times too big) | 6% = 0.06; 0.6 = 60% |
| Not converting the percent | $9,450 ÷ 6 = $1,575 | Divide by 0.06, not by 6 |
| Wrong base | Measuring the fall from $92,000 against $80,000 | Divide the change by the **original** value |
| Multiplying when the percent is "of" the unknown | $9,450 × 0.06 | Divide: $9,450 ÷ 0.06 |
| Rounding too early | One-third of $240,000 using 0.33 gives $79,200 instead of $80,000 | Keep full decimals (or the fraction); round only the final answer |

### Step 10: Exam application

1. Read the last sentence first. Is the question asking for the **part**, the **total** or the **rate**?
2. Find the total the percentage is "of".
3. Convert the percent to a decimal.
4. Choose: **multiply** if the total is known; **divide** if the total is unknown or the rate is wanted.
5. Calculate with full decimals. Round only the final answer, the way the question asks.
6. Reverse-check by multiplying back.

Wrong answer choices are usually built from the typical mistakes: the multiply-instead-of-divide figure, the wrong-base figure and a misplaced decimal. If your answer matches a choice, still run the reverse check.

One rounding exception you will meet later: documentary stamp tax is charged per $100 "or portion", so you round the amount **up** to the next $100 before calculating (V-18).

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Percent (%) | "Per hundred": 6% = 6/100 = 0.06 |
| Decimal | A number written with a decimal point, such as 0.06 |
| Fraction | A top number (numerator) over a bottom number (denominator), such as 3/8 |
| Rate | The percentage used in a calculation, written as a decimal |
| Total (base, whole) | The amount the percentage is taken **of** |
| Part | The result of rate × total |
| Percent triangle | Part on top, Total × Rate below; cover the unknown to see the formula |
| Percentage change | (new − old) ÷ old |
| Original value | The starting amount; the base for any percentage change |

## 5. Practical example

**Rita**, a sales associate at a Tampa brokerage, meets sellers who bought their St. Petersburg home for **$320,000**.

1. **"How much has our home gained?"** Recent comparable sales suggest about **$352,000**. Change: $32,000 ÷ $320,000 (what they paid) = **+10%**.
2. **The offer.** They list at $352,000, and a buyer offers 5% below the list price. Discount: $352,000 × 0.05 = $17,600. Offer: $352,000 − $17,600 = **$334,400** (or $352,000 × 0.95).
3. **The buyer's down payment.** The buyer's lender letter shows $66,880 down. Rate: $66,880 ÷ $334,400 = 0.20 = **20%** down.
4. **The commission.** If the agreed total commission is 5.5% and the sale closes at $334,400: $334,400 × 0.055 = **$18,392**.

Each answer used the same triangle. The only real decision each time was: *what is the total?* The answer was the purchase cost for the gain, the list price for the discount, and the sale price for the down payment and the commission.

## 6. Portugal comparison

**Familiar principle.** Percentages, fractions and proportions are the same arithmetic everywhere. The course's Portugal–Florida comparison entries contain nothing that changes this lesson.

**Florida rule.** Exam numbers are written in US notation: a decimal **point** and **commas** between thousands ($1,250.50).

**Precise difference.** Only the notation. If you usually write a decimal comma (0,06) and a thousands point (1.250), train your eye: on the exam, 0.06 is six hundredths and 1,250 is one thousand two hundred fifty.

**Exam implication.** No Portuguese rule is involved. Read every number in US format and apply the triangle.

## 7. Legal and numerical requirements

**Memorize exactly**

- **Part = Total × Rate** · **Total = Part ÷ Rate** · **Rate = Part ÷ Total**.
- Percent → decimal: ÷ 100 (point two places left). Decimal → percent: × 100 (point two places right). Fraction → decimal: top ÷ bottom (3/8 = 0.375 = 37.5%).
- **Percentage change = (new − old) ÷ old.**
- Section 14 (computations and closing) = **6%** of the exam (V-02).

**Understand**

- No statute governs this lesson: it is arithmetic. The one real skill is choosing the right base.
- When the percent is taken **of the unknown**, divide.
- The base for a change is the **original** value, so a rise and a fall of the same dollar amount give different percentages.
- Round only at the end, unless the question or a tax rule says otherwise (documentary stamps: round up to the next $100, V-18).

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"6% is 0.6."** No. 6% = **0.06**; 0.6 is 60%.
- **"A percentage always means multiply."** No. When the percentage is taken of the unknown amount, **divide**.
- **"Percentage change is measured on the new value."** No. Always divide by the **original** value.
- **"A 15% rise is undone by a 15% fall."** No. The fall is measured on the larger base: $80,000 → $92,000 is +15%, but $92,000 → $80,000 is only about −13%.
- **"Rounding a rate early (1/3 → 0.33) is fine."** No. On $240,000 it gives $79,200 instead of $80,000: early rounding can shift the answer by hundreds of dollars. Round once, at the end.

## 10. Memory sheet

- **Part = Total × Rate.** Cover the unknown: Total = Part ÷ Rate; Rate = Part ÷ Total.
- Percent ↔ decimal: move the point **two places**. Fraction → decimal: **top ÷ bottom** (3/8 = 37.5%).
- **Percent of the unknown → divide.**
- **Change ÷ original**: $80,000 → $92,000 = +15%; back down = about −13%.
- Reverse-check every answer. Round only at the end.

## 2. Why it matters

**In practice.** Florida listings give living area in **square feet**, land in square feet or **acres**, and distances in **miles**. Lots on a street or on the water can be compared by the **front foot**, and carpet or concrete is often priced by the **square yard**. A buyer from Lisbon will ask you "how many square metres is that?", and a seller will ask "what is my lot worth per front foot compared with the one next door?". The first needs a quick metric conversion; the second is pure US-unit math.

**On the exam.** Area is one of the study manual's twelve math patterns (pattern 11). It belongs to Section 14, which carries **6%** of the exam (V-02). These questions test unit conversion as much as geometry: dividing square feet by 3 instead of 9 is the classic wrong answer.

## 3. Simple explanation

> **Source status.** The measurement constants below (square yard, acre, mile, square mile) come from the study manual and have not been independently checked by this course: **(manual; not yet verified, V-26)**. V-26 notes that they are standard definitions. The metric equivalents are verified arithmetic (1 ft = 0.3048 m exactly; PT-17).

### Step 1: What it represents

| Measure | What it measures | Units | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Length / perimeter** | Distance along a line, or all the way around a shape | Inches, feet (ft), yards (yd), miles | Fencing around a lot |
| **Area** | A flat surface | Square feet (sq ft), square yards (sq yd), acres | Living area, lot size |
| **Volume** | Space in three dimensions | Cubic feet, cubic yards | Concrete for a slab |
| **Front feet** | The length of a lot's side along the street or the water | Feet | A 75' × 150' lot has 75 front feet |

Symbols: **'** means feet and **"** means inches, so 75' × 150' means 75 feet by 150 feet.

### Step 2: Units

**Constants (manual; not yet verified, V-26)**

| Unit | Equals | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1 foot | 12 inches | Definition |
| 1 yard | 3 feet | Definition |
| 1 square yard | **9 square feet** | 3 ft × 3 ft |
| 1 cubic yard | **27 cubic feet** | 3 ft × 3 ft × 3 ft |
| 1 mile | **5,280 feet** | = 1,760 yards (5,280 ÷ 3) |
| 1 acre | **43,560 square feet** | Definition |
| 1 square mile | **640 acres** | Definition |

**Why 9 and not 3?** A square yard is 3 feet long *and* 3 feet wide, so it contains 3 × 3 = 9 square feet. A cubic yard adds a third dimension: 3 × 3 × 3 = 27.

**Direction rule.** Going from a **bigger** unit to a **smaller** one, multiply (5 acres × 43,560 = 217,800 sq ft). Going from a **smaller** unit to a **bigger** one, divide (12,600 sq ft ÷ 43,560 ≈ 0.29 acre).

*Metric aside (never used in exam math):* 1 sq ft ≈ 0.0929 m²; 1 acre ≈ 4,047 m² ≈ 0.405 ha; 1 mile ≈ 1.61 km.

### Step 3: Formula

| To find | Formula |
|---|---|
| Area of a rectangle (or square) | length × width |
| Area of a triangle | ½ × base × height (the height is measured at a right angle to the base) |
| Area of a composite shape | Split it into rectangles and triangles, compute each, then add (subtract any cut-out) |
| Perimeter | Add all the sides; for a rectangle, 2 × (length + width) |
| Volume (if asked) | length × width × height; cubic yards = cubic feet ÷ 27 |
| Price per front foot | price ÷ front feet |
| Price per sq ft (or per acre) | price ÷ sq ft (or ÷ acres) |
| Total price from a unit price | area in the price's unit × unit price |

### Step 4: When it applies

- **Same unit first.** Every dimension must be in the same unit before you multiply. Inches → feet: divide by 12 (6 in = 0.5 ft; 4 in ≈ 0.333 ft).
- **Match the price's unit.** If the price is per square yard, convert the area to square yards (÷ 9). If it is per acre, divide by 43,560.
- **Front feet.** The frontage is the dimension along the street or water. It is **usually the first number given**: a 75' × 150' lot has 75 front feet, and 150 is the depth. If the question tells you which side faces the street or water, use that side.
- **Triangles.** Use the perpendicular height, not a slanted side, and do not forget the ½.
- **Volume** only when a thickness or depth appears (a concrete slab, fill dirt).

### Step 5: Worked example

**A. Rectangle and price per square foot.** An Ocala lot is 90 ft × 140 ft and sells at $14 per sq ft.

1. Area: 90 × 140 = **12,600 sq ft**.
2. Price: 12,600 × $14 = **$176,400**.
3. In acres: 12,600 ÷ 43,560 = 0.289, about **0.29 acre**.

**B. Composite (L-shaped) house.** The main block is 50 ft × 32 ft and a wing is 18 ft × 14 ft.

1. Main block: 50 × 32 = 1,600 sq ft. Wing: 18 × 14 = 252 sq ft.
2. Total: 1,600 + 252 = **1,852 sq ft**.

**C. Triangle.** A corner parcel has a base of 180 ft and a height of 220 ft. Area: ½ × 180 × 220 = **19,800 sq ft**.

**D. Front foot.** An 80' × 125' lot sells for $112,000. Front feet = 80. Price per front foot: $112,000 ÷ 80 = **$1,400**.

**E. Volume.** A slab is 30 ft × 20 ft and 6 inches thick. Thickness: 6 ÷ 12 = 0.5 ft. Volume: 30 × 20 × 0.5 = 300 cubic ft. In cubic yards: 300 ÷ 27 ≈ **11.11 cubic yd**.

### Step 6: Reasonableness check

- The number of square yards is about **one-ninth** of the number of square feet, and the number of acres is far smaller still.
- A quarter acre is 43,560 ÷ 4 = **10,890 sq ft**, so a typical lot of about 10,000 sq ft is "a bit under a quarter acre". One acre is roughly a square about 209 ft on each side.
- For the same lot, the price per front foot is much larger than the price per square foot, because each front foot carries the whole depth behind it.
- Work backwards: 1,600 + 252 = 1,852 ✓; $1,400 × 80 = $112,000 ✓.

### Step 7: Common errors

| Error | Correction |
|---|---|
| Dividing sq ft by **3** to get sq yd | Divide by **9** (3 × 3) |
| Dividing cubic ft by 9 to get cubic yd | Divide by **27** (3 × 3 × 3) |
| Mixing feet and yards (or inches) in one multiplication | Convert every dimension to one unit first |
| Forgetting the **½** for a triangle | Area = ½ × base × height |
| Using the depth as the frontage | Front feet = the street or water side, usually the first number |
| Rounding acres early (0.29 instead of 0.289…) | Keep full decimals until the last step |

### Step 10: Exam application

1. **Sketch** the shape and label every dimension.
2. **Convert** all dimensions to one unit (usually feet).
3. **Compute the area** by shape. Split composite shapes; use ½ for triangles.
4. **Convert the area** to the unit of the price: ÷ 9 for square yards, ÷ 43,560 for acres.
5. **Multiply** by the unit price, or **divide** the price by the units (front feet, sq ft, acres).
6. **Check** by working backwards and by size: square yards ≈ one-ninth of square feet.

Expect wrong answer choices built from ÷ 3 instead of ÷ 9, from a missing ½, and from using the depth instead of the frontage.

## 5. Practical example

**A canal lot in Cape Coral.** A couple from Lisbon is looking at a lot with **80 feet on the canal** and **125 feet of depth**, listed at **$240,000**. A second canal lot nearby has 90 feet of frontage, is 110 feet deep, and is listed at $256,500.

| | Lot 1 | Lot 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Front feet | 80 | 90 |
| Price per front foot | $240,000 ÷ 80 = **$3,000** | $256,500 ÷ 90 = **$2,850** |
| Area | 80 × 125 = 10,000 sq ft | 90 × 110 = 9,900 sq ft |
| Price per sq ft | $240,000 ÷ 10,000 = $24.00 | $256,500 ÷ 9,900 ≈ $25.91 |
| Acres | 10,000 ÷ 43,560 ≈ 0.23 | 9,900 ÷ 43,560 ≈ 0.23 |

Lot 2 costs more in total but **less per front foot**, which is exactly the comparison front-foot pricing is designed for. Per square foot it is dearer, because its shallower depth gives it less area for a higher price. For the clients' intuition you add that Lot 1 is about 929 m² (10,000 × 0.0929). Then you return to feet: Florida works in US customary units, and the exam uses nothing else.

They also want a paver patio of 24 ft × 18 ft, quoted at $65 per square yard: 24 × 18 = 432 sq ft; 432 ÷ 9 = 48 sq yd; 48 × $65 = **$3,120**.

## 6. Portugal comparison

**Familiar principle.** In Portugal you work in the metric system: m² and hectares (1 ha = 10,000 m²) (PT-17).

## 9. Common misconceptions

## 2. Why it matters

**In practice.** Two questions come up in almost every listing appointment and every pay day. The seller asks: *"What do I have to sell for to walk away with $X?"* You ask: *"What will I actually receive from this commission?"* Both answers depend on doing the percentages in the right order and on the right base.

**On the exam.** The commission chain and net-to-seller are patterns 2 and 3 of the study manual's twelve math patterns. They sit in Section 14, which carries **6%** of the exam (V-02). The wrong answer choices are built from predictable mistakes, such as multiplying the net by 1.06 or applying the associate's split to the whole commission. If you know the mistakes, you can spot the trap.

## 3. Simple explanation

### Step 1: What it represents

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Total commission** | The brokerage fee for the sale; in exam problems, a percentage of the **sale price**. On the closing statement it is customarily a seller debit, that is, an amount the seller owes (manual; not yet verified). |
| **Listing brokerage** | The brokerage that took the listing (works with the seller) |
| **Cooperating (selling) brokerage** | The brokerage that brought the buyer |
| **Co-brokerage split** | How the total commission is divided between the two brokerages |
| **Associate split** | How a brokerage's share is divided between the brokerage and its sales associate |
| **Net to seller** | What the seller keeps after the commission and the other selling costs |

In exam problems the money flows **down a chain**: sale price → total commission → each brokerage's share → each associate's share.

### Step 2: Units

Everything is in dollars and percentages. The key point about units is that **each split percentage is a percentage of the amount just above it** in the chain, not of the sale price. "The associate receives 60%" means 60% of her brokerage's share.

Convert every percentage to a decimal: 6% = 0.06, half = 0.50, 60% = 0.60.

### Step 3: Formula

**Commission chain (multiply down):**

- Total commission = Price × Rate
- Brokerage share = Total commission × brokerage split
- Associate share = Brokerage share × associate split
- In one line: **Associate share = Price × Rate × brokerage split × associate split**

**Working backwards (divide up):** divide by each split in turn, then use Rate = Total commission ÷ Price, or Price = Total commission ÷ Rate.

**Net to seller:**

**Price = (Net + fixed costs) ÷ (1 − rate)**

**Why this works.** The price is 100%. The commission takes the rate (say 6%) **of the price**, so the seller keeps 94% of the price (1 − 0.06 = 0.94). That 94% must cover the seller's net plus any fixed dollar costs. So 0.94 × Price = Net + fixed costs, and dividing both sides by 0.94 gives the price. It is the percent triangle again: the percentage is taken of the unknown price, so you **divide**.

### Step 4: When it applies

- **Read each split carefully.** "The listing brokerage receives half" = 50% of the total. "The brokerage keeps 40%" means the associate receives 60%. With a split written as "70/30", read the wording to see who receives which part.
- **Net-to-seller** applies whenever the seller names an amount to keep **after** the commission.
- **Fixed costs** are amounts stated in dollars (for example, "$4,500 of other closing costs", or a mortgage payoff when the seller's target is the cash in hand). They go in the numerator, **before** dividing.
- **A cost stated as a percentage of the price** belongs with the rate, not in the numerator: a 6% commission plus a 1% fee means dividing by 1 − 0.07 = 0.93.
- **Rounding.** Follow the question (often to the nearest dollar). After rounding, your check may differ by a few cents; that is rounding, not an error.

### Step 5: Worked example

**A. Down the chain.** A Jacksonville home sells for $360,000 with a 5% total commission. The listing brokerage receives half, and the listing associate receives 70% of her brokerage's share.

1. Total commission: $360,000 × 0.05 = $18,000.
2. Listing brokerage: $18,000 × 0.50 = $9,000.
3. Listing associate: $9,000 × 0.70 = **$6,300**.
4. One line: 360,000 × 0.05 × 0.50 × 0.70 = $6,300 ✓.

**B. Up the chain.** The associate received $6,300, which was 70% of her brokerage's share. The brokerage's share was half of the total commission on a $360,000 sale. What was the rate?

1. Brokerage share: $6,300 ÷ 0.70 = $9,000.
2. Total commission: $9,000 ÷ 0.50 = $18,000.
3. Rate: $18,000 ÷ $360,000 = 0.05 = **5%**.

**C. Net to seller.** A seller wants to net $188,000 after a 6% commission, with no other costs.

1. Price = $188,000 ÷ (1 − 0.06) = $188,000 ÷ 0.94 = **$200,000**.
2. Check: $200,000 × 6% = $12,000; $200,000 − $12,000 = $188,000 ✓.
3. **The common error:** $188,000 × 1.06 = $199,280. That takes 6% of the net, not of the price, and leaves the seller short. At $199,280 the commission is $11,956.80, and the seller nets $187,323.20, which is **$676.80 short**.

**D. Net with fixed costs.** A seller wants to net $277,000 after a 6% commission and $5,000 of other closing costs.

1. Price = ($277,000 + $5,000) ÷ 0.94 = $282,000 ÷ 0.94 = **$300,000**.
2. Check: $300,000 − $18,000 commission − $5,000 costs = $277,000 ✓.

### Step 6: Reasonableness check

- Down the chain every number gets smaller: associate share < brokerage share < total commission < price.
- The net-to-seller price must be **more** than the net plus the costs, and more than (net + costs) × (1 + rate).
- **Always check by taking the commission from your answer:** price − (price × rate) − fixed costs should equal the net.
- Working backwards, each step should get **larger**. If a number shrinks, you multiplied instead of dividing.

### Step 7: Common errors

| Error | What it looks like | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Net × (1 + rate) | $188,000 × 1.06 = $199,280 | Net ÷ (1 − rate) = $200,000 |
| Net × (1 − rate) or net − commission | $188,000 × 0.94 = $176,720 | That is smaller than the net: impossible |
| Applying the associate's % to the total or the price | 60% of the whole commission | Apply it to the brokerage's share |
| Adding fixed costs after dividing | $188,000 ÷ 0.94 + costs | Add them **before** dividing |
| Reading "brokerage keeps 40%" as the associate's share | Associate gets 40% | Associate gets 60% |
| Multiplying when working backwards | $6,300 × 0.70 | Divide by each split |

### Step 10: Exam application

**Commission chain**

1. Write the chain: price → total → brokerage → associate.
2. Mark each split and what it is a percentage **of**.
3. Going down, multiply. Going up, divide.
4. Check that the numbers shrink going down and grow going up.

**Net to seller**

1. Add the fixed dollar costs to the net.
2. Subtract the rate (plus any other percentage-of-price costs) from 1.
3. Divide.
4. Check by taking the commission (and costs) from your answer.

If one answer choice equals net × 1.06 and another equals net ÷ 0.94, the first is the trap.

## 5. Practical example

**A listing appointment in Sarasota.** The sellers tell Joana, a listing associate, that they need **$464,000** after selling to buy their next home. Their other closing costs are estimated at **$6,000**. Joana's brokerage proposes a **6%** total commission, to be split 50/50 with any cooperating brokerage. Joana receives **60%** of her brokerage's share.

1. **Minimum price:** ($464,000 + $6,000) ÷ 0.94 = $470,000 ÷ 0.94 = **$500,000**.
2. **Check:** $500,000 × 0.06 = $30,000. $500,000 − $30,000 − $6,000 = $464,000 ✓.
3. **Why not "$470,000 plus 6%"?** $470,000 × 1.06 = $498,200, and at that price the sellers would net $498,200 × 0.94 − $6,000 = $462,308, which is $1,692 short.
4. **The chain at $500,000:** total $30,000 → listing brokerage $15,000 → Joana $15,000 × 0.60 = **$9,000**. The cooperating brokerage also receives $15,000. If the buyer's associate is on a 70% split, she receives $10,500.

Joana shows the sellers the check line. Clients trust a number they can verify.

## 2. Why it matters

**In practice.** Investors and homeowners measure success in percentages: *"I made 18% on that townhouse"*, *"our condo has gone up 15% since we bought it"*. When a seller says "I sold at a 15% profit for $276,000", you should be able to say at once what they paid. The answer is not $234,600.

**On the exam.** Profit on cost is pattern 4 of the study manual's twelve math patterns, and percentage of change is one of its two extra patterns. Both sit in Section 14, which carries **6%** of the exam (V-02). The manual calls percentages taken of **different bases** the single most-missed idea in exam math. The trap answer is usually waiting among the choices.

## 3. Simple explanation

### Step 1: What it represents

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Cost** | What the owner paid for the property (the *base* of every profit or loss percentage) |
| **Sale price** | What the property sold for |
| **Profit** | Sale price − cost, when the sale price is higher |
| **Loss** | Cost − sale price, when the sale price is lower |
| **Appreciation** | An increase in value over time, measured on the original value |
| **Depreciation (in value)** | A decrease in market value over time, measured on the original value |

Profit and appreciation are the same calculation as the percentage change in L14-1. The base is always the **starting** amount: what was paid, or the original value.

### Step 2: Units

The amounts are in dollars. The rates are percentages **of cost**. Two multipliers do most of the work:

| Situation | Multiplier | At 15% |
|---|---|---|
| Profit or appreciation | 1 + rate | 1.15 |
| Loss or depreciation | 1 − rate | 0.85 |

The multiplier turns the cost into the sale price in one step: a 15% profit means the sale price is 115% of the cost.

### Step 3: Formula

- **Profit (or loss) in dollars** = Sale − Cost
- **Profit or loss percentage = (Sale − Cost) ÷ Cost** (positive = profit, negative = loss)
- **Sale = Cost × (1 + profit%)**, or Sale = Cost × (1 − loss%)
- **Cost = Sale ÷ (1 + profit%)**, or **Cost = Sale ÷ (1 − loss%)**

For appreciation, replace "cost" with "original value". If a value changes by a percentage **each year**, apply the multiplier once per year, because each year's percentage is taken of that year's starting value.

### Step 4: When it applies

- **"Profit on cost", "on what she paid", "since purchase"** → the cost (original value) is the base. This is the normal case. If a question explicitly says the percentage is based on the sale price, follow the question.
- **Is the cost known?** Then multiply (to find the sale price) or divide the difference by the cost (to find the percentage).
- **Is the cost the unknown?** Then the percentage is "of" the unknown, so **divide** the sale price by (1 + profit%) or (1 − loss%). Divide, do not multiply.
- **Improvements.** If the question tells you to include money spent on improvements, add it to the cost before calculating.

### Step 5: Worked example

**A. Recover the cost (profit).** An investor sold a property for $276,000 at a 15% profit. What did she pay?

1. Sale = Cost × 1.15, so Cost = $276,000 ÷ 1.15 = **$240,000**.
2. Check: $240,000 × 1.15 = $276,000 ✓. Profit $36,000 ÷ $240,000 = 15% ✓.
3. **Never** $276,000 × 0.85 = $234,600. That takes 15% of the **sale price**, the wrong base.

**B. Loss percentage.** A Daytona Beach condo bought for $325,000 sells for $286,000.

1. Loss: $325,000 − $286,000 = $39,000.
2. $39,000 ÷ $325,000 (the cost) = 0.12 = **12% loss**.

**C. Recover the cost (loss).** A lot sold for $255,000, a 15% loss on what the owner paid.

1. Sale = Cost × 0.85, so Cost = $255,000 ÷ 0.85 = **$300,000**.
2. Check: $300,000 × 0.85 = $255,000 ✓.

**D. Appreciation over two years.** A $300,000 home appreciates 4% a year for 2 years.

1. Year 1: $300,000 × 1.04 = $312,000.
2. Year 2: $312,000 × 1.04 = **$324,480**.
3. Total gain: $24,480 ÷ $300,000 = **8.16%**, not 8%. The second year's 4% was taken of a larger base.

### Step 6: Reasonableness check

- **Profit:** the cost must be **less** than the sale price. **Loss:** the cost must be **more** than the sale price.
- Multiply back: cost × (1 ± rate) must give the sale price exactly.
- The correct cost and the trap answer are close. In Example A, $240,000 and $234,600 differ by only $5,400. Only the multiply-back check separates them reliably.

### Step 7: Common errors

| Error | Example | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Taking the percent of the sale price | $276,000 × 0.85 = $234,600 | $276,000 ÷ 1.15 = $240,000 |
| Adding the percent to the sale price | $276,000 × 1.15 = $317,400 | That moves in the wrong direction entirely |
| Using the sale price as the base for the % | $36,000 ÷ $276,000 ≈ 13% | Divide by the **cost**: $36,000 ÷ $240,000 = 15% |
| Dividing by (1 + %) for a loss | $255,000 ÷ 1.15 | Loss → divide by (1 − %) = 0.85 |
| Adding yearly percentages | 4% + 4% = 8% | Apply 1.04 twice = 8.16% |
| Confusing a fall in market value with tax depreciation | Treating a market loss like straight-line depreciation | Tax depreciation (residential rental 27.5 years, nonresidential 39 years, straight-line, never land; V-77) is a different topic |

### Step 10: Exam application

1. **Find the cost (or original value).** Is it given, or is it the unknown?
2. **Known cost:** percentage = (sale − cost) ÷ cost, or sale = cost × (1 ± rate).
3. **Unknown cost:** cost = sale ÷ (1 + profit%) or sale ÷ (1 − loss%).
4. **Multiply back** to confirm.

The answer choices usually include the trap figures. For "sold for $276,000 at a 15% profit", expect to see $234,600 (× 0.85) and $317,400 (× 1.15) next to the correct **$240,000**. When a percentage is "of" the unknown, divide.

## 4. Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Cost | The amount paid for the property; the base for profit or loss |
| Sale price | The amount received on the sale |
| Profit | Sale price − cost (sale above cost) |
| Loss | Cost − sale price (sale below cost) |
| Percentage profit (loss) on cost | (Sale − cost) ÷ cost |
| Appreciation | An increase in value over time, measured on the original value |
| Depreciation (market) | A decrease in value over time; not the same as tax depreciation |
| Multiplier | 1 + rate (gain) or 1 − rate (loss); converts the cost into the sale price |
| Compounding | Applying a yearly percentage to each year's new starting value |

## 5. Practical example

**Two clients in Orlando.**

**Carlos** tells you he sold a townhouse last year for **$295,000** "at an 18% profit". How much did he pay?

- Cost = $295,000 ÷ 1.18 = **$250,000**. Check: $250,000 × 1.18 = $295,000 ✓. Profit: $45,000.
- If you had computed $295,000 × 0.82 = $241,900, you would have overstated his profit by $8,100.

**Marta** bought a condo for **$380,000** and a recent valuation suggests **$437,000**. How much has it appreciated?

- Gain: $437,000 − $380,000 = $57,000. $57,000 ÷ $380,000 (the original value) = **15%**.
- Marta then asks: "If I sell at $437,000, what percentage of that price is my gain?" $57,000 ÷ $437,000 ≈ 13%. That is a percentage of the **sale price**, a different question with a different base. In exam language, her profit is 15% **on cost** (before any selling costs, such as the commission in L14-3).

## 6. Portugal comparison

**Familiar principle.** Profit, loss and appreciation percentages are pure arithmetic, so there is no meaningful difference between Portugal and Florida here. The course's comparison entries contain nothing specific to this lesson.

**Florida rule (exam).** Profit or loss is a percentage **of cost**: (sale − cost) ÷ cost. The cost is recovered by dividing.

**Precise difference.** None in the math. How a gain is **taxed** is a separate topic in each country. The US federal rules are taught in the tax lesson (for example, the home-sale exclusion, V-78). Portuguese tax rules on gains are not in the course's comparison entries and **require verification**.

**Exam implication.** Treat "profit" questions as arithmetic, not tax questions, unless the question mentions tax.

## 7. Legal and numerical requirements

**Memorize exactly**

- **Profit or loss % = (Sale − Cost) ÷ Cost** (measured on cost).
- **Cost = Sale ÷ (1 + profit%)**; for a loss, **Cost = Sale ÷ (1 − loss%)**.
- $276,000 at a 15% profit → **$240,000**, never $276,000 × 0.85 = $234,600.

**Understand**

- No statute is involved: this is the percent triangle with the cost as the total.
- When a percentage is "of" the unknown, divide.
- A gain followed by an equal percentage loss does not bring you back to the start, and yearly percentages compound.
- A fall in market value is not the same thing as tax depreciation (V-77).

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"A 15% profit means the cost was 85% of the sale price."** No. The **sale** is 115% of the cost, so divide by 1.15.
- **"Profit percentage is measured on the sale price."** No. It is measured **on cost** unless the question says otherwise.
- **"Up 10% then down 10% is break-even."** No. $250,000 → $275,000 → $247,500, a 1% loss.
- **"4% a year for two years is 8%."** No. Compounded, it is 8.16%.
- **"For a loss, divide by 1.15 too."** No. A 15% loss means dividing by **0.85**.

## 10. Memory sheet

- **Profit is on cost:** (Sale − Cost) ÷ Cost.
- **Cost known → multiply:** Sale = Cost × (1 ± rate).
- **Cost unknown → divide:** Cost = Sale ÷ (1 + profit%) or ÷ (1 − loss%).
- **$276,000 ÷ 1.15 = $240,000**, never × 0.85 ($234,600).
- Yearly percentages compound; equal % up then down ≠ break-even.
- When a percent is "of" the unknown, divide. Always multiply back.

## 3. Simple explanation

**Prorating** divides an expense or income between seller and buyer as of closing. Florida course conventions: the **365-day method** (actual days in each month) and **the buyer owns the day of closing** — unless the question says otherwise.

**Paid in arrears** (property taxes, mortgage interest): the bill comes later, so the seller owes the buyer for the seller's days (Jan 1 through the day before closing): **debit seller, credit buyer**.

**Paid in advance** (rent the seller collected, prepaid insurance or association dues): the buyer gets credit for unused rent (credit buyer, debit seller); the seller gets credit for prepaid expenses beyond closing (debit buyer, credit seller).

Worked example (manual): closing May 20; annual taxes $5,475 unpaid; daily $15. Seller's days: Jan 31 + Feb 28 + Mar 31 + Apr 30 + May 19 = 139 → **$2,085**, debit seller / credit buyer.

## 10. Memory sheet

365 days · buyer owns closing day · arrears → debit seller · advance rent → credit buyer.

## 3. Simple explanation

A **debit** is what a party owes; a **credit** is what a party receives.

| Item | Buyer | Seller |
|---|---|---|
| Purchase price | Debit | Credit |
| Binder deposit | Credit (single entry) | — |
| New mortgage | Credit | — |
| **Assumed mortgage** | **Credit** | **Debit** |
| Existing mortgage payoff | — | Debit |
| Deed doc stamps | — | Debit |
| Note stamps + intangible | Debit | — |
| Unpaid taxes (proration) | Credit | Debit |
| Commission | — | Debit (customarily) |

**Correction (V-24):** the study manual's table shows the assumed mortgage as a buyer credit only. An assumed mortgage is a **double entry**: the buyer is credited because it pays part of the price, and the seller is debited because the seller hands over the debt instead of receiving that cash.

**Cash due:** buyer brings total debits − total credits; seller receives total credits − total debits.

## 10. Memory sheet

Deposit: buyer credit only. New loan: buyer credit only. Assumed loan: debit seller + credit buyer. Prorations between parties: double entries.
