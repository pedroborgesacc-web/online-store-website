## 2. Why it matters

**In practice.** Almost every number you discuss with a Florida client is a percentage of something: the commission on a sale price, a down payment, an offer "5% under list price", or the change in a home's value since it was bought. You already do these calculations in Portugal. Here you must do them quickly, in US number notation, and with the decimal point in the right place.

**On the exam.** Section 14, "Real Estate Computations and Closing of Transactions", carries **6%** of the state exam (V-02). The study manual estimates about ten pure-arithmetic questions across the whole exam, and its twelve math patterns include mortgage, appraisal and tax problems as well as the topics of this chapter (manual; not yet verified). Most of them rest on the one idea in this lesson: the **percent triangle**. Learn it once and the commission, net-to-seller and profit lessons become variations on it.

## 3. Simple explanation

### Step 1: What it represents

Most exam math is one idea: **a part is a rate (a percentage) of a total.**

| Word | Meaning | Real estate examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Total** (also called the *base* or the *whole*) | The amount the percentage is taken **of** | Sale price, purchase cost, original value |
| **Rate** | The percentage, written as a decimal when you calculate | Commission rate, down-payment percentage, profit percentage |
| **Part** | The piece that results | Commission in dollars, down payment in dollars, profit in dollars |

Take a **6% commission on a $300,000 sale**. The total is $300,000, the rate is 6% (0.06) and the part is the commission: $300,000 × 0.06 = **$18,000**.

### Step 2: Units

**Percent** means "per hundred": 6% = 6/100 = 0.06. Before you multiply or divide, turn every percent into a decimal.

| Conversion | How | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Percent → decimal | Divide by 100: move the decimal point **two places left** | 6% = 0.06 · 0.5% = 0.005 · 125% = 1.25 |
| Decimal → percent | Multiply by 100: move the point **two places right** | 0.375 = 37.5% · 0.08 = 8% |
| Fraction → decimal | Divide the top number (numerator) by the bottom number (denominator) | 3/8 = 3 ÷ 8 = 0.375 = 37.5% |
| Decimal → fraction | Write it over 10, 100 or 1,000, then simplify | 0.25 = 25/100 = 1/4 |
| Percent → fraction | Write it over 100, then simplify | 12.5% = 12.5/100 = 125/1,000 = 1/8 |

**Equivalents worth knowing by heart:** 1/2 = 0.5 = 50% · 1/4 = 0.25 = 25% · 3/4 = 0.75 = 75% · 1/8 = 0.125 = 12.5% · 1/3 = 33⅓% ≈ 0.3333 · 2/3 = 66⅔% ≈ 0.6667.

**US notation.** The period is the decimal point and commas group the thousands: $1,250.50 is one thousand two hundred fifty dollars and fifty cents.

The part and the total always have the **same unit** (dollars with dollars). The rate has no unit.

### Step 3: Formula

**The percent triangle:** **Part = Total × Rate.** Rearranged:

- **Total = Part ÷ Rate**
- **Rate = Part ÷ Total**

Picture a triangle with **Part** at the top and **Total × Rate** at the bottom (Section 8). Cover the number you want. If the two numbers left are side by side, multiply them. If one sits above the other, divide the top one by the bottom one.

**Word clues:** "of" usually means multiply (6% *of* the price). "Is" or "was" means equals (the commission *was* $9,450). "What percent" asks for the rate.

**Percentage change** is a special case in which the total is the starting value:

**Percentage change = (new − old) ÷ old**

A positive result is an increase; a negative result is a decrease.

### Step 4: When it applies

1. Identify the **total the percentage is taken of**. This is the most important decision in all exam math.
2. If that total is known, **multiply** to get the part.
3. If the percentage is applied to the **unknown** (the total is what you are looking for), **divide**.
4. For a change over time, the base is always the **original** (old) value.

| The question says | Total (base) | Unknown | Operation |
|---|---|---|---|
| "a 6% commission on a $300,000 sale" | Sale price, known | Commission | Multiply: $300,000 × 0.06 |
| "the 6% commission was $9,450" | Sale price, unknown | Sale price | Divide: $9,450 ÷ 0.06 |
| "$41,250 down on a $275,000 home" | Price, known | Rate | Divide: $41,250 ÷ $275,000 |
| "value went from $80,000 to $92,000" | Old value, $80,000 | % change | Change ÷ old value |

### Step 5: Worked example

**A. Find the total.** A 6% commission was $9,450. What was the sale price?

1. Part = $9,450. Rate = 0.06. The total is unknown, so Total = Part ÷ Rate.
2. $9,450 ÷ 0.06 = **$157,500**.
3. Check: $157,500 × 0.06 = $9,450 ✓.

**B. Find the rate.** A buyer in Lakeland pays $41,250 down on a $275,000 home.

1. Rate = Part ÷ Total = $41,250 ÷ $275,000 = 0.15 = **15%**.
2. Check: $275,000 × 0.15 = $41,250 ✓.

**C. Percentage change.** A lot bought for $80,000 sells for $92,000.

1. Change = $92,000 − $80,000 = $12,000.
2. $12,000 ÷ $80,000 (the old value) = 0.15 = **+15%**.
3. Reverse the story: a fall from $92,000 to $80,000 is $12,000 ÷ $92,000 = 0.1304, about **−13%**.

The dollar change is the same, but the **base changes**, so the percentages differ.

### Step 6: Reasonableness check

- A commission is a small fraction of the price. If your "price" is smaller than the commission, you multiplied instead of divided. ($9,450 × 0.06 = $567, which cannot be the price of a house.)
- A rate below 100% always gives a part smaller than the total.
- Estimate with easy percentages: 10% means moving the point one place left, 1% two places. So 1% of $157,500 is $1,575, and 6% is about 6 × $1,575 = $9,450 ✓.
- The same dollar change is a bigger percentage of a smaller base.

### Step 7: Common errors

| Error | What it looks like | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Decimal placement | Using 0.6 for 6% (ten times too big) | 6% = 0.06; 0.6 = 60% |
| Not converting the percent | $9,450 ÷ 6 = $1,575 | Divide by 0.06, not by 6 |
| Wrong base | Measuring the fall from $92,000 against $80,000 | Divide the change by the **original** value |
| Multiplying when the percent is "of" the unknown | $9,450 × 0.06 | Divide: $9,450 ÷ 0.06 |
| Rounding too early | One-third of $240,000 using 0.33 gives $79,200 instead of $80,000 | Keep full decimals (or the fraction); round only the final answer |

### Step 10: Exam application

1. Read the last sentence first. Is the question asking for the **part**, the **total** or the **rate**?
2. Find the total the percentage is "of".
3. Convert the percent to a decimal.
4. Choose: **multiply** if the total is known; **divide** if the total is unknown or the rate is wanted.
5. Calculate with full decimals. Round only the final answer, the way the question asks.
6. Reverse-check by multiplying back.

Wrong answer choices are usually built from the typical mistakes: the multiply-instead-of-divide figure, the wrong-base figure and a misplaced decimal. If your answer matches a choice, still run the reverse check.

One rounding exception you will meet later: documentary stamp tax is charged per $100 "or portion", so you round the amount **up** to the next $100 before calculating (V-18).

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Percent (%) | "Per hundred": 6% = 6/100 = 0.06 |
| Decimal | A number written with a decimal point, such as 0.06 |
| Fraction | A top number (numerator) over a bottom number (denominator), such as 3/8 |
| Rate | The percentage used in a calculation, written as a decimal |
| Total (base, whole) | The amount the percentage is taken **of** |
| Part | The result of rate × total |
| Percent triangle | Part on top, Total × Rate below; cover the unknown to see the formula |
| Percentage change | (new − old) ÷ old |
| Original value | The starting amount; the base for any percentage change |

## 5. Practical example

**Rita**, a sales associate at a Tampa brokerage, meets sellers who bought their St. Petersburg home for **$320,000**.

1. **"How much has our home gained?"** Recent comparable sales suggest about **$352,000**. Change: $32,000 ÷ $320,000 (what they paid) = **+10%**.
2. **The offer.** They list at $352,000, and a buyer offers 5% below the list price. Discount: $352,000 × 0.05 = $17,600. Offer: $352,000 − $17,600 = **$334,400** (or $352,000 × 0.95).
3. **The buyer's down payment.** The buyer's lender letter shows $66,880 down. Rate: $66,880 ÷ $334,400 = 0.20 = **20%** down.
4. **The commission.** If the agreed total commission is 5.5% and the sale closes at $334,400: $334,400 × 0.055 = **$18,392**.

Each answer used the same triangle. The only real decision each time was: *what is the total?* The answer was the purchase cost for the gain, the list price for the discount, and the sale price for the down payment and the commission.

## 6. Portugal comparison

**Familiar principle.** Percentages, fractions and proportions are the same arithmetic everywhere. The course's Portugal–Florida comparison entries contain nothing that changes this lesson.

**Florida rule.** Exam numbers are written in US notation: a decimal **point** and **commas** between thousands ($1,250.50).

**Precise difference.** Only the notation. If you usually write a decimal comma (0,06) and a thousands point (1.250), train your eye: on the exam, 0.06 is six hundredths and 1,250 is one thousand two hundred fifty.

**Exam implication.** No Portuguese rule is involved. Read every number in US format and apply the triangle.

## 7. Legal and numerical requirements

**Memorize exactly**

- **Part = Total × Rate** · **Total = Part ÷ Rate** · **Rate = Part ÷ Total**.
- Percent → decimal: ÷ 100 (point two places left). Decimal → percent: × 100 (point two places right). Fraction → decimal: top ÷ bottom (3/8 = 0.375 = 37.5%).
- **Percentage change = (new − old) ÷ old.**
- Section 14 (computations and closing) = **6%** of the exam (V-02).

**Understand**

- No statute governs this lesson: it is arithmetic. The one real skill is choosing the right base.
- When the percent is taken **of the unknown**, divide.
- The base for a change is the **original** value, so a rise and a fall of the same dollar amount give different percentages.
- Round only at the end, unless the question or a tax rule says otherwise (documentary stamps: round up to the next $100, V-18).

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"6% is 0.6."** No. 6% = **0.06**; 0.6 is 60%.
- **"A percentage always means multiply."** No. When the percentage is taken of the unknown amount, **divide**.
- **"Percentage change is measured on the new value."** No. Always divide by the **original** value.
- **"A 15% rise is undone by a 15% fall."** No. The fall is measured on the larger base: $80,000 → $92,000 is +15%, but $92,000 → $80,000 is only about −13%.
- **"Rounding a rate early (1/3 → 0.33) is fine."** No. On $240,000 it gives $79,200 instead of $80,000: early rounding can shift the answer by hundreds of dollars. Round once, at the end.

## 10. Memory sheet

- **Part = Total × Rate.** Cover the unknown: Total = Part ÷ Rate; Rate = Part ÷ Total.
- Percent ↔ decimal: move the point **two places**. Fraction → decimal: **top ÷ bottom** (3/8 = 37.5%).
- **Percent of the unknown → divide.**
- **Change ÷ original**: $80,000 → $92,000 = +15%; back down = about −13%.
- Reverse-check every answer. Round only at the end.

## 2. Why it matters

**In practice.** Florida listings give living area in **square feet**, land in square feet or **acres**, and distances in **miles**. Lots on a street or on the water can be compared by the **front foot**, and carpet or concrete is often priced by the **square yard**. A buyer from Lisbon will ask you "how many square metres is that?", and a seller will ask "what is my lot worth per front foot compared with the one next door?". The first needs a quick metric conversion; the second is pure US-unit math.

**On the exam.** Area is one of the study manual's twelve math patterns (pattern 11). It belongs to Section 14, which carries **6%** of the exam (V-02). These questions test unit conversion as much as geometry: dividing square feet by 3 instead of 9 is the classic wrong answer.

## 3. Simple explanation

> **Source status.** The measurement constants below (square yard, acre, mile, square mile) come from the study manual and have not been independently checked by this course: **(manual; not yet verified, V-26)**. V-26 notes that they are standard definitions. The metric equivalents are verified arithmetic (1 ft = 0.3048 m exactly; PT-17).

### Step 1: What it represents

| Measure | What it measures | Units | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Length / perimeter** | Distance along a line, or all the way around a shape | Inches, feet (ft), yards (yd), miles | Fencing around a lot |
| **Area** | A flat surface | Square feet (sq ft), square yards (sq yd), acres | Living area, lot size |
| **Volume** | Space in three dimensions | Cubic feet, cubic yards | Concrete for a slab |
| **Front feet** | The length of a lot's side along the street or the water | Feet | A 75' × 150' lot has 75 front feet |

Symbols: **'** means feet and **"** means inches, so 75' × 150' means 75 feet by 150 feet.

### Step 2: Units

**Constants (manual; not yet verified, V-26)**

| Unit | Equals | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1 foot | 12 inches | Definition |
| 1 yard | 3 feet | Definition |
| 1 square yard | **9 square feet** | 3 ft × 3 ft |
| 1 cubic yard | **27 cubic feet** | 3 ft × 3 ft × 3 ft |
| 1 mile | **5,280 feet** | = 1,760 yards (5,280 ÷ 3) |
| 1 acre | **43,560 square feet** | Definition |
| 1 square mile | **640 acres** | Definition |

**Why 9 and not 3?** A square yard is 3 feet long *and* 3 feet wide, so it contains 3 × 3 = 9 square feet. A cubic yard adds a third dimension: 3 × 3 × 3 = 27.

**Direction rule.** Going from a **bigger** unit to a **smaller** one, multiply (5 acres × 43,560 = 217,800 sq ft). Going from a **smaller** unit to a **bigger** one, divide (12,600 sq ft ÷ 43,560 ≈ 0.29 acre).

*Metric aside (never used in exam math):* 1 sq ft ≈ 0.0929 m²; 1 acre ≈ 4,047 m² ≈ 0.405 ha; 1 mile ≈ 1.61 km.

### Step 3: Formula

| To find | Formula |
|---|---|
| Area of a rectangle (or square) | length × width |
| Area of a triangle | ½ × base × height (the height is measured at a right angle to the base) |
| Area of a composite shape | Split it into rectangles and triangles, compute each, then add (subtract any cut-out) |
| Perimeter | Add all the sides; for a rectangle, 2 × (length + width) |
| Volume (if asked) | length × width × height; cubic yards = cubic feet ÷ 27 |
| Price per front foot | price ÷ front feet |
| Price per sq ft (or per acre) | price ÷ sq ft (or ÷ acres) |
| Total price from a unit price | area in the price's unit × unit price |

### Step 4: When it applies

- **Same unit first.** Every dimension must be in the same unit before you multiply. Inches → feet: divide by 12 (6 in = 0.5 ft; 4 in ≈ 0.333 ft).
- **Match the price's unit.** If the price is per square yard, convert the area to square yards (÷ 9). If it is per acre, divide by 43,560.
- **Front feet.** The frontage is the dimension along the street or water. It is **usually the first number given**: a 75' × 150' lot has 75 front feet, and 150 is the depth. If the question tells you which side faces the street or water, use that side.
- **Triangles.** Use the perpendicular height, not a slanted side, and do not forget the ½.
- **Volume** only when a thickness or depth appears (a concrete slab, fill dirt).

### Step 5: Worked example

**A. Rectangle and price per square foot.** An Ocala lot is 90 ft × 140 ft and sells at $14 per sq ft.

1. Area: 90 × 140 = **12,600 sq ft**.
2. Price: 12,600 × $14 = **$176,400**.
3. In acres: 12,600 ÷ 43,560 = 0.289, about **0.29 acre**.

**B. Composite (L-shaped) house.** The main block is 50 ft × 32 ft and a wing is 18 ft × 14 ft.

1. Main block: 50 × 32 = 1,600 sq ft. Wing: 18 × 14 = 252 sq ft.
2. Total: 1,600 + 252 = **1,852 sq ft**.

**C. Triangle.** A corner parcel has a base of 180 ft and a height of 220 ft. Area: ½ × 180 × 220 = **19,800 sq ft**.

**D. Front foot.** An 80' × 125' lot sells for $112,000. Front feet = 80. Price per front foot: $112,000 ÷ 80 = **$1,400**.

**E. Volume.** A slab is 30 ft × 20 ft and 6 inches thick. Thickness: 6 ÷ 12 = 0.5 ft. Volume: 30 × 20 × 0.5 = 300 cubic ft. In cubic yards: 300 ÷ 27 ≈ **11.11 cubic yd**.

### Step 6: Reasonableness check

- The number of square yards is about **one-ninth** of the number of square feet, and the number of acres is far smaller still.
- A quarter acre is 43,560 ÷ 4 = **10,890 sq ft**, so a typical lot of about 10,000 sq ft is "a bit under a quarter acre". One acre is roughly a square about 209 ft on each side.
- For the same lot, the price per front foot is much larger than the price per square foot, because each front foot carries the whole depth behind it.
- Work backwards: 1,600 + 252 = 1,852 ✓; $1,400 × 80 = $112,000 ✓.

### Step 7: Common errors

| Error | Correction |
|---|---|
| Dividing sq ft by **3** to get sq yd | Divide by **9** (3 × 3) |
| Dividing cubic ft by 9 to get cubic yd | Divide by **27** (3 × 3 × 3) |
| Mixing feet and yards (or inches) in one multiplication | Convert every dimension to one unit first |
| Forgetting the **½** for a triangle | Area = ½ × base × height |
| Using the depth as the frontage | Front feet = the street or water side, usually the first number |
| Rounding acres early (0.29 instead of 0.289…) | Keep full decimals until the last step |

### Step 10: Exam application

1. **Sketch** the shape and label every dimension.
2. **Convert** all dimensions to one unit (usually feet).
3. **Compute the area** by shape. Split composite shapes; use ½ for triangles.
4. **Convert the area** to the unit of the price: ÷ 9 for square yards, ÷ 43,560 for acres.
5. **Multiply** by the unit price, or **divide** the price by the units (front feet, sq ft, acres).
6. **Check** by working backwards and by size: square yards ≈ one-ninth of square feet.

Expect wrong answer choices built from ÷ 3 instead of ÷ 9, from a missing ½, and from using the depth instead of the frontage.

## 5. Practical example

**A canal lot in Cape Coral.** A couple from Lisbon is looking at a lot with **80 feet on the canal** and **125 feet of depth**, listed at **$240,000**. A second canal lot nearby has 90 feet of frontage, is 110 feet deep, and is listed at $256,500.

| | Lot 1 | Lot 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Front feet | 80 | 90 |
| Price per front foot | $240,000 ÷ 80 = **$3,000** | $256,500 ÷ 90 = **$2,850** |
| Area | 80 × 125 = 10,000 sq ft | 90 × 110 = 9,900 sq ft |
| Price per sq ft | $240,000 ÷ 10,000 = $24.00 | $256,500 ÷ 9,900 ≈ $25.91 |
| Acres | 10,000 ÷ 43,560 ≈ 0.23 | 9,900 ÷ 43,560 ≈ 0.23 |

Lot 2 costs more in total but **less per front foot**, which is exactly the comparison front-foot pricing is designed for. Per square foot it is dearer, because its shallower depth gives it less area for a higher price. For the clients' intuition you add that Lot 1 is about 929 m² (10,000 × 0.0929). Then you return to feet: Florida works in US customary units, and the exam uses nothing else.

They also want a paver patio of 24 ft × 18 ft, quoted at $65 per square yard: 24 × 18 = 432 sq ft; 432 ÷ 9 = 48 sq yd; 48 × $65 = **$3,120**.

## 6. Portugal comparison

**Familiar principle.** In Portugal you work in the metric system: m² and hectares (1 ha = 10,000 m²) (PT-17).

## 2. Why it matters

**In practice.** Two questions come up in almost every listing appointment and every pay day. The seller asks: *"What do I have to sell for to walk away with $X?"* You ask: *"What will I actually receive from this commission?"* Both answers depend on doing the percentages in the right order and on the right base.

**On the exam.** The commission chain and net-to-seller are patterns 2 and 3 of the study manual's twelve math patterns. They sit in Section 14, which carries **6%** of the exam (V-02). The wrong answer choices are built from predictable mistakes, such as multiplying the net by 1.06 or applying the associate's split to the whole commission. If you know the mistakes, you can spot the trap.

## 3. Simple explanation

### Step 1: What it represents

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Total commission** | The brokerage fee for the sale; in exam problems, a percentage of the **sale price**. On the closing statement it is customarily a seller debit, that is, an amount the seller owes (manual; not yet verified). |
| **Listing brokerage** | The brokerage that took the listing (works with the seller) |
| **Cooperating (selling) brokerage** | The brokerage that brought the buyer |
| **Co-brokerage split** | How the total commission is divided between the two brokerages |
| **Associate split** | How a brokerage's share is divided between the brokerage and its sales associate |
| **Net to seller** | What the seller keeps after the commission and the other selling costs |

In exam problems the money flows **down a chain**: sale price → total commission → each brokerage's share → each associate's share.

### Step 2: Units

Everything is in dollars and percentages. The key point about units is that **each split percentage is a percentage of the amount just above it** in the chain, not of the sale price. "The associate receives 60%" means 60% of her brokerage's share.

Convert every percentage to a decimal: 6% = 0.06, half = 0.50, 60% = 0.60.

### Step 3: Formula

**Commission chain (multiply down):**

- Total commission = Price × Rate
- Brokerage share = Total commission × brokerage split
- Associate share = Brokerage share × associate split
- In one line: **Associate share = Price × Rate × brokerage split × associate split**

**Working backwards (divide up):** divide by each split in turn, then use Rate = Total commission ÷ Price, or Price = Total commission ÷ Rate.

**Net to seller:**

**Price = (Net + fixed costs) ÷ (1 − rate)**

**Why this works.** The price is 100%. The commission takes the rate (say 6%) **of the price**, so the seller keeps 94% of the price (1 − 0.06 = 0.94). That 94% must cover the seller's net plus any fixed dollar costs. So 0.94 × Price = Net + fixed costs, and dividing both sides by 0.94 gives the price. It is the percent triangle again: the percentage is taken of the unknown price, so you **divide**.

### Step 4: When it applies

- **Read each split carefully.** "The listing brokerage receives half" = 50% of the total. "The brokerage keeps 40%" means the associate receives 60%. With a split written as "70/30", read the wording to see who receives which part.
- **Net-to-seller** applies whenever the seller names an amount to keep **after** the commission.
- **Fixed costs** are amounts stated in dollars (for example, "$4,500 of other closing costs", or a mortgage payoff when the seller's target is the cash in hand). They go in the numerator, **before** dividing.
- **A cost stated as a percentage of the price** belongs with the rate, not in the numerator: a 6% commission plus a 1% fee means dividing by 1 − 0.07 = 0.93.
- **Rounding.** Follow the question (often "to the nearest dollar", which means ordinary rounding, up or down). After rounding, your check may differ from the target net by a few cents, in either direction; that is rounding, not an error.

### Step 5: Worked example

**A. Down the chain.** A Jacksonville home sells for $360,000 with a 5% total commission. The listing brokerage receives half, and the listing associate receives 70% of her brokerage's share.

1. Total commission: $360,000 × 0.05 = $18,000.
2. Listing brokerage: $18,000 × 0.50 = $9,000.
3. Listing associate: $9,000 × 0.70 = **$6,300**.
4. One line: 360,000 × 0.05 × 0.50 × 0.70 = $6,300 ✓.

**B. Up the chain.** The associate received $6,300, which was 70% of her brokerage's share. The brokerage's share was half of the total commission on a $360,000 sale. What was the rate?

1. Brokerage share: $6,300 ÷ 0.70 = $9,000.
2. Total commission: $9,000 ÷ 0.50 = $18,000.
3. Rate: $18,000 ÷ $360,000 = 0.05 = **5%**.

**C. Net to seller.** A seller wants to net $188,000 after a 6% commission, with no other costs.

1. Price = $188,000 ÷ (1 − 0.06) = $188,000 ÷ 0.94 = **$200,000**.
2. Check: $200,000 × 6% = $12,000; $200,000 − $12,000 = $188,000 ✓.
3. **The common error:** $188,000 × 1.06 = $199,280. That takes 6% of the net, not of the price, and leaves the seller short. At $199,280 the commission is $11,956.80, and the seller nets $187,323.20, which is **$676.80 short**.

**D. Net with fixed costs.** A seller wants to net $277,000 after a 6% commission and $5,000 of other closing costs.

1. Price = ($277,000 + $5,000) ÷ 0.94 = $282,000 ÷ 0.94 = **$300,000**.
2. Check: $300,000 − $18,000 commission − $5,000 costs = $277,000 ✓.

### Step 6: Reasonableness check

- Down the chain every number gets smaller: associate share < brokerage share < total commission < price.
- The net-to-seller price must be **more** than the net plus the costs, and more than (net + costs) × (1 + rate).
- **Always check by taking the commission from your answer:** price − (price × rate) − fixed costs should equal the net.
- Working backwards, each step should get **larger**. If a number shrinks, you multiplied instead of dividing.

### Step 7: Common errors

| Error | What it looks like | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Net × (1 + rate) | $188,000 × 1.06 = $199,280 | Net ÷ (1 − rate) = $200,000 |
| Net × (1 − rate) or net − commission | $188,000 × 0.94 = $176,720 | That is smaller than the net: impossible |
| Applying the associate's % to the total or the price | 60% of the whole commission | Apply it to the brokerage's share |
| Adding fixed costs after dividing | $188,000 ÷ 0.94 + costs | Add them **before** dividing |
| Reading "brokerage keeps 40%" as the associate's share | Associate gets 40% | Associate gets 60% |
| Multiplying when working backwards | $6,300 × 0.70 | Divide by each split |

### Step 10: Exam application

**Commission chain**

1. Write the chain: price → total → brokerage → associate.
2. Mark each split and what it is a percentage **of**.
3. Going down, multiply. Going up, divide.
4. Check that the numbers shrink going down and grow going up.

**Net to seller**

1. Add the fixed dollar costs to the net.
2. Subtract the rate (plus any other percentage-of-price costs) from 1.
3. Divide.
4. Check by taking the commission (and costs) from your answer.

If one answer choice equals net × 1.06 and another equals net ÷ 0.94, the first is the trap.

## 5. Practical example

**A listing appointment in Sarasota.** The sellers tell Joana, a listing associate, that they need **$464,000** after selling to buy their next home. Their other closing costs are estimated at **$6,000**. Joana's brokerage proposes a **6%** total commission, to be split 50/50 with any cooperating brokerage. Joana receives **60%** of her brokerage's share.

1. **Minimum price:** ($464,000 + $6,000) ÷ 0.94 = $470,000 ÷ 0.94 = **$500,000**.
2. **Check:** $500,000 × 0.06 = $30,000. $500,000 − $30,000 − $6,000 = $464,000 ✓.
3. **Why not "$470,000 plus 6%"?** $470,000 × 1.06 = $498,200, and at that price the sellers would net $498,200 × 0.94 − $6,000 = $462,308, which is $1,692 short.
4. **The chain at $500,000:** total $30,000 → listing brokerage $15,000 → Joana $15,000 × 0.60 = **$9,000**. The cooperating brokerage also receives $15,000. If the buyer's associate is on a 70% split, she receives $10,500.

Joana shows the sellers the check line. Clients trust a number they can verify.

## 2. Why it matters

**In practice.** Investors and homeowners measure success in percentages: *"I made 18% on that townhouse"*, *"our condo has gone up 15% since we bought it"*. When a seller says "I sold at a 15% profit for $276,000", you should be able to say at once what they paid. The answer is not $234,600.

**On the exam.** Profit on cost is pattern 4 of the study manual's twelve math patterns, and percentage of change is one of its two extra patterns. Both sit in Section 14, which carries **6%** of the exam (V-02). The manual calls percentages taken of **different bases** the single most-missed idea in exam math. The trap answer is usually waiting among the choices.

## 3. Simple explanation

### Step 1: What it represents

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Cost** | What the owner paid for the property (the *base* of every profit or loss percentage) |
| **Sale price** | What the property sold for |
| **Profit** | Sale price − cost, when the sale price is higher |
| **Loss** | Cost − sale price, when the sale price is lower |
| **Appreciation** | An increase in value over time, measured on the original value |
| **Depreciation (in value)** | A decrease in market value over time, measured on the original value |

Profit and appreciation are the same calculation as the percentage change in L14-1. The base is always the **starting** amount: what was paid, or the original value.

### Step 2: Units

The amounts are in dollars. The rates are percentages **of cost**. Two multipliers do most of the work:

| Situation | Multiplier | At 15% |
|---|---|---|
| Profit or appreciation | 1 + rate | 1.15 |
| Loss or depreciation | 1 − rate | 0.85 |

The multiplier turns the cost into the sale price in one step: a 15% profit means the sale price is 115% of the cost.

### Step 3: Formula

- **Profit (or loss) in dollars** = Sale − Cost
- **Profit or loss percentage = (Sale − Cost) ÷ Cost** (positive = profit, negative = loss)
- **Sale = Cost × (1 + profit%)**, or Sale = Cost × (1 − loss%)
- **Cost = Sale ÷ (1 + profit%)**, or **Cost = Sale ÷ (1 − loss%)**

For appreciation, replace "cost" with "original value". If a value changes by a percentage **each year** and each year's percentage is taken of that year's starting value (compounding), apply the multiplier once per year. If the question instead says the yearly percentage is taken of the **original** value (simple or straight-line appreciation: the same dollar amount every year), multiply the yearly rate by the number of years. Follow the question's wording.

### Step 4: When it applies

- **"Profit on cost", "on what she paid", "since purchase"** → the cost (original value) is the base. This is the normal case. If a question explicitly says the percentage is based on the sale price, follow the question.
- **Is the cost known?** Then multiply (to find the sale price) or divide the difference by the cost (to find the percentage).
- **Is the cost the unknown?** Then the percentage is "of" the unknown, so **divide** the sale price by (1 + profit%) or (1 − loss%). Divide, do not multiply.
- **Improvements.** If the question tells you to include money spent on improvements, add it to the cost before calculating.

### Step 5: Worked example

**A. Recover the cost (profit).** An investor sold a property for $276,000 at a 15% profit. What did she pay?

1. Sale = Cost × 1.15, so Cost = $276,000 ÷ 1.15 = **$240,000**.
2. Check: $240,000 × 1.15 = $276,000 ✓. Profit $36,000 ÷ $240,000 = 15% ✓.
3. **Never** $276,000 × 0.85 = $234,600. That takes 15% of the **sale price**, the wrong base.

**B. Loss percentage.** A Daytona Beach condo bought for $325,000 sells for $286,000.

1. Loss: $325,000 − $286,000 = $39,000.
2. $39,000 ÷ $325,000 (the cost) = 0.12 = **12% loss**.

**C. Recover the cost (loss).** A lot sold for $255,000, a 15% loss on what the owner paid.

1. Sale = Cost × 0.85, so Cost = $255,000 ÷ 0.85 = **$300,000**.
2. Check: $300,000 × 0.85 = $255,000 ✓.

**D. Appreciation over two years.** A $300,000 home appreciates 4% a year for 2 years, each year's 4% taken of that year's value (compounding).

1. Year 1: $300,000 × 1.04 = $312,000.
2. Year 2: $312,000 × 1.04 = **$324,480**.
3. Total gain: $24,480 ÷ $300,000 = **8.16%**, not 8%. The second year's 4% was taken of a larger base.
4. Contrast: if the question said the 4% each year is of the **original** value, the gain would be $300,000 × 0.04 × 2 = $24,000 (8%), for a value of $324,000.

### Step 6: Reasonableness check

- **Profit:** the cost must be **less** than the sale price. **Loss:** the cost must be **more** than the sale price.
- Multiply back: cost × (1 ± rate) must give the sale price exactly.
- The correct cost and the trap answer are close. In Example A, $240,000 and $234,600 differ by only $5,400. Only the multiply-back check separates them reliably.

### Step 7: Common errors

| Error | Example | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Taking the percent of the sale price | $276,000 × 0.85 = $234,600 | $276,000 ÷ 1.15 = $240,000 |
| Adding the percent to the sale price | $276,000 × 1.15 = $317,400 | That moves in the wrong direction entirely |
| Using the sale price as the base for the % | $36,000 ÷ $276,000 ≈ 13% | Divide by the **cost**: $36,000 ÷ $240,000 = 15% |
| Dividing by (1 + %) for a loss | $255,000 ÷ 1.15 | Loss → divide by (1 − %) = 0.85 |
| Adding yearly percentages that compound | 4% + 4% = 8% | Apply 1.04 twice = 8.16% (add the rates only if the question says each year's % is of the original value) |
| Confusing a fall in market value with tax depreciation | Treating a market loss like straight-line depreciation | Tax depreciation (residential rental 27.5 years, nonresidential 39 years, straight-line (equal amounts each year), never land; V-77) is a different topic |

### Step 10: Exam application

1. **Find the cost (or original value).** Is it given, or is it the unknown?
2. **Known cost:** percentage = (sale − cost) ÷ cost, or sale = cost × (1 ± rate).
3. **Unknown cost:** cost = sale ÷ (1 + profit%) or sale ÷ (1 − loss%).
4. **Multiply back** to confirm.

The answer choices usually include the trap figures. For "sold for $276,000 at a 15% profit", expect to see $234,600 (× 0.85) and $317,400 (× 1.15) next to the correct **$240,000**. When a percentage is "of" the unknown, divide.

## 4. Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Cost | The amount paid for the property; the base for profit or loss |
| Sale price | The amount received on the sale |
| Profit | Sale price − cost (sale above cost) |
| Loss | Cost − sale price (sale below cost) |
| Percentage profit (loss) on cost | (Sale − cost) ÷ cost |
| Appreciation | An increase in value over time, measured on the original value |
| Depreciation (market) | A decrease in value over time; not the same as tax depreciation |
| Multiplier | 1 + rate (gain) or 1 − rate (loss); converts the cost into the sale price |
| Compounding | Applying a yearly percentage to each year's new starting value |

## 5. Practical example

**Two clients in Orlando.**

**Carlos** tells you he sold a townhouse last year for **$295,000** "at an 18% profit". How much did he pay?

- Cost = $295,000 ÷ 1.18 = **$250,000**. Check: $250,000 × 1.18 = $295,000 ✓. Profit: $45,000.
- If you had computed $295,000 × 0.82 = $241,900, you would have overstated his profit by $8,100.

**Marta** bought a condo for **$380,000** and a recent valuation suggests **$437,000**. How much has it appreciated?

- Gain: $437,000 − $380,000 = $57,000. $57,000 ÷ $380,000 (the original value) = **15%**.
- Marta then asks: "If I sell at $437,000, what percentage of that price is my gain?" $57,000 ÷ $437,000 ≈ 13%. That is a percentage of the **sale price**, a different question with a different base. In exam language, her profit is 15% **on cost** (before any selling costs, such as the commission in L14-3).

## 6. Portugal comparison

**Familiar principle.** Profit, loss and appreciation percentages are pure arithmetic, so there is no meaningful difference between Portugal and Florida here. The course's comparison entries contain nothing specific to this lesson.

**Precise difference.** None in the math. How a gain is **taxed** is a separate topic in each country. The US federal rules are taught in the tax lesson (for example, the home-sale exclusion, V-78). Portuguese tax rules on gains are not in the course's comparison entries and **require verification**.

**Exam implication.** Treat "profit" questions as arithmetic, not tax questions, unless the question mentions tax.

## 7. Legal and numerical requirements

**Memorize exactly**

- **Profit or loss % = (Sale − Cost) ÷ Cost** (measured on cost).
- **Cost = Sale ÷ (1 + profit%)**; for a loss, **Cost = Sale ÷ (1 − loss%)**.
- $276,000 at a 15% profit → **$240,000**, never $276,000 × 0.85 = $234,600.

**Understand**

- No statute is involved: this is the percent triangle with the cost as the total.
- When a percentage is "of" the unknown, divide.
- A gain followed by an equal percentage loss does not bring you back to the start, and yearly percentages compound unless the question says each year's percentage is of the original value.
- A fall in market value is not the same thing as tax depreciation (V-77).

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"A 15% profit means the cost was 85% of the sale price."** No. The **sale** is 115% of the cost, so divide by 1.15.
- **"Profit percentage is measured on the sale price."** No. It is measured **on cost** unless the question says otherwise.
- **"Up 10% then down 10% is break-even."** No. $250,000 → $275,000 → $247,500, a 1% loss.
- **"4% a year for two years is always 8%."** No. When each year's 4% is taken of that year's value, it compounds to 8.16%. It is 8% only if the question says the 4% is of the original value each year.
- **"For a loss, divide by 1.15 too."** No. A 15% loss means dividing by **0.85**.

## 10. Memory sheet

- **Profit is on cost:** (Sale − Cost) ÷ Cost.
- **Cost known → multiply:** Sale = Cost × (1 ± rate).
- **Cost unknown → divide:** Cost = Sale ÷ (1 + profit%) or ÷ (1 − loss%).
- **$276,000 ÷ 1.15 = $240,000**, never × 0.85 ($234,600).
- Yearly percentages compound (unless the question says "of the original value"); equal % up then down ≠ break-even.
- When a percent is "of" the unknown, divide. Always multiply back.

## 2. Why it matters

In practice, the first thing a buyer asks is: *"How much cash do I need?"* The answer comes from three numbers you will calculate in this lesson: the loan the lender will make (set by the **loan-to-value ratio**), the **down payment**, and the cost of any **discount points**. A low appraisal changes all three, and the buyer, not the lender, pays the difference.

On the exam, Section 14 (Real Estate Computations and Closing of Transactions) carries 6% of the questions, about 6 of the 100. These four patterns are short and formula-based, which makes them easy points. They are also full of traps. The wrong answer choices are built from predictable mistakes: months left unconverted, the price used instead of a lower appraisal, or points charged on the price instead of on the loan.

## 3. Simple explanation

### Step 1: What it represents

| Calculation | The question it answers |
|---|---|
| **Simple interest** | What does it cost to borrow a sum for a period when interest is charged only on the original amount? |
| **First-payment amortization** | Of one monthly payment, how much is interest and how much reduces the debt? |
| **LTV (loan-to-value)** | What share of the property's value is the lender financing, and so how much must the buyer bring? |
| **Discount points** | How much does the borrower pay the lender up front, as a percent of the loan? |

An **amortized** loan is repaid in equal monthly payments. Each payment first covers that month's interest, and the rest reduces the **principal** (the amount still owed). Early in the loan most of the payment is interest. Each month the balance is a little lower, so the interest is a little lower and the principal portion is a little higher.

### Step 2: Units

- **Money:** US dollars, calculated to the cent.
- **Interest rates** are quoted **per year** (annual), written as a percent, unless the question clearly says otherwise. Convert the percent to a decimal: 6.5% = 0.065.
- **Time (T)** must be in **years**. Divide months by 12: 18 months = 1.5; 9 months = 0.75; 30 months = 2.5.
- **One month of interest** uses the annual rate ÷ 12.
- **LTV** is a percent: 0.90 = 90%.
- **Points:** 1 point = 1% of the **loan amount** (manual; not yet verified). 2.5 points = 0.025 × loan.

No metric conversion is involved here. The only "unit" traps are time and percentages.

### Step 3: Formula

| Pattern | Formula |
|---|---|
| Simple interest | **I = P × R × T** (Principal × annual Rate × Time in years) |
| Rearranged | P = I ÷ (R × T) · R = I ÷ (P × T) · T = I ÷ (P × R) |
| One month's interest | Balance × annual rate ÷ 12 |
| Principal paid | Monthly payment − that month's interest |
| New balance | Old balance − principal paid |
| LTV | Loan ÷ **lesser** of price or appraised value |
| Loan from an LTV | LTV% × lesser of price or appraised value |
| Down payment | **Price** − loan |
| Cost of points | Points% × **loan amount** |
| Yield rule (course) | Each point ≈ +1/8% (0.125%) lender yield (manual; not yet verified, V-26) |

**Why the lesser value?** The lender's protection is the property. If the buyer agrees to pay $400,000 and the appraiser says the home is worth $390,000, the property is only worth $390,000 as security, so the lender lends a percentage of $390,000. If the appraisal is *higher* than the price, the lender still uses the price, because it is the lesser of the two. The **down payment** is always measured against the **price**, because the seller must receive the full price no matter how much the lender lends.

### Step 4: When it applies

- **Simple interest:** short-term loans, seller financing for part of a year, or any question that says "simple interest".
- **Amortization:** a fixed monthly payment on a mortgage. Questions normally *give* you the payment; your job is to split it into interest and principal.
- **LTV:** any question that gives a lender's percentage ("an 80% loan") together with a price and an appraisal.
- **Points:** any question where the lender charges points, or where a question asks how many points raise the yield by a given amount (course rule above).

### Step 5: Worked example

**A. Simple interest.** $60,000 borrowed at 8% per year for 9 months.
T = 9 ÷ 12 = 0.75 → I = 60,000 × 0.08 × 0.75 = **$3,600**.

**B. First payment.** A $300,000 loan at 6.5% with a monthly principal-and-interest payment of $1,896.20.
Interest = 300,000 × 0.065 ÷ 12 = **$1,625.00** → principal = 1,896.20 − 1,625.00 = **$271.20** → new balance = 300,000 − 271.20 = **$299,728.80**.

**C. LTV and down payment.** Price $400,000; appraisal $390,000; the lender makes a 90% LTV loan.
Lesser value = $390,000 → loan = 390,000 × 0.90 = **$351,000** → down payment = 400,000 − 351,000 = **$49,000**.

**D. Points.** 1.75 points on a $304,000 loan: 304,000 × 0.0175 = **$5,320**.
Yield version (course rule, not yet verified): a lender wants a 7% yield on a 6.75% loan. The gap is 0.25%; 0.25 ÷ 0.125 = **2 points**. On a $250,000 loan that costs 250,000 × 0.02 = **$5,000**.

### Step 6: Reasonableness check

- Interest for less than a year must be **less** than one full year's interest: 60,000 × 0.08 = $4,800 for a year, so $3,600 for 9 months makes sense.
- One month's interest is about 1/12 of a year's interest. Early in a long-term loan the principal portion is small ($271.20 against $1,625).
- The loan cannot exceed the lesser value, and with a 90% LTV the down payment is **at least** 10% of the price. It is more when the appraisal is low ($49,000 > $40,000 ✓).
- Each point is 1% of the loan, so 1.75 points on about $300,000 is roughly $5,250. $5,320 is close ✓.

### Step 7: Common errors

| Error | What goes wrong | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Using T = 18 for 18 months | Interest 12 times too large | T in years: 1.5 |
| Forgetting ÷ 12 for one month | You get a year's interest | Balance × rate ÷ 12 |
| Treating the whole payment as principal | Balance falls far too fast | Subtract the interest first |
| LTV on the higher value | Loan too large | Lesser of price or appraisal |
| Down payment = appraisal − loan | Cash needed understated | Price − loan |
| Points on the price | Cost overstated | Points × loan |

### Step 10: Exam application

- **Read the time unit first.** If the question says months, divide by 12 before anything else.
- **LTV questions:** find both "price" and "appraised value". The loan uses the lower one; the down payment uses the price.
- **First-payment questions** usually ask for the *principal* portion. Expect the wrong answers to include the month's interest and the full payment.

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"18 months means T = 18."** No. T is in years: 18 ÷ 12 = 1.5.

- **"A point is 1% of the price."** A point is 1% of the **loan**.

## 2. Why it matters

A Florida sale closed with a deed normally pays **documentary stamp tax** on that deed. When the buyer finances, there are two more taxes: documentary stamps on the **note** and the **intangible tax** on the mortgage. Sellers ask "what will I net?" and buyers ask "what are my closing costs?", and you cannot answer either question without these three figures. They also appear as lines on the closing statement (lesson L14-8).

For the exam, these are Florida-specific rates that you must know exactly. The questions test four things: the rate, the base (price or loan), the **rounding up** to the next $100, and who customarily pays. Wrong answer choices are built from rounding down, the wrong base, or the intangible tax charged on an assumed loan.

## 3. Simple explanation

### Step 1: What it represents

Florida collects three taxes on the documents of a typical financed sale:

| Tax | What it taxes | Source (verified) |
|---|---|---|
| **Documentary stamp tax on the deed** ("deed stamps") | The deed, measured by the **total consideration**, meaning the full price, including any existing mortgage the buyer takes over | s. 201.02; Miami-Dade surtax s. 201.031 (V-18) |
| **Documentary stamp tax on the note** ("note stamps") | Promissory notes and other written obligations to pay money | s. 201.08 (V-18) |
| **Nonrecurring intangible tax** | Obligations secured by a mortgage or lien on Florida real property: a **one-time** tax paid when the mortgage is recorded | s. 199.133 (V-19) |

### Step 2: Units

- **"Per $100 or portion thereof"** (V-18): count how many $100 units the amount contains. Any part of a $100 unit counts as a full unit. In practice: **round the amount UP to the next $100**, then divide by 100. An amount already in whole hundreds (such as $330,000) stays as it is.
- **Mill** = one-thousandth = $1 per $1,000 = 0.001. **2 mills = 0.002**.
- The intangible tax is **not rounded**. Multiply the exact loan amount by 0.002.
- All results are in dollars and cents.

### Step 3: Formula

| Tax | Formula |
|---|---|
| Deed stamps | (Price rounded UP to next $100) ÷ 100 × **$0.70** |
| Deed stamps, Miami-Dade, single-family residence | Same, × **$0.60** |
| Deed stamps, Miami-Dade, other property | Same, × ($0.60 + $0.45 surtax) = **$1.05** |
| Note stamps | (Loan rounded UP to next $100) ÷ 100 × **$0.35** |
| Intangible tax | New mortgage amount × **0.002** (no rounding) |

### Step 4: When it applies

| Situation | Deed stamps | Note stamps | Intangible tax |
|---|---|---|---|
| All-cash sale | Yes, on full price | No | No |
| Sale with a **new** mortgage | Yes, on full price | Yes, on the new loan | Yes, on the new loan |
| Buyer **assumes** the seller's mortgage | Yes, on full price; the assumed balance counts as consideration (V-18) | Yes, on the assumed balance: course convention, confirmed by the Florida Department of Revenue, DOR (V-25) | **No**: not due again on an assumed mortgage when it was paid originally and the debt is unchanged (s. 199.145(3), V-25) |

Two more qualifications:

- **Miami-Dade:** deed stamps are $0.60 per $100 instead of $0.70. The $0.45 surtax applies to documents **other than** those involving only a single-family residence, so for that other property the combined rate is $1.05 per $100 (V-18; s. 125.0167 limits the surtax to Miami-Dade).
- **Cap on note stamps:** the $2,450 cap applies **only to unsecured documents**. A note secured by a mortgage has **no cap** (V-18).

**Who pays (by custom):** the seller customarily pays deed stamps; the buyer customarily pays note stamps and intangible tax on financing. The sales contract can allocate them differently. (Customary allocation: manual; not yet verified. V-18 and V-19 verified the rates and bases, not who pays.)

### Step 5: Worked example

**Manual example, Broward County:** $412,480 sale; buyer takes a new $330,000 loan.

| Tax | Work | Result | Customarily paid by |
|---|---|---|---|
| Deed stamps | $412,480 → $412,500 → 4,125 × $0.70 | **$2,887.50** | Seller |
| Note stamps | $330,000 (already a whole $100) → 3,300 × $0.35 | **$1,155.00** | Buyer |
| Intangible tax | 330,000 × 0.002 | **$660.00** | Buyer |

### Step 6: Reasonableness check

- Deed stamps are about **0.7% of the price**: 412,480 × 0.007 ≈ 2,887 ✓.
- Note stamps on the same amount are **half** the deed rate; intangible tax is **0.2%** of the loan, which is always less than the note stamps.
- **Divisibility check:** a correct deed-stamp answer divided by 0.70 (0.60 or 1.05 in Miami-Dade) gives a whole number (2,887.50 ÷ 0.70 = 4,125 ✓). A note-stamp answer divided by 0.35 gives a whole number. If it does not, you skipped the rounding. (A rounded-*down* answer passes this check, so also confirm you went **up**.)
- When the price is not a round $100, the right answer is slightly **higher** than the "plain" multiplication, never lower.

## 6. Portugal comparison

**Familiar principle (PT-09):** in Portugal the **buyer** pays IMT (progressive, on the higher of the price and the taxable property value) and Imposto do Selo at 0.8%. Young buyers up to 35 have exemptions up to set limits (330,539 € in the 2026 tables for full exemption). **Precise difference:** the payer is reversed for the main tax, and Florida's rates are **flat per $100** and rounded **up** to the next $100, with no progressive brackets.

**Example:** a $350,050 sale in Orange County → round up to $350,100 → 3,501 × $0.70 = **$2,450.70** in deed stamps, normally charged to the seller.

**Exam implication:** do not let the Portuguese reflex ("the buyer pays the transfer tax") answer a Florida question. Round stamps up; charge intangible tax only on new money.

## 7. Legal and numerical requirements

**Memorize exactly**

- Deed stamps: **$0.70 per $100 or portion thereof** of the total consideration; round **UP** to the next $100; full price, including any mortgage assumed (s. 201.02, V-18).
- Miami-Dade: **$0.60** per $100 for a single-family residence; other property $0.60 + **$0.45** surtax = $1.05 (ss. 201.02 and 201.031, V-18). The manual gives these figures and V-18 confirms them.
- Note stamps: **$0.35 per $100 or portion thereof**, on new **and** assumed mortgages (s. 201.08; course convention confirmed by V-25). Cap of $2,450 only for unsecured documents; no cap for mortgage-secured notes (V-18).
- Intangible tax: **2 mills (0.002)** × the **new** mortgage; no rounding; one-time, paid when the mortgage is recorded (s. 199.133, V-19). Not due again on an assumed mortgage when it was paid originally and the debt is unchanged (s. 199.145(3), V-25).
- Customarily: seller pays deed stamps; buyer pays note stamps + intangible tax (manual; not yet verified). Contracts can allocate differently.

**Understand**

- "Or portion thereof" is the legal reason for rounding up: a partial $100 is taxed as a whole $100.
- The deed is taxed on everything the seller receives, including debt the buyer takes over. That is why an assumed mortgage is part of the deed-stamp base.
- The intangible tax is a one-time tax on the mortgage debt, so an unchanged debt that already paid it is not taxed again. The note stamps, by contrast, apply to the assumed obligation (V-25).

- **"Deed stamps are on the cash the seller receives."** No. They are on the full consideration, including any assumed mortgage.

## 2. Why it matters

At every closing, some bills cover a period that the seller and the buyer **share**. Property taxes cover the calendar year, rent covers the month, and association dues may cover the whole year. **Prorating** makes each party pay only for its own days. In practice, prorations decide a few thousand dollars on most closing statements, and buyers and sellers will ask you to explain them.

On the exam, proration questions test three skills: counting days correctly, applying the course convention for the closing day, and deciding **who is debited and who is credited**. That last decision is also asked on its own, without any arithmetic. These lines then reappear on the closing statement (L14-8).

## 3. Simple explanation

### Step 1: What it represents

A **proration** divides an expense or an income between seller and buyer as of the closing date. The question behind every proration is: *someone has paid, or will pay, a bill that covers both owners' days. How much does the other owner owe them?*

- **Paid in arrears** means the bill is paid at the **end** of the period it covers. Florida property taxes and mortgage interest work this way. At closing the bill is usually **unpaid**, so the seller owes the buyer for the seller's days.
- **Paid in advance** means the bill is paid at the **start** of the period. Rent, insurance premiums and association dues work this way. At closing someone has already paid for days that belong to the other party.

Why are property taxes in arrears? Florida assesses property as of **January 1**, and the tax bill for that year is **due November 1** (V-76). For most of the year, the current year's taxes are unpaid. The buyer, who owns the property in November, will pay the whole year's bill. So the seller must give the buyer the seller's share at closing.

### Step 2: Units

- **Days.** The **365-day method** uses the actual days in each month: Jan 31, Feb 28 (non-leap year), Mar 31, Apr 30, May 31, Jun 30, Jul 31, Aug 31, Sep 30, Oct 31, Nov 30, Dec 31.
- **Daily rate** for an annual item = annual amount ÷ 365. For a monthly item (rent, one month's interest), daily rate = that month's amount ÷ the actual days in **that** month.
- **Money:** dollars, rounded to the cent only at the **end**. Keep the daily rate unrounded.

### Step 3: Formula

| Quantity | Formula |
|---|---|
| Daily rate (annual item) | Annual amount ÷ 365 |
| Daily rate (monthly item) | Monthly amount ÷ days in that month |
| Seller's days | Jan 1 (or the 1st of the month) **through the day before closing** |
| Buyer's days | **Closing day** through Dec 31 (or the end of the month) |
| Seller's share | Daily rate × seller's days |
| Buyer's share | Daily rate × buyer's days, or total − seller's share |

The **course convention** (V-79, unresolved): a 365-day year and **the buyer owns the day of closing**, unless the question says otherwise. No statute or rule sets these. They come from the contract and the course. The manual says the FR/BAR contract (the Florida Realtors/Florida Bar residential sale contract, a privately published form, not a statute or rule) prorates through the day before closing. That form could not be checked against an official source, so this statement stays flagged (V-79, unresolved).

### Step 4: When it applies

Decide the **direction** first by asking two questions: *Has the bill been paid yet? Who paid it?*

| Item | Paid | Typical status at closing | Days to compute | Entry |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Property taxes, current year | In arrears | Unpaid | Seller's days | **Debit seller, credit buyer** |
| Property taxes already paid by the seller (late-year closing) | Already paid | Seller paid the full year | Buyer's days | Debit buyer, credit seller |
| Interest on a mortgage the buyer assumes | In arrears | Current month unpaid | Seller's days | **Debit seller, credit buyer** |
| Rent the seller collected | In advance | Seller holds the buyer's days | Buyer's days | **Credit buyer, debit seller** |
| Prepaid insurance or association dues | In advance | Seller paid beyond closing | Buyer's days | **Debit buyer, credit seller** |

The rule behind every row: **the party who is out of pocket is credited, and the other party is debited by the same amount.** A party is out of pocket when it paid (or will pay) an expense that covers the other party's days, or when the other party collected income that belongs to its days.

**Other methods.** The FREC syllabus expects both the **360-day** and the **365-day** methods (V-79). In a 360-day method every month counts as 30 days and the daily rate = annual ÷ 360. Example: taxes of $3,600, closing March 10. 365-day: 3,600 ÷ 365 × 68 days (31 + 28 + 9) = **$670.68**. 360-day: $10 × 69 days (30 + 30 + 9) = **$690**. Always use the method the question states. If it states none, use the course convention.

### Step 5: Worked example

**A. Unpaid taxes (manual example).** Closing May 20; annual taxes $5,475, unpaid.
Daily rate = 5,475 ÷ 365 = **$15**. Seller's days = Jan 31 + Feb 28 + Mar 31 + Apr 30 + May 19 = **139**. Seller's share = 139 × 15 = **$2,085** → **debit seller, credit buyer**.

**B. Rent collected in advance.** The seller collected June rent of $2,100 on June 1; closing is June 21.
Daily rate = 2,100 ÷ 30 = $70. Buyer's days = June 21–30 = **10**. Buyer's share = 10 × 70 = **$700** → **credit buyer, debit seller**.

**C. Interest on an assumed loan.** The buyer assumes a $252,000 loan at 5%; interest is paid in arrears. Closing is September 19.
September interest = 252,000 × 0.05 ÷ 12 = $1,050. Daily = 1,050 ÷ 30 (September's days) = $35. Seller's days = Sept 1–18 = **18**. Seller's share = 18 × 35 = **$630** → **debit seller, credit buyer**.

### Step 6: Reasonableness check

- **Seller's days + buyer's days = 365** (example A: 139 + 226 = 365 ✓). For a month: they add up to the days in that month (B: 20 + 10 = 30 ✓).
- **Seller's share + buyer's share = the total bill** (A: 2,085 + 3,390 = 5,475 ✓).
- A closing near mid-year gives about half the bill to each party. A May 20 closing gives the seller a bit under 40%, and $2,085 is about 38% of $5,475 ✓.
- Direction check: for unpaid taxes, the buyer will pay the whole bill, so the buyer must be **credited**.

### Step 7: Common errors

| Error | Effect | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Giving the closing day to the seller | Seller's days one too many | Seller: Jan 1 to the day **before** closing |
| Using 30-day months when the question says 365 | Wrong day count | Actual days per month |
| Reversing the entry for unpaid taxes | Buyer debited | Arrears → debit seller, credit buyer |
| Computing the buyer's share when asked for the seller's | Wrong figure | Read who the question asks about |
| Rounding the daily rate too early | Off by cents | Round only the final answer |
| Monthly item divided by 365 | Wrong daily rate | Month's amount ÷ days in that month |

Rounding example: May rent $1,850, closing May 25, buyer's days 7. Correct: 1,850 ÷ 31 × 7 = **$417.74**. Rounding the daily rate to $59.68 first gives $417.76.

### Step 10: Exam application

1. Identify the item: **arrears** (taxes, mortgage interest) or **advance** (rent, insurance, dues).
2. Ask: *paid or unpaid? by whom?* Then choose the direction from the Step 4 table.
3. Use the method the question states. If it states none: 365 days, buyer owns the closing day.
4. Count the days covered by the amount that changes hands: unpaid taxes or interest → seller's days; prepaid dues or insurance → buyer's days; rent the seller collected → buyer's days.
5. Multiply, round at the end, and check that the days and shares add up.

A question may ask only for the entry, for example "How do unpaid taxes appear?" The answer is **debit seller, credit buyer**.

## 5. Practical example

**An investor buys a tenant-occupied house in Orlando.** Closing is **August 12** (non-leap year); the buyer owns the day of closing (course convention, V-79).

| Item | Facts | Work | Amount | Entry |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Property taxes | $6,205 for the year, unpaid | 6,205 ÷ 365 = $17/day × 223 seller's days (Jan 1 – Aug 11) | **$3,791** | Debit seller, credit buyer |

- The party who is out of pocket (it paid an expense covering the other's days, or the other collected income belonging to its days) is credited, and the other party is debited by the same amount.
- No verification entry covers the debit/credit directions. They follow from who has paid, as the manual and the course's practice questions teach.
- If a question says the seller has *already paid* the taxes, the direction reverses: debit buyer, credit seller for the buyer's days.

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"The seller owns the closing day."** Under the course convention, the buyer does, so the seller's days stop the day before closing (V-79).
- **"The 365-day method is Florida law."** It is a contract and course convention. Follow the question's method if it gives one.
- **"Unpaid taxes are the buyer's debit because the buyer pays them later."** No. Because the buyer will pay the whole bill, the buyer is **credited** for the seller's share.
- **"Rent collected in advance is a credit to the seller."** The seller holds the buyer's money, so the entry is **credit buyer, debit seller**.
- **"Prorations are single entries."** Prorations between buyer and seller are **double entries**.

## 10. Memory sheet

- **365 days · buyer owns closing day** (course convention, V-79, not law; follow the question).
- Seller's days = Jan 1 → day before closing. Buyer's days = closing day → Dec 31.
- **Arrears (taxes, interest) unpaid → debit seller, credit buyer.**
- **Advance rent → credit buyer, debit seller.**
- Prepaid dues or insurance → debit buyer, credit seller.
- Check: days add to 365; shares add to the bill. Round only at the end.

## 2. Why it matters

The closing statement is where everything in this chapter comes together: the price, the deposit, the loan, the transfer taxes (L14-6), the prorations (L14-7) and the commission. Buyers want to know the exact **cash to close**, and sellers want to know their **net proceeds**. As a sales associate you will review these figures before closing and explain each line.

The exam usually asks for **one cell** of the statement, for example "How does the binder deposit appear?" or "How is an assumed mortgage entered?" It also asks you to compute the buyer's cash due or the seller's net. Each line follows from simple logic, so learn the logic rather than memorizing a table.

## 3. Simple explanation

### Step 1: What it represents

A **closing statement** (settlement statement) is the accounting of the sale for each party. Each party has two columns:

- **Debit:** an amount the party **owes** at closing (is charged).
- **Credit:** an amount the party **receives** at closing, or has already paid toward what it owes.

At the bottom, the buyer's debits minus credits is the cash the buyer must bring. The seller's credits minus debits is the cash the seller takes home.

### Step 2: Units

- **Money:** dollars and cents.
- **Entries:** each line goes into one or more cells (buyer debit, buyer credit, seller debit, seller credit).
- **Single entry:** the line appears for **one party only**, because the money moves between that party and an outsider (the lender, the tax collector, the brokerage, the title company).
- **Double entry:** the line appears for **both parties**, as a debit to one and a credit to the other for the same amount, because money or value (such as a debt taken over) moves **between buyer and seller**.

### Step 3: Formula

| Result | Formula |
|---|---|
| Cash due **from the buyer** | Total buyer debits − total buyer credits |
| Cash due **to the seller** | Total seller credits − total seller debits |

If the seller's debits are larger than the seller's credits, the result is negative and the seller must bring money to closing.

### Step 4: When it applies

Ask for each line: **does money or value move between buyer and seller (double entry), or between one party and an outsider (single entry)?**

| Item | Buyer | Seller | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Purchase price | Debit | Credit | Buyer owes it; seller receives it (double) |
| Binder (earnest money) deposit | **Credit** (single entry) | — | Already paid into escrow; counts toward the price. The seller is already credited with the full price |
| New mortgage | **Credit** | — | Lender's money pays part of the price for the buyer (single) |
| **Assumed mortgage** | **Credit** | **Debit** | Double entry (V-24, corrected) |
| Existing mortgage payoff | — | Debit | Seller's own debt paid to the lender from proceeds (single) |
| Deed doc stamps | — | Debit | Customarily the seller's tax (single) |
| Note stamps + intangible tax | Debit | — | Customarily the buyer's taxes on financing (single) |
| Unpaid taxes (proration) | Credit | Debit | Seller owes the buyer for the seller's days (double) |
| Accrued interest on an assumed loan | Credit | Debit | Paid in arrears; seller owes the buyer (double) |
| Rent the seller collected in advance | Credit | Debit | Seller holds the buyer's days (double) |
| Prepaid dues or insurance paid by the seller | Debit | Credit | Buyer owes the seller for days after closing (double) |
| Commission | — | Debit (customarily) | Seller customarily pays the listing brokerage (single) |
| Buyer's other closing costs (lender, title) | Debit | — | Paid to outsiders (single) |
| Seller's other closing costs | — | Debit | Paid to outsiders (single) |

**Correction (V-24):** the study manual's table shows the assumed mortgage as a **buyer credit only**. That is wrong. An assumed mortgage is a **double entry**. The buyer is credited because the debt the buyer takes over pays part of the price. The seller is debited because the seller hands over the debt instead of receiving that amount in cash. Compare: a **new** mortgage is a buyer credit only, and the seller's **payoff** of an existing loan is a seller debit only.

### Step 5: Worked example

**An assumption sale (outside Miami-Dade).** Price $200,000; deposit $10,000; the buyer assumes the seller's $120,000 loan; unpaid taxes proration $1,500; commission 6% = $12,000; deed stamps 2,000 × $0.70 = $1,400; note stamps on the assumed note (course convention, V-25) 1,200 × $0.35 = $420; intangible tax $0 (not new money).

| Line | Buyer debit | Buyer credit | Seller debit | Seller credit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Price | 200,000 | | | 200,000 |
| Deposit | | 10,000 | | |
| Assumed mortgage | | 120,000 | 120,000 | |
| Taxes (proration) | | 1,500 | 1,500 | |
| Commission | | | 12,000 | |
| Deed stamps | | | 1,400 | |
| Note stamps | 420 | | | |
| **Totals** | **200,420** | **131,500** | **134,900** | **200,000** |

Buyer brings 200,420 − 131,500 = **$68,920**. Seller receives 200,000 − 134,900 = **$65,100**.

### Step 6: Reasonableness check

- **Quick estimate for the buyer:** start with price − loan − deposit, then add the buyer's other debits and subtract the buyer's other credits. Here 200,000 − 120,000 − 10,000 = 70,000, plus 420 and minus 1,500 = 68,920 ✓.
- **Money in = money out.** Cash coming in (buyer's cash + deposit + any new loan) must equal cash going out (seller's net + payoffs + commission + taxes + other costs). Here, in: 68,920 + 10,000 = 78,920. Out: 65,100 + 12,000 + 1,400 + 420 = 78,920 ✓. Double entries between buyer and seller cancel out and never appear in this check.

### Step 7: Common errors

| Error | Correct |
|---|---|
| Deposit as a seller credit (or a double entry) | Buyer credit only. The seller is already credited with the full price |
| Assumed mortgage as a buyer credit only (the manual's table) | **Debit seller, credit buyer** (V-24) |
| New mortgage as a seller debit | Buyer credit only. The seller's own old loan is the payoff |
| Unpaid-tax proration as debit buyer | Debit seller, credit buyer |
| Seller's payoff on the buyer's side | Seller debit only |
| Subtracting in the wrong order | Buyer: debits − credits. Seller: credits − debits |
| Forgetting the deposit when computing the buyer's cash | The deposit is a credit and reduces the cash due |

### Step 10: Exam application

- **One-cell questions:** decide whether money or value moves between the parties (double) or to or from an outsider (single), then who owes and who receives.
- **Most-tested cells:** deposit = buyer credit only. New loan = buyer credit only. Assumed loan = debit seller + credit buyer. Payoff = seller debit only. Unpaid taxes = debit seller, credit buyer. Commission = seller debit (customarily).
- **Cash questions:** write two lists, debits and credits, for the party asked about. Do not mix in the other party's lines.
- If a practice source shows an assumed mortgage as a buyer credit only, it reflects the manual's error. Answer **debit seller, credit buyer**. (If a question asks only about the buyer's side, the buyer's entry is a credit.)

## 6. Portugal comparison

**Familiar principle:** in any sale, someone must account for the price, the loans and the taxes so each party knows what it pays and receives.

**Florida rule:** on the Florida closing statement, deed stamps are customarily a **seller debit**, and note stamps and intangible tax are **buyer debits** on financing.

**Precise difference (PT-09):** in Portugal the buyer pays IMT and Imposto do Selo, so the main transfer taxes sit on the buyer's side. In Florida the main deed tax customarily sits on the **seller's** side. How a Portuguese sale is documented and settled between the parties is not covered by this course's comparison entries and **requires verification**.

**Exam implication:** place deed stamps as a seller debit unless the question says the contract allocates them differently.

## 7. Legal and numerical requirements

**Memorize exactly**

- **Debit** = what a party owes. **Credit** = what a party receives.
- Price: debit buyer, credit seller. Deposit: **credit buyer only** (single). New mortgage: **credit buyer only**. Payoff: **debit seller only**.
- **Assumed mortgage: debit seller AND credit buyer** (double entry). This is a correction of the manual's table (V-24).
- Prorations between the parties: double entries. Unpaid taxes: debit seller, credit buyer.
- Deed stamps: seller debit. Note stamps + intangible tax: buyer debit. Commission: seller debit. These reflect custom (manual; not yet verified), and contracts can allocate differently.
- **Buyer's cash = buyer debits − buyer credits. Seller's cash = seller credits − seller debits.**

**Understand**

- V-24 (corrected) verifies the three mortgage lines: assumed = double entry; new = buyer credit only; payoff = seller debit only. The other placements follow the same accounting logic and come from the study manual (manual; not yet verified).
- A double entry moves value from one party to the other, so it changes both nets but cancels out in the money-in = money-out check.
- The deposit is a credit to the buyer and not to the seller, because the seller is already credited with the full price, and the deposit is part of that price.

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"The deposit is credited to the seller."** It is a **buyer credit only**. The seller's credit is the full price.
- **"An assumed mortgage is just a buyer credit, like a new loan."** It is a double entry: debit seller, credit buyer (V-24 corrects the manual).
- **"The new loan is debited to the seller."** The seller's debit is the payoff of the seller's own old loan, not the buyer's new loan.
- **"Unpaid taxes are a buyer debit."** Debit seller, credit buyer: the buyer will pay the whole bill later.
- **"Commission is split between buyer and seller on the statement."** It is customarily a seller debit.

## 10. Memory sheet

- Debit = owes · credit = receives.
- **Deposit: buyer credit only. New loan: buyer credit only. Payoff: seller debit only.**
- **Assumed loan: debit seller + credit buyer** (V-24 corrects the manual).
- Prorations between the parties: double entries (unpaid taxes → debit seller, credit buyer).
- Deed stamps + commission → seller debits; note stamps + intangible → buyer debits (custom: manual; not yet verified).
- Buyer brings debits − credits; seller gets credits − debits; money in = money out.
