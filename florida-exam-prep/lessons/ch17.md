## 2. Why it matters

**In practice.** Investor clients judge a property by its numbers, not by its kitchen. A retiree buying a duplex in Tampa and a family buying a small retail strip in Ocala will ask the same questions: "What's the cap rate?", "Will it still make money after the mortgage?", "How much should I borrow?" You need to speak this language and know which number goes where.

**On the exam.** Chapter 17 (investments and business brokerage) is worth about 2% of the exam (V-02), roughly two questions out of 100 (V-01), shared with business opportunity brokerage (L17-2). From this lesson expect a vocabulary question (leverage, liquidity, types of risk) or a short calculation: cash flow = NOI − debt service, or cap rate = NOI ÷ price. This course's practice questions may ask you to type the number, so the arithmetic must be exact.

## 3. Simple explanation

### What an investor actually buys

An investor buys an **income stream** (rent, year after year) and a **future value** (the eventual sale price). All figures are in US dollars and **per year** unless a question says otherwise.

### Advantages and disadvantages

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| **Income**: rent paid by tenants | **Illiquidity**: slow and costly to sell |
| **Appreciation**: the value rises over time | **Management burden**: tenants, repairs, vacancies, paperwork |
| **Leverage**: borrowed money magnifies the return on the owner's own money | **Risk**: income and value can fall (see the risk table below) |
| **Tax shelter**: deductions such as depreciation reduce taxable income | |

### Leverage: borrowed money working for you, or against you

**Equity** is the owner's own stake: the property's value minus the debt on it. **Leverage** is using borrowed money to increase the return on that equity.

Here is how it works. Buy a $400,000 property with $100,000 of your own money and a $300,000 loan.

- If the value rises 10% to $440,000, your equity grows from $100,000 to $140,000. That is **+40%** on your money.
- If the value falls 10% to $360,000, your equity shrinks to $60,000. That is **−40%** on your money.

(Loan repayment and costs are ignored to keep it simple.)

Leverage also works on income. Compare two **percentages**: what the property earns (NOI ÷ price) and what the loan costs each year (annual debt service ÷ loan amount). If the property's percentage is higher, borrowing raises your return on equity. This is **positive leverage**. If the loan's percentage is higher, borrowing lowers your return. This is **negative leverage**. Compare percentages, not dollars: a property can still cover its mortgage payments in dollars and yet earn a lower percentage than the loan costs (see Step 5 of the practical example). **Leverage cuts both ways**, and more debt also means more **financial risk**.

### Liquidity

**Liquidity** is how quickly and cheaply an asset can be turned into cash without losing value. Cash is perfectly liquid. Real estate is **illiquid**: selling takes marketing time, negotiation, inspections, the buyer's financing and a closing, and commissions and closing costs reduce what the seller keeps.

### The income ladder: from rent to cash flow

Each line below is defined before it is used, and each one builds on the line above it.

1. **Potential gross income (PGI):** all the rent you would collect if every unit were rented all year.
2. **Vacancy and collection loss:** rent lost to empty units and to tenants who do not pay.
3. **Effective gross income (EGI)** = PGI − vacancy and collection loss.
4. **Operating expenses:** the costs of running the property. They include fixed costs (property taxes, insurance), variable costs (repairs, utilities, management) and **reserves for replacement** (money set aside for items such as roofs and air-conditioning units). **Debt service and depreciation are NOT operating expenses** (manual; not yet verified).
5. **Net operating income (NOI)** = EGI − operating expenses.
6. **Debt service:** the total mortgage payments for the year (principal + interest). Monthly payment × 12.
7. **Cash flow** (before income taxes) = **NOI − debt service**.

### Cap rate

The **capitalization rate (cap rate)** = **NOI ÷ price** (or value), written as a percentage. It is the first-year return the property would give a buyer who paid all cash.

Debt service is left out on purpose. The cap rate measures the **property**, not the buyer's loan, so two buyers with different mortgages buy the same building at the same cap rate.

The same three numbers can be rearranged. Appraisers call this **IRV**: Income = Rate × Value.

- Rate = NOI ÷ Value
- Value = NOI ÷ Rate
- NOI = Value × Rate

For the same NOI, a **higher cap rate means a lower price**, and a lower cap rate means a higher price.

### Two more ways an investment grows

- **Equity buildup:** each mortgage payment includes some principal, so the loan shrinks and the owner's equity grows.
- **Appreciation:** the property's value rises over time, which also increases equity.

### Types of risk

| Risk | What it means | Florida-style example |
|---|---|---|
| **Business risk** | The property's own operations earn less than expected | A major tenant of a strip center closes |
| **Financial risk** | Risk created by debt: the more you borrow, the larger the fixed payment the income must cover, and the greater the risk of default and foreclosure | A highly leveraged duplex loses one tenant and the rent no longer covers the mortgage |
| **Market risk** | Changes in supply and demand reduce rents or values | New apartment buildings open nearby and push rents down |
| **Interest-rate risk** | Rising rates raise borrowing costs and can push values down | An adjustable-rate loan's payment increases |
| **Liquidity risk** | You cannot sell quickly without cutting the price | The owner needs cash fast in a slow market |

### Tax shelter, in one paragraph

The main tax shelter is **depreciation** (cost recovery). It is a tax deduction for the building's wear, taken **straight-line** over **27.5 years for residential rental** property or **39 years for nonresidential** property. **Land is never depreciated** (V-77). Depreciation costs no cash in the year it is deducted, so it can shelter part of the cash flow from income tax. It is **not** an operating expense and does **not** reduce NOI. Taxation is covered in more detail elsewhere in the course.

## 4. Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Equity | The owner's stake: value minus debt |
| Leverage | Using borrowed money to increase return on equity; it magnifies gains and losses |
| Positive / negative leverage | The property's percentage return is higher / lower than the loan's percentage cost |
| Liquidity | How quickly and cheaply an asset converts to cash without losing value |
| Operating expenses | Costs of running the property (taxes, insurance, repairs, management, reserves); not debt service, not depreciation |
| NOI | Net operating income = EGI − operating expenses |
| Debt service | Total annual mortgage payments (principal + interest) |
| Cash flow | NOI minus debt service (before income taxes) |
| Cap rate | NOI ÷ price (or value), as a percentage |
| Equity buildup | Growth in equity as the loan principal is repaid |
| Appreciation | Increase in property value over time |
| Depreciation (cost recovery) | Tax deduction for the building over 27.5 or 39 years; never land (V-77) |

## 5. Practical example

**Ana** is a sales associate in Lakeland. Her investor client, **Mr. Duarte**, is looking at a fourplex listed at **$720,000**. All figures are hypothetical and annual.

**Step 1: Build the NOI.**

| Line | Amount |
|---|---|
| Potential gross income: 4 units × $1,850/month × 12 | $88,800 |
| − Vacancy and collection loss (5% of $88,800) | − $4,440 |
| = Effective gross income | $84,360 |
| − Property taxes | − $9,600 |
| − Insurance | − $8,200 |
| − Repairs and maintenance | − $4,800 |
| − Management | − $5,060 |
| − Reserves for replacement | − $1,500 |
| − Utilities and lawn care | − $1,200 |
| = Total operating expenses | $30,360 |
| **NOI** ($84,360 − $30,360) | **$54,000** |

The mortgage payment is not on this list, and neither is depreciation.

**Step 2: Cap rate at the asking price.** $54,000 ÷ $720,000 = 0.075 = **7.5%**.

**Step 3: Check the price against the market.** Ana finds that similar fourplexes in the area sell at about an 8% cap rate. Value = $54,000 ÷ 0.08 = **$675,000**. The asking price is above what the local cap rate supports, and that gives Mr. Duarte a negotiating point.

**Step 4: Add the loan.** He would put 25% down ($180,000) and borrow $540,000. The payment is $3,300 a month, so debt service = $3,300 × 12 = **$39,600** a year.

- Cash flow = $54,000 − $39,600 = **$14,400**.
- Return on his cash: $14,400 ÷ $180,000 = **8%**, compared with 7.5% if he paid all cash. The loan costs $39,600 ÷ $540,000 ≈ 7.33% a year (annual debt service as a percentage of the loan, principal included). That is less than the property earns (7.5%), so this is **positive leverage**.

**Step 5: The bad year.** Suppose new apartments open nearby (market risk), vacancy rises, and NOI falls to $42,000. Cash flow = $42,000 − $39,600 = **$2,400**, about 1.3% on his $180,000. An all-cash buyer would still earn $42,000 ÷ $720,000 ≈ 5.8%. In dollars the NOI still covers the payments, but as a percentage the loan now costs more (≈ 7.33%) than the property earns (≈ 5.8%). That is **negative leverage**: leverage cuts both ways.

## 6. Portugal comparison

**The familiar principle.** Gross rent, vacancy, operating costs, net income, borrowing and the trade-off between return and risk are financial arithmetic. They work the same way in euros as in dollars.

**The Florida rule.** For the exam: cash flow = NOI − debt service; cap rate = NOI ÷ price; debt service and depreciation are not operating expenses; depreciation runs 27.5 years (residential rental) or 39 years (nonresidential), never on land (V-77).

**The precise difference.** For the formulas there is **no meaningful difference**. What differs is the surrounding law. The depreciation periods are US federal tax rules (V-77). How Portugal taxes rental income or allows deductions is not covered by this course's comparison entries and **requires verification**.

**Exam implication.** Work in US dollars with annual figures, using the US definitions above. Never use a Portuguese tax rule to answer a Florida question.

## 7. Legal and numerical requirements

**Memorize exactly**

- Advantages: income, appreciation, **leverage**, tax shelter. Disadvantages: **illiquidity**, management burden, risk.
- **Cash flow = NOI − debt service.**
- **Cap rate = NOI ÷ price.** Value = NOI ÷ cap rate.
- NOI = (PGI − vacancy and collection loss) − operating expenses. Debt service and depreciation are not operating expenses (manual; not yet verified).
- Other measures: equity buildup and appreciation.
- Risks: business, financial (debt), market, interest-rate, liquidity.
- Depreciation: straight-line, 27.5 years residential rental, 39 years nonresidential, land never (V-77).

**Understand**

- Leverage helps only when the property's percentage return (NOI ÷ price) is higher than the loan's percentage cost (annual debt service ÷ loan). It magnifies losses as well as gains.
- The cap rate ignores financing because it measures the property, not the buyer.
- Annual debt service = monthly payment × 12. Mixing monthly and annual figures is the most common calculation error.
- For the same NOI, a higher cap rate means a lower value.
- Source status: the formulas and risk vocabulary are standard course definitions from the study manual, and no verification entry covers them (manual; not yet verified). They are definitions, not statutes. The depreciation periods are verified (V-77), and the chapter weight comes from V-02.

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"Mortgage payments are an operating expense."** No. NOI is calculated before debt service. Subtract debt service only to get cash flow.
- **"Depreciation reduces NOI."** No. It is a tax deduction, not an operating expense.
- **"Cap rate = cash flow ÷ price."** No. Cap rate uses **NOI**, before any loan.
- **"A higher cap rate means a more expensive property."** The opposite: for the same NOI, a higher cap rate means a lower price.
- **"Leverage always increases returns."** Only when the property's percentage return is higher than the loan's percentage cost. Otherwise it lowers returns, and it always magnifies losses.
- **"If NOI covers the mortgage payments, leverage is positive."** No. Positive cash flow is not the test. Compare percentages: in the bad year above, NOI covers the payments but the property earns about 5.8% while the loan costs about 7.33%.
- **"Liquidity means profitability."** No. Liquidity is how quickly and cheaply an asset converts to cash. A very profitable building can still be illiquid.
- **"The land under a rental building is depreciated too."** No. Land is never depreciated (V-77).
- **"Debt service is the monthly payment."** For annual analysis it is the monthly payment × 12.

- **Cap rate = NOI ÷ price** · Value = NOI ÷ rate · higher rate = lower value.
- No mortgage and no depreciation in operating expenses.
- Leverage cuts both ways: positive only if the property's % return (NOI ÷ price) beats the loan's % cost (debt service ÷ loan).
- Risks: business · financial (debt) · market · interest-rate · liquidity.

## 2. Why it matters

**In practice.** Florida towns have plenty of small businesses: cafés, salons, dry cleaners, small motels. When the owners sell, they often call the agent who sold their house. Selling a business is not like selling a house. You are selling a working enterprise, and part of the price pays for things nobody can touch. You need to know what is in the package, when a license is needed and which disclosure rules apply.

**On the exam.** This is a low-priority topic. It shares chapter 17's roughly 2% of the exam (V-02) with investment analysis. Expect probably no more than one question, usually "what is goodwill?" or "does this person need a real estate license?".

## 3. Simple explanation

### What is being sold: a going concern

**Business opportunity brokerage** means helping someone sell (or buy) a business for compensation. What changes hands is a **going concern**: a business that is operating today and is expected to keep operating, with customers, staff, suppliers and a name people recognize. The buyer pays for the whole working unit because it already produces income. A café with loyal customers is usually worth more than its tables, machines and coffee beans sold one by one.

### The package

| Component | What kind of property | Example: a café | How it usually transfers |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Real estate** (if any) | Real property | The land and building, if the business owner owns them | Deed |
| **Lease of the premises** (if the business rents) | A leasehold: the business's right, as tenant, to occupy the premises under the lease | The café's lease of a shop unit | Assignment of the lease, subject to the lease's terms |
| **Goodwill** | Intangible | Reputation, regular customers, the café's name | Included in the sale contract |
| **Fixtures** | Items attached to the premises | Walk-in cooler, built-in counters | Listed one by one in the contract |
| **Equipment** | Personal property | Espresso machines, ovens, point-of-sale terminals | Bill of sale |
| **Inventory** | Personal property | Coffee beans, pastries, packaged goods | Bill of sale, usually counted near closing |

**Fixtures need special care.** Whether a counter or cooler goes with the business or stays with the building depends on who owns the building and on what the contract says, so list each item in writing. If the business rents its space, items it installed for its trade (**trade fixtures**) raise the same question: the lease and the sale contract should say who owns them and whether they pass to the buyer.

### Goodwill

**Goodwill** is the intangible value of a business's reputation and customer base: the reason customers keep coming back. A practical way to picture it is as the part of the price that the identifiable assets do not explain. Goodwill depends on the business continuing to operate, which is why buyers want a going concern and not a closed shop.

Buyers usually pay for the **profit** a business produces, so the income thinking from investment analysis (L17-1) applies here too: a steady, well-documented income stream supports a higher price.

### When a real estate license is needed

Under Chapter 475, **brokering business opportunities for compensation requires a real estate license, particularly when real property is included** (manual; not yet verified).

Read this carefully. The manual's own sentence says a license is required "when real property is included". That describes the clearest case. It does **not** say that a business sale without real estate is free of licensing. The course position, and the exam answer, is that a person who sells a business for another person for compensation (such as a commission) needs a real estate license. The exact scope of the statute has not been verified yet.

### Brokerage-relationship disclosures

Earlier lessons covered the brokerage-relationship disclosure rules of s. 475.278: for example, the single agent notice, the no brokerage relationship notice, and the principal's written consent before a single agent becomes a transaction broker (V-48). **Business sales are outside these disclosure rules, except for property with four or fewer residential units** (V-20, verified). So a café in a commercial building is outside them. If the deal also involves property with four or fewer residential units, the business-sale exception does not reach that property, and the rules apply to it.

### Practical cautions (good practice, not law)

- Ask for the financial records (profit and loss statements, tax returns), because the price rests on income.
- Keep the sale confidential. Staff, suppliers and customers may react badly to rumors.
- List every component and its price in writing: real estate or lease, goodwill, fixtures, equipment, inventory.

## 4. Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Business opportunity brokerage | Selling a business for another for compensation (goodwill, inventory, fixtures, possibly real estate) |
| Going concern | A business that is operating and expected to continue, sold as a working whole |
| Goodwill | Intangible value of a business's reputation and customer base |
| Fixtures | Items attached to the premises (counters, coolers); list them in the contract |
| Trade fixtures | Items a business tenant installs for its trade; the lease and the sale contract should say who owns them |
| Equipment | Movable business assets (machines, terminals); personal property |
| Inventory | Goods held for sale or use (stock); personal property |
| Leasehold | A tenant's right to occupy and use property under a lease |
| Bill of sale | The document that transfers personal property |
| Deed | The document that transfers real property |

## 5. Practical example

**Rui** is a sales associate in Tampa. **Helena** owns *Café Azulejo* (a fictional business). It is a busy café in a building she also owns. She asks Rui's brokerage to sell the business for a commission, and Rui handles the listing. All figures are hypothetical.

| Component | Agreed value |
|---|---|
| Land and building (commercial) | $780,000 |
| Equipment and fixtures (espresso machines, ovens, walk-in cooler, counters) | $95,000 |
| Inventory (counted near closing) | $25,000 |
| Goodwill (name, reputation, regular customers) | $250,000 |
| **Total price** | **$1,150,000** |

Goodwill here is the part of the price that the other assets do not explain: $1,150,000 − $780,000 − $95,000 − $25,000 = **$250,000**.

**Does Rui need a license?** Yes. He is brokering a business opportunity for compensation, and real property is included (manual; not yet verified).

**Do the brokerage-relationship disclosures apply?** This is a business sale and the building is commercial, so it is outside the s. 475.278 disclosure rules (V-20).

**Variation 1: the duplex.** Suppose Helena's deal also includes a duplex (two residential units) behind the café. That is property with four or fewer residential units, so the business-sale exception does not reach it and Rui follows the disclosure rules for it (V-20).

**Variation 2: no building.** Suppose instead that Helena rents her shop space, so the deal is the lease, goodwill, equipment and inventory. Rui should not conclude that he can work without a license. For exam purposes, brokering a business opportunity for compensation requires a real estate license. The manual's emphasis on real property is not a safe harbor (manual; not yet verified).

## 7. Legal and numerical requirements

- **Goodwill** = the intangible value of reputation and customer base.

- The manual's "when real property is included" does not mean "only when real property is included". Do not treat a sale with no real estate as license-free.

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"A business is personal property, so no license is needed."** Wrong. Brokering a business opportunity for compensation requires a real estate license, particularly when real property is included (manual; not yet verified).
- **"Only a securities license is needed to sell a business."** The course answer is a real estate license.
- **"Only an attorney may sell a business for someone else."** No. This is business opportunity brokerage, which is done under a real estate license.
- **"Goodwill is the value of the building."** No. Goodwill is intangible: reputation and customer base. The building is real estate.
- **"Goodwill, plottage and equity are the same thing."** No. Plottage is the value gain when parcels are combined. Equity is value minus debt. Goodwill is the business's intangible value.
- **"The brokerage-relationship disclosures apply to every sale."** Business sales are outside them, except property with four or fewer residential units (V-20).
- **"No building in the deal means no license."** Do not assume it. The manual's mention of real property describes the clearest case; it is not an exemption.
