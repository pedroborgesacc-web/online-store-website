## 2. Why it matters

**In practice.** Every pricing conversation with a Florida seller runs on these ideas. An owner says "we spent $85,000 on the pool, so add $85,000", or "the county says it's worth $360,000". You need precise words to explain why neither number is the market value. The same ideas explain why the biggest house on a modest street is hard to sell, and why a developer will pay extra for the lot next door.

**On the exam.** Appraisal is one of the largest sections of the Florida exam: **8%** of the content (V-02, confirmed), so about 8 of the 100 questions (V-01). Questions on principles test vocabulary: you get a one-line scenario and four principle names. This lesson is also the base for L16-2 to L16-4. Substitution sits under all three appraisal approaches, contribution sets the adjustment amounts, and anticipation drives the income approach.

## 3. Simple explanation

*Source status: the definitions and principles in this lesson come from the study manual. No official-source verification entry covers appraisal theory, so they are taught as (manual; not yet verified). The Florida tax facts marked with a V-number are verified.*

### Value needs four things: DUST

A property has value only when all four **elements of value** are present at the same time:

| Element | Meaning | If it is missing… |
|---|---|---|
| **D**emand | People want it **and** can pay for it | Nobody bids, however nice the property |
| **U**tility | It can be used; it satisfies a need | A parcel with no possible use attracts no buyer |
| **S**carcity | Supply is limited | Something unlimited (like air) has no market price |
| **T**ransferability | Ownership can legally pass to a buyer | If title cannot be conveyed, nobody can buy it |

If you remove any one element, value disappears. That is why the exam asks for all four together. Learn the exact words: the S is **Scarcity** (not "supply") and the T is **Transferability** (the legal ability to pass ownership to a buyer). Answer options often swap in similar-sounding words.

### Value, price and cost are three different numbers

First, one term: an **arm's-length sale** is a sale between parties who are independent of each other and each act in their own interest (not relatives, not related companies).

- **Market value** is the **most probable price** in an **arm's-length** sale between **informed parties** under **no unusual pressure**. It is an estimate of what *should* happen in a normal sale.
- **Price** is what was **actually paid** in one particular sale. It is a historical fact. A rushed sale, a sale between relatives or a poorly informed buyer can produce a price far from market value.
- **Cost** is what it took to **build** the property. Building something does not mean anyone will pay that much for it.
- **Assessed value** is the value set for **property taxes**. In Florida, property is assessed as of **January 1** (V-76, confirmed). A homestead's assessed value may rise each year by no more than **3% or the CPI change, whichever is lower** (Save Our Homes, V-67, confirmed). So a home owned for many years can carry an assessed value far below its market value. (A proposed 2026 amendment, V-23, is pending. It is **not law** and not exam content.)
- **Insurable value** is the value used for insurance coverage. It concerns what can be damaged or destroyed, mainly the building, so it can differ from market value.

### The principles of value, and why they work

**Substitution** is *the foundation of appraisal*. A buyer will not pay more than the cost of an equally desirable substitute. It works because informed buyers have alternatives. If a similar house two streets away is available for $410,000, few buyers will pay $450,000 for yours. Each approach uses a different substitute: a similar sold property (sales comparison), building a similar property (cost), or buying a similar income stream (income).

**Highest and best use (HBU)** is the use that is **legally permissible, physically possible, financially feasible and maximally productive**. Learn the tests in that order. First remove the uses the law does not allow (zoning, for example). Then remove the uses the site cannot physically support, and then the uses that would not pay for themselves. Of the uses left, the one producing the highest value wins. HBU matters because land is valued for the best use the market can make of it, which may not be its current use.

**Anticipation** says value reflects **expected future benefits**. Buyers pay today for what they expect to receive later: rent, enjoyment, resale. It is the **basis of the income approach** (L16-4).

**Contribution** says a feature is worth **what it adds to value, not what it cost**. A pool that cost $85,000 but adds $35,000 to market value contributes $35,000.

**Increasing and decreasing returns** apply contribution to the question "how much should we improve?" At first, improvements may add more value than they cost (increasing returns). Past a certain point, each extra dollar spent adds less than a dollar of value (decreasing returns).

**Conformity** says value is best protected when a property is reasonably similar to its surroundings in size, style, quality and use. It has two corollaries:

- **Progression**: a **modest** property **gains** value from **better** neighbours.
- **Regression**: a **superior** property **loses** value from **lesser** neighbours.

**Plottage and assemblage.** Combining adjacent parcels can create a site worth more than the separate parcels. The **act** of combining is **assemblage**. The **extra value** created is **plottage**. For example, two lots worth $100,000 each might be worth $260,000 together. The plottage value is then $60,000.

## 5. Practical example

**Setting.** You are a sales associate preparing a CMA for Linda. A CMA is a licensee's pricing tool and is never called an appraisal (manual; not yet verified; see L15-1). Linda owns a 4-bedroom pool home in a Sarasota subdivision of mostly smaller 3-bedroom homes. It has been her homestead since 2012.

| What Linda says | Value type or principle | What you explain |
|---|---|---|
| "I paid $280,000 in 2012." | Price | A historical fact about one sale, not today's market value |
| "The county's assessed value is $360,000." | Assessed value | A tax value. As her homestead, its growth has been capped by Save Our Homes (V-67), so it can sit well below market value |
| "The pool and summer kitchen cost us $85,000." | Contribution | Recent sales show that similar homes with pools sell for about $35,000 more. The features contribute about $35,000, not $85,000 |
| "Mine is the biggest house on the street." | Conformity → regression | Smaller neighbours pull a superior house's value down. Her small neighbour benefits (progression) |
| "Buyers will compare it with the new 4-bedroom homes nearby." | Substitution | If an equally desirable new home is available at $545,000, informed buyers will not pay much more for hers |
| "A new interchange is planned near the subdivision." | Anticipation | Buyers pay today for expected future benefits, such as better access |
| "A developer bought the two corner lots on the main road to combine them." | Assemblage / plottage | Assemblage is the act. Any extra value of the combined site is plottage. Whether it can become a shop depends first on what zoning permits (HBU test 1) |

Your CMA will show a price range based on comparable sales, not on Linda's purchase price, her costs or the county's figure.

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"The price paid is the market value."** No. Price is one actual sale. Market value is the most probable price in an arm's-length sale between informed parties under no unusual pressure.
- **"It cost $85,000, so it adds $85,000."** No. Under contribution, a feature is worth what it adds to value.
- **"Assessed value is market value."** No. Assessed value is for taxes and, for a Florida homestead, its growth is capped (V-67).
- **"Progression means prices rising over time."** No. It is about neighbours: a modest home lifted by better ones.
- **"Plottage and assemblage mean the same thing."** No. Assemblage is the act of combining; plottage is the extra value.
- **"Highest and best use is the most profitable use imaginable."** No. It must first be legally permissible, then physically possible, then financially feasible.
- **"Anticipation is about past sales."** No. It is about expected future benefits. Past sales are the evidence for substitution.

## 2. Why it matters

**In practice.** Every time you price a Florida home, you do a rough sales comparison. You look at what similar homes sold for and ask how this one is different. An appraiser does the same work formally, on an **adjustment grid**: a table that lists each comparable sale and the dollar corrections made to it. When a buyer's lender orders an appraisal, or a seller disputes your CMA (comparative market analysis: a licensee's pricing tool, never called an appraisal; manual; not yet verified), you must be able to read that grid and explain every plus and minus.

**On the exam.** Appraisal is **8%** of the exam (V-02, confirmed), so about 8 of the 100 questions (V-01). Sales comparison questions are short and predictable. They ask which way to adjust, what one comparable's adjusted price is, and which comparable deserves the most weight. The wrong answers are built on two mistakes: adjusting the subject, and adjusting in the wrong direction.

## 3. Simple explanation

> **Source status.** The appraisal method in this lesson comes from the study manual. No official-source verification entry covers appraisal methods, so the rules are taught as **(manual; not yet verified)**. The arithmetic itself needs no verification.

### Step 1: What it represents

The **sales comparison approach** estimates a property's value from the prices of **recent sales of similar properties**. It rests on **substitution** (L16-1): an informed buyer will not pay more than the cost of an equally desirable substitute. So what buyers just paid for similar homes shows what this home is worth.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Subject (property)** | The property being valued |
| **Comparable ("comp")** | A recent sale of a similar property, ideally nearby and an arm's-length sale (independent parties, L16-1) |
| **Adjustment** | A dollar amount added to or subtracted from a comp's sale price for one difference from the subject |
| **Adjusted sale price** | The comp's price after all adjustments: what the comp would have sold for if it were just like the subject |
| **Adjustment grid** | The table showing the comps, their differences and the adjustments |

The approach is **best for homes and vacant land** (manual), because these sell often and buyers really do shop for them by comparison.

### Step 2: Units

- Sale prices and adjustments are in **US dollars**.
- A feature adjustment is a **lump sum** (for example, a pool: $25,000).
- A size adjustment is **dollars per square foot × the difference in square feet**. US living area is measured in square feet. *Metric aside only: 1 m² ≈ 10.76 sq ft.*
- The amount of each adjustment is the feature's **contribution** (L16-1): what it adds to market value, **not what it cost** to build.

### Step 3: Formula

**Adjusted sale price = comp's sale price − value of what the comp has that the subject lacks + value of what the subject has that the comp lacks**

You adjust the **comparable, never the subject**. The question to ask is: "If this comp were exactly like the subject, what would it have sold for?"

| Situation | Adjustment to the comp's price | Mnemonic |
|---|---|---|
| **C**omp **B**etter (it has a feature the subject lacks, or more of it) | **S**ubtract | **CBS** |
| **C**omp **I**nferior (it lacks a feature the subject has, or has less) | **A**dd | **CIA** |
| Both have the same feature | No adjustment | — |

**Why subtract when the comp is better?** The comp's price already includes the value of its extra feature. The subject does not have that feature, so it would not earn that money. You take it out.

### Step 4: When it applies

- **Property types:** best for homes and vacant land (manual). Brand-new or special-purpose buildings with few sales point to the cost approach (L16-3); income property points to the income approach (L16-4).
- **Choosing comps:** use **recent**, similar, nearby, arm's-length **sales**. A licensee's CMA also shows currently listed and expired listings to shape a price range (manual), but only sold properties show what buyers actually paid.
- **Bracketing:** the adjusted prices of good comps **bracket** the subject's value, meaning some fall above it and some below (manual).
- **Weighting:** the comp with the **fewest and smallest adjustments** is the most similar to the subject. It is the most reliable, so it carries **the most weight** (manual). Judge "smallest" by the size of each adjustment, ignoring the plus or minus sign: +$15,000 and −$15,000 net to zero, but they are still two large adjustments.
- **Final figure:** a weighted judgement, never a simple average (reconciliation, L16-4).

### Step 5: Worked example

**A. The manual's grid example.** A comp sold for **$410,000**. It has a pool worth $25,000 that the subject lacks. The subject has a renovated kitchen worth $15,000 that the comp lacks.

| Item | Who is better? | Amount |
|---|---|---|
| Comp's sale price | | $410,000 |
| Pool (only the comp has it) | Comp better → subtract | −$25,000 |
| Renovated kitchen (only the subject has it) | Comp inferior → add | +$15,000 |
| **Indicated value from this comp** | | **$400,000** |

**B. A three-comp grid in Lakeland.** Subject: 1,800 sq ft, 3 bedrooms, 2 baths, 2-car garage, no pool. The appraiser's market data show these contributions: pool $25,000; living area $100 per sq ft; 2-car garage instead of 1-car $8,000.

| | Comp A | Comp B | Comp C |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sale price | $392,000 | $441,000 | $388,000 |
| Living area | 1,800 (same): 0 | 1,900, better: −$10,000 | 1,650, inferior: +$15,000 |
| Garage | 1-car, inferior: +$8,000 | 2-car (same): 0 | 1-car, inferior: +$8,000 |
| Pool | None (same): 0 | Pool, better: −$25,000 | Pool, better: −$25,000 |
| **Adjusted price** | **$400,000** | **$406,000** | **$386,000** |
| Number of adjustments | 1 | 2 | 3 |
| Total size of adjustments | $8,000 | $35,000 | $48,000 |

Comp A needs the fewest and smallest adjustments, so it carries the most weight, and the indicated value is close to **$400,000**. The adjusted prices ($386,000 to $406,000) bracket that figure. The simple average of the three ($397,333) is **not** the method.

### Step 6: Reasonableness check

- **Direction test.** Comp better → its adjusted price goes **down**. Comp inferior → it goes **up**. Ask: "Would the subject sell for more or less than this comp?"
- **Clustering.** Adjusted prices should sit closer together than raw prices. In example B the raw prices span $53,000 ($388,000 to $441,000); the adjusted prices span only $20,000.
- **Size.** Each adjustment is a market contribution, often much less than what the feature cost.
- **Work backwards.** $400,000 − $15,000 + $25,000 = $410,000 ✓.

### Step 7: Common errors

| Error | Correction |
|---|---|
| Adjusting the subject's value | Adjust only the comparables |
| Adding the value of a feature only the comp has | Comp better → subtract (CBS) |
| Using what the feature cost | Use what it adds to market value (contribution) |
| Adjusting for a feature both properties share | No difference, no adjustment |
| Giving most weight to the highest price or the most recent sale | Most weight = fewest and smallest adjustments |
| Treating a small net adjustment as "few adjustments" | Count each adjustment and its size, ignoring signs |
| Averaging the adjusted prices | Weigh them; never a simple average |
| Using a sale between relatives as a comp | Comps should be arm's-length sales (L16-1) |

## 5. Practical example

**Setting.** You are preparing a CMA for Mr. and Mrs. Alvarez, who own a 3-bedroom, 2-bath home without a pool in Port St. Lucie. Last year they renovated the kitchen for $55,000. You review possible comps:

| Candidate | Your decision | Rule applied |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Same subdivision, sold 2 months ago for $398,000, same size, has a pool, older kitchen | **Use** | Recent, similar, nearby sale |
| 2. Same model, sold to the seller's daughter at a discount | **Reject** | Not an arm's-length sale (L16-1) |
| 3. Same model, but in a different market area across the county | **Weak**; prefer nearby sales | Comps should be similar and nearby |
| 4. Active listing at $455,000 | **Show as competition**, not as proof of value | A listing is not a sale |
| 5. Expired listing at $470,000 | **Show**: buyers did not pay that price | CMAs include expired listings (manual) |

Market data show a pool adds about $24,000 and a renovated kitchen about $15,000. For comp 1: comp better (pool) → −$24,000; comp inferior (older kitchen) → +$15,000. $398,000 − $24,000 + $15,000 = **$389,000**.

Mrs. Alvarez objects: "The kitchen cost us $55,000, so add $55,000." You explain contribution: buyers pay about $15,000 more for a renovated kitchen, so that is the adjustment. Mr. Alvarez asks you to "raise our value because comp 1 has a pool". You explain that the comp is adjusted, never the subject: the pool comes **off** the comp's price. Finally, you label your work as a CMA. It is not an appraisal, and you may never call it one (manual; not yet verified; see L16-4).

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"The comp has a pool, so add the pool to the subject."** No. The subject is never adjusted. Subtract the pool's value from the comp.
- **"A better comp means a higher value for the subject."** No. A better comp is adjusted **down** to look like the subject.
- **"The adjustment equals what the feature cost."** No. It equals what the feature adds to market value.
- **"The highest adjusted price gets the most weight."** No. The comp with the fewest and smallest adjustments does.
- **"Average the adjusted prices to get the value."** No. Reconciliation weighs; it never simply averages.
- **"Active listings prove value."** No. They show competition. Sales show what buyers actually paid.

## 10. Memory sheet

- Best for **homes and vacant land**; based on **substitution**.
- **Adjust the comp, never the subject.**
- **CBS**: Comp Better → Subtract. **CIA**: Comp Inferior → Add.
- Adjustment = **contribution**, not cost.
- Adjusted comps **bracket** the subject.
- Most weight = **fewest and smallest** adjustments.
- $410,000 − $25,000 + $15,000 = **$400,000**.
- Weigh, **don't average**.

## 2. Why it matters

**In practice.** Some Florida properties almost never sell: a church, a school, a fire station. Others are so new that the clearest way to value them is to ask what they would cost to build. The cost approach also gives you the words to explain why a 1970s house in Ocala is worth less than a new house of the same size next door: the old house has suffered **depreciation**. In appraisal, depreciation means any loss in value, from any cause.

**On the exam.** Appraisal is **8%** of the exam (V-02, confirmed), so about 8 of the 100 questions (V-01). Typical questions are a calculation (land + cost new − depreciation), a classification of depreciation ("an airport is built nearby…") and a definition (reproduction or replacement cost). The traps: depreciating the land, calling external obsolescence curable, and borrowing the tax depreciation periods (V-77) for an appraisal question.

## 3. Simple explanation

> **Source status.** The cost approach and the three types of depreciation come from the study manual. No verification entry covers appraisal methods, so they are taught as **(manual; not yet verified)**. The tax depreciation facts used for contrast are verified (V-77). The arithmetic itself needs no verification.

### Step 1: What it represents

The cost approach asks: **what would it cost to create a substitute today?** A buyer will not pay more than the cost of buying a similar lot and building an equally useful building (substitution, L16-1). So the value is the land, plus the building as if new, minus the value the existing building has already lost.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Improvements** | The buildings and other structures on the land |
| **Land value** | Estimated separately, by sales comparison of similar lots (L16-2) |
| **Cost new** | What it would cost **today** to build the improvements new |
| **Reproduction cost** | Cost of an **exact replica**: same design, same materials |
| **Replacement cost** | Cost of a building with **equal utility** (usefulness), built with **current materials and standards** |
| **Accrued depreciation** | All the value the existing improvements have lost compared with new ones, from any cause |
| **Curable** | Worth fixing: the cure costs no more than the value it adds |
| **Incurable** | Not worth fixing (the cure costs more than it adds), or impossible for the owner to fix |

**The three types of depreciation:**

| Type | Cause | Where it comes from | Examples | Curable? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Physical deterioration** | Wear and tear | The property itself | Worn roof, peeling paint, cracked foundation | Curable **or** incurable |
| **Functional obsolescence** | Outdated design or features | The property itself | Four bedrooms but **one bathroom**; **no closets** | Curable **or** incurable |
| **External (economic) obsolescence** | Forces **outside the property line** | The neighbourhood or the market | A new **airport** nearby; a weak **economy** | **Always incurable** |

**Why is external obsolescence always incurable?** Its cause is outside the property. No amount of money spent on the house moves the airport or fixes the local economy.

**Land never depreciates** (manual). Land does not wear out and cannot go out of style; any change in its value shows up in the separate land estimate. Tax law also never depreciates land (V-77, verified).

### Step 2: Units

- **Building size:** square feet. Cost new is usually given **per square foot**: cost new = sq ft × cost per sq ft. *Metric aside only: a cost per m² ÷ 10.76 ≈ cost per sq ft.*
- **Depreciation:** given in dollars, as a **percentage of cost new**, or through years of **economic life** (the total number of years the building is expected to add value).
- **Land:** dollars, taken from sales of comparable lots.

### Step 3: Formula

**Value = land value + cost new of the improvements − accrued depreciation**

| The question gives… | Accrued depreciation = |
|---|---|
| A percentage | cost new × the percentage |
| Separate amounts | physical + functional + external |
| Age and total economic life | (cost new ÷ economic life) × age, the same amount each year (straight-line) |

Work in this order: cost new → minus depreciation → **then** add land. The depreciation applies to the **improvements only**.

### Step 4: When it applies

- **Best for:** **new construction** and **special-purpose properties** such as **schools, churches and fire stations** (manual). A new building has little depreciation to estimate. A special-purpose building has few comparable sales (so sales comparison is weak) and no rent (so the income approach does not work).
- **Weaker for old buildings:** the older the building, the more depreciation must be estimated, and the less reliable the result.
- **Reproduction or replacement?** The question says which cost it gives; you must recognise the definition. An exact copy of a historic building's original details is reproduction. A modern building that does the same job is replacement.
- **Not tax depreciation.** Appraisal depreciation is a **loss of market value**. Tax depreciation is an income-tax deduction: residential rental property over **27.5 years**, nonresidential over **39 years**, straight-line, land never (V-77, confirmed). Do not use these periods in a cost approach question unless the question itself gives them.

### Step 5: Worked example

**A. Percentage method: a church in Tallahassee.** Land: $240,000 (from sales of similar sites). Building: 12,000 sq ft; replacement cost $185 per sq ft. Accrued depreciation: 20%.

1. Cost new: 12,000 × $185 = **$2,220,000**.
2. Depreciation: $2,220,000 × 0.20 = **$444,000**.
3. Depreciated building: $2,220,000 − $444,000 = **$1,776,000**.
4. Add land: $1,776,000 + $240,000 = **$2,016,000**.

If you wrongly took 20% off the land as well, you would get $1,968,000: a wrong answer the exam may offer.

**B. Straight-line method: a fire station in Polk County.** Land: $180,000. Replacement cost new: $1,350,000. The building is 9 years old; its total economic life is 45 years.

1. Depreciation per year: $1,350,000 ÷ 45 = **$30,000**.
2. Accrued depreciation: $30,000 × 9 = **$270,000** (9 ÷ 45 = 20%).
3. Depreciated building: $1,350,000 − $270,000 = **$1,080,000**.
4. Add land: $1,080,000 + $180,000 = **$1,260,000**.

### Step 6: Reasonableness check

- The value is **at most** land + cost new. It equals that sum only for a building with no depreciation.
- The **land figure passes through unchanged**. If your answer changed it, you depreciated land.
- The depreciation percentage lies between 0 and 100%, and a new building should show little.
- Shortcut check for A: building value = 80% of cost new: 0.80 × $2,220,000 = $1,776,000 ✓.

### Step 7: Common errors

| Error | Correction |
|---|---|
| Depreciating the land | Depreciate the improvements only; land never depreciates |
| Forgetting to add the land | Value = land **+** depreciated improvements |
| Using the original (historical) construction cost | Cost new = today's cost |
| Calling external obsolescence curable | It is **always incurable** |
| Calling a poor floor plan "external" | Inside the property, outdated design → functional |
| Calling highway noise "functional" | Outside the property line → external |
| Mixing up the two costs | Reproduction = replica; replacement = equal utility, current materials |
| Using 27.5 or 39 years in an appraisal question | Those are tax periods (V-77); use the life the question gives |

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"Apply the depreciation percentage to the whole property."** No. Only the improvements depreciate; land never does.

## 2. Why it matters

**In practice.** Investors buying Florida duplexes, small apartment buildings or shops talk in **NOI** (net operating income) and **cap rates**, and a seller's flyer often makes both look better than they are. You need to rebuild the numbers yourself. You also need to know where your role ends: you may give a price opinion, but some transactions require an appraisal by an appraiser.

**On the exam.** Appraisal is **8%** of the exam (V-02, confirmed), so about 8 of the 100 questions (V-01). Expect an IRV calculation from raw income and expenses, a GRM question, a reconciliation question and a "who may appraise" question. The manual warns of three planted errors that keep coming back: adjusting the subject (L16-2), treating mortgage payments as operating expenses, and averaging the three approaches.

## 3. Simple explanation

> **Source status.** The income approach, the multipliers, reconciliation and the "who may appraise" rules come from the study manual. No verification entry covers them, so they are taught as **(manual; not yet verified)**. The tax depreciation facts used for contrast are verified (V-77). The arithmetic itself needs no verification.

### Step 1: What it represents

The **income capitalization approach** values a property by the income it produces. It rests on **anticipation** (L16-1): an investor pays today for the income expected in the future. It is the approach for **income property**: rentals and commercial buildings.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Potential gross income (PGI)** | All the income the property could earn in a year if fully rented and every tenant paid |
| **Vacancy and collection loss** | Income lost to empty units and unpaid rent |
| **Effective gross income (EGI)** | PGI − vacancy and collection loss |
| **Operating expenses** | Costs of running the property: **fixed** (property taxes, insurance), **variable** (utilities, repairs, maintenance, management) and **reserves for replacement** (money set aside for items such as roofs and appliances) |
| **Net operating income (NOI)** | EGI − operating expenses |
| **Capitalization (cap) rate** | NOI ÷ value: the yearly return investors expect from the property's income |
| **Debt service** | Mortgage payments (principal and interest) |
| **GRM / GIM** | Gross rent multiplier / gross income multiplier: quick shortcuts from gross income to value |

### Step 2: Units

- In the NOI build, every figure is **annual** dollars. Convert monthly rent: × 12.
- A vacancy percentage is applied to **PGI**.
- The cap rate is a yearly percentage. Turn it into a decimal before dividing: 7.2% = 0.072.
- **GRM uses monthly** gross rent. **GIM uses annual** gross income. Both multipliers are plain numbers, without a dollar sign.

### Step 3: Formula

**Build NOI:** PGI − vacancy and collection loss = **EGI**; EGI − operating expenses (fixed + variable + reserves) = **NOI**.

**IRV:** **I**ncome = **R**ate × **V**alue. Cover the letter you want:

| To find | Formula |
|---|---|
| Value | NOI ÷ cap rate |
| Cap rate | NOI ÷ value (or sale price) |
| Income (NOI) | value × cap rate |

**Multipliers:** GRM = price ÷ **monthly** gross rent, so value = monthly rent × GRM. GIM = price ÷ **annual** gross income, so value = annual gross income × GIM.

| Deduct as operating expenses | Never deduct |
|---|---|
| Property taxes and special assessments | **Debt service** (mortgage principal and interest) |
| Hazard and liability insurance | **Depreciation** |
| Utilities, repairs, maintenance, management | |
| Reserves for replacement | |

**Why these two are excluded.** The building produces the same income whether the buyer pays cash or borrows, so financing (debt service) cannot change the property's value. Depreciation is a tax deduction (residential rental 27.5 years, nonresidential 39 years, V-77), not money spent running the building.

### Step 4: When it applies

- **Income approach:** income property (manual).
- **GRM:** a rougher shortcut for **houses and duplexes** (manual). **GIM:** the same idea with **annual** gross income. Neither looks at expenses or vacancy, which is why they are rough.

**Reconciliation.** The appraiser's last step is **reconciliation**: weighing the approaches by their **reliability for this property** to reach one final value. It is **never a simple average**. It is also not "always take the highest" or "always use sales comparison". Homes lean on sales comparison (L16-2), new or special-purpose buildings on cost (L16-3), and income property on income.

**USPAP.** The **Uniform Standards of Professional Appraisal Practice** govern appraisal practice.

**Who may appraise.** The manual uses the term **federally related transaction** without defining it. As a general description only (requires verification), it refers to a real estate financing transaction overseen by federal financial regulators that requires an appraisal, such as a mortgage loan from a federally regulated lender. On the exam, the phrase itself is your cue: a state-licensed or certified appraiser is required.

| Work | Who may do it | May charge? | May be called an "appraisal"? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Appraisal for a **federally related transaction** | A **state-licensed or certified appraiser**, following USPAP | Yes | Yes |
| **CMA** (comparative market analysis): sold, listed and expired comparables shape a price range | A real estate licensee | **Yes** | **Never** |
| **BPO** (broker price opinion): ordered by a lender or investor, e.g. for a short sale (a sale for less than the loan balance) or a portfolio check | A real estate licensee | **Yes** | **Never** |

The manual's trap: "an experienced broker may appraise for a federally related transaction if she follows USPAP." **No.** The licence class is the barrier, not the standards. Per the manual, a licensee who does appraisal work **outside** federally related transactions must still comply with USPAP (manual; not yet verified).

### Step 5: Worked example

**A. NOI and IRV: an 8-unit building in Jacksonville.** Each unit rents for $1,750 per month.

| Line | Working | Amount |
|---|---|---|
| PGI | 8 × $1,750 × 12 | $168,000 |
| − Vacancy and collection loss | 5% × $168,000 | −$8,400 |
| **= EGI** | | **$159,600** |
| − Property taxes (fixed) | | −$18,500 |
| − Insurance (fixed) | | −$14,200 |
| − Utilities, common areas (variable) | | −$6,300 |
| − Repairs and maintenance (variable) | | −$9,800 |
| − Management (variable) | | −$9,600 |
| − Reserves for replacement | | −$4,000 |
| **= NOI** | $159,600 − $62,400 | **$97,200** |

The owner's statement also lists mortgage payments of $58,000 and a tax depreciation deduction of $24,000. **Ignore both.**

Cap rate from a comparable: a similar building sold for $1,250,000 with an NOI of $90,000. R = $90,000 ÷ $1,250,000 = 0.072 = **7.2%**.

Value: $97,200 ÷ 0.072 = **$1,350,000**.

*The trap:* deducting the mortgage gives an NOI of $39,200 and a "value" of about $544,444, which is badly wrong.

**B. GRM: a single-family rental in Orlando.** A comparable rental house sold for $297,000 while renting for $2,200 per month: GRM = $297,000 ÷ $2,200 = **135**. The subject rents for $2,300 per month: $2,300 × 135 = **$310,500**.

**C. GIM: a small retail building.** Annual gross income is $96,000; similar buildings sell at a GIM of 9.5. Value: $96,000 × 9.5 = **$912,000**.

**D. Reconciliation: a Gainesville duplex about 40 years old.**

| Approach | Indication | Reliability for this property |
|---|---|---|
| Sales comparison | $612,000 | High: several recent nearby duplex sales |
| Income | $598,000 | High: duplex buyers buy rent; good rent and expense data |
| Cost | $655,000 | Low: an old building, with much depreciation to estimate |

The appraiser gives most weight to sales comparison and income and reconciles to a figure such as **$605,000**, explaining why. The simple average ($621,667) would be pulled up by the least reliable approach.

### Step 6: Reasonableness check

- **PGI ≥ EGI ≥ NOI**, always. (EGI equals PGI only when the question gives no vacancy or collection loss.)
- **Work backwards:** value × rate = NOI. $1,350,000 × 0.072 = $97,200 ✓.
- **Rate up, value down.** The same $97,200 at 8% gives $1,215,000, less than at 7.2%.
- **Size:** at 7.2%, the value is about 13.9 times NOI (1 ÷ 0.072). If your value is smaller than the NOI, you multiplied instead of dividing. If it is about 12 times too small, you used monthly income.
- **Multipliers:** when rent is the only income, a GRM is 12 times the GIM for the same property.

### Step 7: Common errors

| Error | Correction |
|---|---|
| Deducting mortgage payments (debt service) | Never an operating expense |
| Deducting depreciation | Never an operating expense |
| Skipping vacancy and collection loss | PGI − vacancy = EGI comes first |
| Using monthly rent in the NOI build | Annual figures: × 12 |
| Dividing by 7.2 instead of 0.072 | Convert the percentage to a decimal |
| NOI × rate to find value | Value = NOI **÷** rate |
| Applying a GRM to annual rent | GRM = monthly; GIM = annual |
| Averaging the three approaches | Reconcile: weigh by reliability |
| "A broker following USPAP may appraise for a federally related transaction" | Only a state-licensed or certified appraiser |

### Step 8: Guided practice

### Step 9: Independent practice

**I1.** Six units at $1,600 per month each. Vacancy and collection loss: 5%. Yearly costs: property taxes $12,400; insurance $9,800; repairs and maintenance $6,900; management $5,500; reserves $2,400; mortgage principal and interest $41,000; owner's tax depreciation $9,000. <details><summary>Check your answer</summary>

</details>

<details><summary>Check your answer</summary>

## 4. Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Income capitalization approach | Value from income; for income property; IRV |
| PGI | Potential gross income: full rent if fully occupied and paid |
| Vacancy and collection loss | Income lost to empty units and unpaid rent |
| EGI | Effective gross income = PGI − vacancy and collection loss |
| Operating expenses | Fixed, variable and reserves; not debt service or depreciation |
| NOI | Net operating income = EGI − operating expenses |
| Capitalization rate | NOI ÷ value |
| IRV | Income = Rate × Value; Value = NOI ÷ rate |
| GRM / GIM | Price ÷ monthly gross rent / price ÷ annual gross income |
| Reconciliation | Weighing the approaches to reach a final value (not averaging) |
| USPAP | Uniform Standards of Professional Appraisal Practice |
| Federally related transaction | Exam cue: the appraisal must be done by a state-licensed or certified appraiser (manual; not yet verified). Broadly, a financing transaction overseen by federal regulators that requires an appraisal (detailed definition requires verification) |
| CMA / BPO | A licensee's pricing analysis / a broker price opinion for a lender or investor: chargeable, never an "appraisal" |

## 5. Practical example

**Setting.** Sofia, an investor from Porto, asks you about a fourplex in St. Petersburg listed at $860,000. The seller's flyer says: "4 units × $2,100 = $8,400/month. Taxes $15,200, insurance $13,900. NOI $71,700, cap rate 8.3%. Mortgage payments $38,000."

**Your rebuild:**

| Line | Amount |
|---|---|
| PGI: $8,400 × 12 | $100,800 |
| − Vacancy and collection loss (5%; the flyer ignored it) | −$5,040 |
| = EGI | $95,760 |
| − Taxes $15,200, insurance $13,900 | −$29,100 |
| − Repairs and maintenance $6,100, management $7,660, reserves $2,400 (all missing from the flyer) | −$16,160 |
| **= NOI** | **$50,500** |

The flyer's NOI took only taxes and insurance off the full rent. The mortgage payments are correctly left out of NOI, but they are not the reason for the gap. Recent fourplex sales nearby show a cap rate of 6.25%: $50,500 ÷ 0.0625 = **$808,000**. As a rougher check, those sales show a GIM of 8.5: $100,800 × 8.5 = $856,800. The GIM ignores expenses, so you rely more on the income figure.

Sofia then asks: "Can you give me an appraisal?" You may prepare a price opinion and charge for it, but never call it an appraisal. If her loan is a federally related transaction, the lender's appraisal must come from a state-licensed or certified appraiser. And her own mortgage payments will never enter the property's NOI.

## 6. Portugal comparison

**The familiar principle.** Investors everywhere compare rent with price; you may know this as a "gross yield" (annual rent ÷ price). That is the GIM turned upside down: a GIM of 10 is a 10% gross yield.

**The Florida rule.** The exam distinguishes a gross measure (GRM on **monthly** rent, GIM on **annual** income) from the **cap rate**, which uses **NOI** after vacancy and operating expenses but before debt service and depreciation. The appraisal for a federally related transaction requires a state-licensed or certified appraiser.

**The precise difference.** This course has no verified Portugal–Florida comparison entry on income valuation or on who may value property. Any Portuguese rule on who may value property for banks, courts or tax purposes **requires verification**.

**Exam implication.** Do not confuse a gross yield with a cap rate. Answer "who may appraise" questions with the Florida/US rule only; never let a Portuguese rule answer a Florida question.

## 7. Legal and numerical requirements

**Memorize exactly**

*Appraisal method and appraisal-practice rules: manual; not yet verified (no verification entry covers them).*

- Income capitalization approach = **income property**. **IRV: Value = NOI ÷ cap rate.**
- **PGI − vacancy and collection loss = EGI; EGI − operating expenses (fixed, variable, reserves) = NOI.**
- **Debt service and depreciation are not operating expenses.**
- **GRM** = price ÷ **monthly** gross rent (houses, duplexes). **GIM** = price ÷ **annual** gross income.
- **Reconciliation**: weigh the approaches by reliability for the property; **never a simple average**.
- **USPAP** governs appraisal practice.
- **Federally related transactions** require a **state-licensed or certified appraiser**.
- A licensee may prepare **CMAs and BPOs** and **may charge** for them, but may **never** label them appraisals.
- Per the manual, a licensee doing appraisal work outside federally related transactions must still follow **USPAP**.

*Verified:*

- Appraisal = **8%** of the exam (V-02).
- Tax depreciation periods (27.5 / 39 years, V-77) belong to income tax, not to NOI.

**Understand**

- Why financing and depreciation stay out of NOI.
- Why a higher cap rate means a lower value.
- Why reconciliation weighs instead of averaging.
- Why experience and USPAP knowledge do not make a broker an appraiser for a federally related transaction: the licence class is the barrier.
- Do not confuse "federally related transaction" (the appraisal rule) with RESPA's "federally related mortgage loans" on 1–4 family residential property (V-53). The phrases look alike but belong to different topics: who may appraise here; RESPA's ban on kickbacks and its title-insurer rule there.

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"Mortgage payments are operating expenses."** No. Debt service is never deducted to find NOI.
- **"Depreciation is an operating expense."** No. It is a tax deduction (V-77), not a cost of running the building.
- **"GRM uses annual rent."** No. GRM uses **monthly** rent; GIM uses **annual** income.
- **"A higher cap rate means a higher value."** No. Value = NOI ÷ rate, so a higher rate gives a lower value.
- **"The final value is the average of the three approaches."** No. Reconciliation weighs them by reliability.
- **"An experienced broker who follows USPAP may appraise for a federally related transaction."** No. A state-licensed or certified appraiser is required.
- **"A licensee may not charge for a CMA or BPO."** No. A licensee may charge, but may never call the work an appraisal.

## 10. Memory sheet

- **IRV**: Value = NOI ÷ rate; Rate = NOI ÷ value; NOI = value × rate.
- PGI − vacancy/collection = EGI − operating expenses (fixed, variable, reserves) = **NOI**.
- **NOI excludes debt service and depreciation.**
- **GRM monthly** (houses, duplexes); **GIM annual**.
- Rate up → value down.
- **Reconcile, don't average.**
- **USPAP** governs appraisal.
- Federally related transaction → **state-licensed or certified appraiser**.
- CMA / BPO: may charge, **never** "appraisal".
