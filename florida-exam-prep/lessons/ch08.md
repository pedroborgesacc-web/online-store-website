## 2. Why it matters

Every listing starts with one question: **what exactly does this seller own?** In Florida you answer it by reading the deed and the title work, and those documents describe ownership with the vocabulary in this lesson. A seller who holds only a **life estate** cannot sell the house permanently, and a buyer who receives a **fee simple** gets something very different from a tenant who signs a lease.

For the exam, this lesson opens Section VIII, Property Rights, which is **8%** of the Sales Associate exam (V-02). The questions are short definition tests with a twist. You may be asked to spot the item that is *not* in the bundle of rights, pick the largest estate, say whether a future interest is a **remainder** or a **reversion**, or recognize **waste**. The same vocabulary returns in Chapter 9 (title and deeds).

## 3. Simple explanation

### Real property is land plus rights

In L01-2 you met the difference between *real estate* (land plus permanent improvements) and *real property* (real estate **plus the rights of ownership**). Those rights are called the **bundle of rights**, like a bundle of sticks. There are five:

| Right | What it lets the owner do | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Possession** | Occupy the property | Live in the house |
| **Control** | Decide how the property is used, within the law | Repaint, renovate, add a pool |
| **Enjoyment** | Use it and benefit from it without interference | Live there in peace, earn income from it |
| **Exclusion** | Keep others out | Refuse entry to trespassers |
| **Disposition** | Transfer it | Sell it, give it away, leave it by will |

The manual's mnemonic is **D.E.E.P. C**: Disposition, Enjoyment, Exclusion, Possession, Control.

**Why call it a "bundle"?** Because the sticks can be separated. An owner who leases her condo hands the **possession** stick to the tenant for a while but keeps **disposition**, so she can still sell. The bundle is never unlimited: government powers such as taxation, eminent domain and **escheat** (L09-1) apply to every owner. Escheat is a power of the state, not a right in the bundle.

### An estate measures "how much" and "how long"

An **estate** is an interest in land. It is defined by how much of the bundle it gives and how long it lasts. Estates fall into two families:

- **Freehold estates** are **ownership** for an **indefinite** time. "Indefinite" does not mean "forever". It means the end date is not fixed in advance. Nobody knows the day a person will die, so an estate that ends at someone's death is still a freehold.
- **Leasehold (nonfreehold) estates** are **possession without ownership**, under a lease (L08-2). An estate for years ends on a date known from the start and gives possession, not ownership, so it is a leasehold however long the term is.

### Fee simple absolute: the whole bundle

**Fee simple absolute** is the largest estate. It carries all five rights, has **no conditions**, lasts **without time limit** and is **inheritable**: when the owner dies it passes by will or to heirs. It is the estate a buyer normally expects to receive.

### Fee simple defeasible: ownership with a condition

A **fee simple defeasible** is also full, inheritable ownership, but it **can be lost if a stated condition occurs or is broken**. Example: a landowner deeds a parcel to a town "so long as the land is used as a school." If the school closes and the town turns the land into a parking lot, the town can lose the property. "Defeasible" means "capable of being defeated".

### Life estate: ownership measured by a life

A **life estate** is ownership that lasts only as long as a named person lives.

- **Ordinary life estate:** measured by the **life tenant's** own life, as in "to Bruno for life". It ends when Bruno dies. Bruno cannot leave it by will, and his heirs do not inherit it.
- **Life estate *pur autre vie*** ("for the life of another"): measured by **someone else's** life, as in "to Bruno for the life of his mother, Celeste". It ends when Celeste dies.

**The life tenant's rights:** full use and possession, the right to collect rents, and the right to sell or lease her interest. She can transfer only what she has, though. A buyer or tenant from a life tenant loses the property when the measuring person dies.

**The life tenant's duties:** pay the **property taxes** and the **upkeep**, and **never commit waste**. **Waste** is damage or neglect that harms the value of the property for whoever owns it next. Cutting down and selling all the valuable timber is waste. So is letting the house fall apart.

**Who gets it next?** When a life estate ends, the property goes to the holder of the **future interest**:

- **Remainder:** the property goes **forward to a third person**, the **remainderman**. In "to Bruno for life, then to Carla", Carla is the remainderman.
- **Reversion:** the property **comes back** to the **grantor** (the person who created the estate) or the grantor's heirs. In "to Bruno for life", where nobody is named after Bruno, the property reverts to the grantor (or, if the grantor has died, to the grantor's heirs).

A familiar family arrangement works the other way round. A parent deeds the home to a child but **keeps (reserves) a life estate**. The parent is the life tenant. The child is the remainderman and owns the home outright when the parent dies.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Bundle of rights | The rights of ownership: possession, control, enjoyment, exclusion, disposition |
| Estate | An interest in land, defined by its rights and its duration |
| Freehold estate | Ownership of indefinite duration (fee simple, life estate) |
| Leasehold (nonfreehold) estate | Possession under a lease, without ownership |
| Fee simple absolute | The most complete ownership: inheritable, unconditional, unlimited in time |
| Fee simple defeasible | Ownership that can be lost if a stated condition occurs or is broken |
| Life estate | Ownership limited to the life of a person |

| *Pur autre vie* | A life estate measured by the life of someone other than the life tenant |
| Grantor | The person who conveys (transfers) the property |
| Remainder / remainderman | The future interest that passes to a third person when a life estate ends / that person |
| Reversion | The future interest that returns to the grantor (or heirs) when a life estate ends |
| Waste | Damage or neglect by a life tenant that harms the future interest |
| Escheat | Government power: property passes to the state when an owner dies with no will and no heirs |

## 5. Practical example

**Helena**, 79, lives in a house in Sarasota. In 2018 she deeded it to her daughter **Marta** and **reserved a life estate** for herself. Now Helena wants to move near Marta in Ocala, and she calls you to "sell the house".

| Question | Answer | Why |
|---|---|---|
| What does Helena own? | An ordinary life estate, measured by her own life | She kept the right to the house for her lifetime |
| What does Marta own? | The remainder; she is the remainderman | The property goes forward to her at Helena's death |
| Can Helena sell the house outright on her own? | No. She can sell only her life estate | A buyer from Helena would lose the house when Helena dies |
| Can Helena rent the house out and keep the rent? | Yes | Collecting rents is a life tenant's right |
| Helena signs a 2-year lease and dies 8 months later. Does the lease continue? | No; it ends at her death | She could lease only what she had |
| Who pays the property taxes and repairs while Helena lives? | Helena | Taxes and upkeep are the life tenant's duties |
| Helena's tenant offers to cut down the old oak trees and sell the wood. Can Helena agree? | No; that would be waste | It permanently reduces what Marta will receive |

**Variation:** had the 2018 deed said only "to Marta for life", naming nobody after Marta, the house would come back to Helena (or her heirs) at Marta's death: a **reversion**.

**Practical habit:** when the title work shows a life estate, identify the life tenant and the holder of the future interest before you market the property.

## 6. Portugal comparison

**Familiar principle.** You know **usufruto**: the right to use and enjoy property that belongs to another for a period, often for life (Portuguese side: requires verification). The Florida arrangement in which a parent gives a home to a child but keeps a life estate may therefore look familiar; whether Portuguese practice works the same way requires verification.

**Florida rule.** A **life estate** is an **ownership estate** (a freehold) measured by a life. The life tenant may use, lease or sell the life interest, must pay taxes and must avoid waste. At the measuring death the property passes by **remainder** (to a third person) or by **reversion** (back to the grantor or heirs).

**Precise difference.** The purpose is similar, but the exam uses only Florida vocabulary: life tenant, remainderman, remainder, reversion, waste, *pur autre vie*. Do not carry over Portuguese rules on what a usufructuary may or must do. Whether they match the Florida duties requires verification.

**Exam implication.** Ask one question: where does the property go when the life estate ends? Forward to someone new means **remainder**. Back to the grantor or heirs means **reversion**.

A fixed end date always means a leasehold (an estate for years), but some leaseholds have no fixed end date either (periodic tenancy, tenancy at will; L08-2).

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"A life tenant's lease survives her death."** It ends with the life estate; she could not lease more than she had.
- **Swapping remainder and reversion.** Remainder goes forward to a third person; reversion comes back to the grantor or heirs.
- **"Freehold means forever."** It means the end date is not fixed in advance. A life estate is a freehold.
- **"An estate for years is a freehold if it is long enough."** Any fixed term is a leasehold, however long, and the term can also be very short, even one week.
- **"The life tenant owns the property, so she can do anything with it."** She must not commit waste, and she must pay taxes and upkeep.
- **"A life estate passes to the life tenant's heirs."** An ordinary life estate ends at the life tenant's death. The property goes to the remainderman or reverts to the grantor.
- **"Escheat is one of the owner's rights."** Escheat is a government power. The bundle is possession, control, enjoyment, exclusion and disposition.

## 10. Memory sheet

- **D.E.E.P. C**: Disposition, Enjoyment, Exclusion, Possession, Control.
- Freehold = ownership, end date unknown. Leasehold = possession without ownership (L08-2).
- Fee simple absolute = everything, no conditions, inheritable. Defeasible = "so long as…", so it can be lost.
- Life tenant: uses, rents, sells or leases **only her life interest**; pays taxes and upkeep; **no waste**.
- **Remainder goes forward** (remainderman). **Reversion comes back** (grantor or heirs).
- *Pur autre vie* = measured by someone else's life.

## 2. Why it matters

Florida licensees lease and manage property for owners. Renting and leasing for others for compensation is licensed activity (the R and L in A BAR SALE, L02-1). Commercial deals are negotiated in terms such as **triple net** and **percentage rent**, and when a building is sold you must know which leases the buyer inherits.

On the exam (Section VIII, Property Rights, 8%, V-02) these are mostly recognition questions: a tenancy or lease is described and you name it. The favourite traps are the **estate for years**, which can be shorter than a year, and the **assignment** that looks like a sublease because it covers only part of the original term.

## 3. Simple explanation

### A leasehold is possession without ownership

In L08-1 you saw that a freehold is ownership. A **leasehold (nonfreehold) estate** is the tenant's right to **possess** property for a time **without owning it**. The owner (the **landlord**, or **lessor**) gives possession to the **tenant** (the **lessee**) and keeps ownership, so the landlord can still sell.

### The four leasehold estates

What separates them is **how the tenancy ends**.

| Estate | How it works | How it ends |
|---|---|---|
| **Estate for years** | Any **fixed term** with a definite start and end: one week, 6 months, 3 years | **Automatically** on the end date; **no notice** needed |
| **Periodic tenancy** | Renews automatically **period to period**: week to week, month to month, quarter to quarter, year to year | Only when a party gives **notice** |
| **Tenancy at will** | No fixed duration | By **proper notice** |
| **Tenancy at sufferance** | A **holdover** tenant: the right to possess has ended, and the tenant stays **without the landlord's consent** | There is no agreement to end; the tenant is there without consent |

**Why does an estate for years need no notice?** Both parties signed knowing the end date, so the calendar ends it. The name misleads on purpose: "for years" does not mean "at least a year".

**Why does a periodic tenancy need notice?** It renews itself every period, so someone must stop the renewal. For Florida **residential** tenancies without a specific term, s. 83.57 (current text, verified V-04) requires **written notice before the end of a period**:

| Period | Minimum notice |
|---|---|
| Year to year | 60 days |
| Quarter to quarter | 30 days |
| Month to month | 30 days |
| Week to week | 7 days |

The study manual gives **15 days** for month to month and **45 days** for quarter to quarter. Fifteen days for month to month is the **outdated** pre-change rule that older books and some practice questions still use; the manual's 45 days for quarter to quarter does not match the verified statute. Use **60 / 30 / 30 / 7** (more in L07-4). If a question uses 15 days, it reflects the old law: answer with current law unless the question says otherwise (V-04).

### The lease as a document

A **lease** is two things at once:

- a **contract**: promises about rent, use and repairs; and
- a **conveyance**: it transfers a leasehold estate (possession) to the tenant.

Because the lease conveys an estate, the tenant's right does not disappear when the landlord sells. **A lease survives the sale**, and the buyer takes the property **subject to existing leases**.

**Elements of a lease:** parties, description of the property, term, rent, signatures.

**Writing and witnesses:**

- A lease for **more than one year** must be **in writing** (statute of frauds); a lease of one year or less may be oral (verified V-05).
- **No witnesses are required for a lease.** Current s. 689.01 still requires **two subscribing witnesses** on deeds, but it now states that no subscribing witnesses are required for a lease of real property (verified V-05). The manual's rule that leases over one year need two witnesses is **outdated**.

### Lease types: who pays the property's expenses?

| Lease type | How rent works | Where you see it |
|---|---|---|
| **Gross** | Tenant pays a **fixed rent**; the **landlord pays** the operating expenses | Typical for apartments |
| **Net** | Tenant pays rent **plus some or all** of the property expenses | Common in commercial property |
| **Triple net** | Tenant pays rent plus **taxes, insurance and maintenance** (all three) | Common in commercial property |
| **Percentage** | **Base rent plus a percentage of the tenant's gross sales**, usually only on sales above a **breakpoint** | Retail stores |
| **Ground** | A **long-term lease of land**; the **tenant builds** on it | Land for a tenant's building |
| **Graduated (step-up)** | Rent rises on a **schedule written in the lease** | Increases known in advance |
| **Index** | Rent moves with an **index**, such as the **CPI** (Consumer Price Index, a measure of inflation) | Increases follow inflation, not a schedule |

**Percentage rent, worked through (illustrative numbers).** A boutique in an Orlando shopping center pays base rent of **$4,000 a month** plus **5%** of annual gross sales above a **$600,000** breakpoint. This year's gross sales are **$750,000**.

- Base rent: $4,000 × 12 = **$48,000**
- Sales above the breakpoint: $750,000 − $600,000 = $150,000
- Percentage rent: $150,000 × 0.05 = **$7,500**
- Total rent for the year: $48,000 + $7,500 = **$55,500**

### Assignment vs sublease

A tenant who wants to leave early, or to share the space, can transfer the leasehold in one of two ways. **The lease itself can restrict either one.**

| | **Assignment** | **Sublease** |
|---|---|---|
| What is transferred | The **entire remaining term**: all the time left and all the space | **Part** of the remaining term **or** part of the space |
| New occupant | **Assignee** | **Subtenant** |
| Who pays the landlord | The **assignee pays the landlord** directly | The **subtenant pays the original tenant**, who pays the landlord |
| Original tenant's liability | **Secondarily liable** (a backup if the assignee does not pay), **unless the landlord releases** her | Stays **primarily liable** to the landlord |

**Measure against what remains, not against the original term.** Suppose a tenant has 20 months left on a 5-year lease. If she transfers all 20 months, that is an **assignment**, even though 20 months is a fraction of the original 60. If she transfers 12 of the 20 months, or half the space, it is a **sublease**.

A **release** ends the original tenant's backup liability. One way to get it is a **novation**: a new agreement with the landlord that puts the new tenant in place of the original one and releases her.

## 5. Practical example

**Bayshore Plaza** is a small mixed-use building in Tampa. Its owner, **Mr. Ferreira**, is selling it, and you are preparing the list of tenants for buyers.

| Space | Facts | Classification |
|---|---|---|
| Café | 5-year lease with a fixed end date; pays rent plus property taxes, insurance and maintenance | Estate for years; **triple net** lease |
| Shoe store | 3-year lease; base rent plus 6% of gross sales above $400,000 a year | Estate for years; **percentage** lease |
| Apartment upstairs | Written agreement with no end date; rent is due monthly, and it renews month to month | **Periodic tenancy** (month to month) |

Three things happen before closing:

1. **The café owner sells her business** with 20 months left on the lease and transfers all 20 months to the buyer of the business, who will pay Mr. Ferreira directly. This is an **assignment**. The café owner stays **secondarily liable** unless Mr. Ferreira releases her.
2. **The shoe store rents half its space** to a phone-repair kiosk. This is a **sublease** (part of the space). The kiosk pays the shoe store, and the shoe store stays **primarily liable** to Mr. Ferreira.
3. **Mr. Ferreira wants the apartment empty** for the new owner. He must give **written notice at least 30 days before the end of a monthly period** (s. 83.57, V-04).

**The sale itself:** the buyer of Bayshore Plaza takes the building **subject to** the café's and the shoe store's leases. The new owner cannot cancel them just because the building changed hands.

## 6. Portugal comparison

**Familiar principle.** You already know residential and commercial leasing in practice. In Portugal, urban leases are governed by the Civil Code and the NRAU; their written-form and registration requirements require verification.

**Florida rule.** A lease of one year or less may be oral. A longer lease must be in writing (statute of frauds) but needs **no witnesses** (s. 689.01, V-05). Residential tenancies without a specific term end with **60 / 30 / 30 / 7** days' written notice (s. 83.57, V-04). The exam also uses US leasing vocabulary: gross, net, triple net, percentage, ground, graduated, index, assignment and sublease.

**Precise difference.** Florida's day counts and categories are what is tested. Portuguese notice periods must not be transferred. Whether Portuguese law draws the same assignment and sublease liability lines requires verification.

**Exam implication.** For any notice question, use the current s. 83.57 figures. For any classification question, apply the Florida definitions literally.

## 7. Legal and numerical requirements

**Memorize exactly**

- **Estate for years:** any fixed term, even one week; ends **automatically without notice**.
- **Periodic residential tenancy, notice to end** (s. 83.57, current; verified V-04): **year 60 · quarter 30 · month 30 · week 7 days**, in writing, before the end of a period. **Outdated:** 15 days for month to month (pre-change law; answer with current law unless the question says otherwise). The manual's 45 days for quarter to quarter does not match the verified statute.
- **Tenancy at will:** no fixed duration; ends with proper notice. **Tenancy at sufferance:** holdover without consent.
- A lease over **one year** must be **in writing** (statute of frauds) (verified V-05).
- **No subscribing witnesses** are required for leases (s. 689.01, verified V-05). **Outdated:** "two witnesses for leases over one year". Deeds still need two witnesses.
- **Triple net** = taxes + insurance + maintenance.
- **Assignment** = the entire remaining term; the assignee pays the landlord; the original tenant is **secondarily** liable unless released.
- **Sublease** = part of the term or space; the subtenant pays the tenant; the tenant stays **primarily** liable.
- A lease **survives the sale**; the buyer takes subject to existing leases.

**Understand**

- Source status: the leasehold categories, lease elements, lease types, assignment/sublease rules and the rule that a lease survives a sale come from the study manual. The s. 83.57 notice periods (V-04) and the witness rule (V-05) are verified corrections of the manual; the writing requirement for leases over one year is confirmed in V-05.
- Liability follows the chain of payment. An assignment takes the original tenant out of the payment chain, but she stays a backup until released. A sublease keeps her in the middle of the chain.
- Related disclosure (L07-4): a **residential lease of 1 year or longer** requires the landlord's **flood disclosure** at or before signing (since October 1, 2025; verified V-07). Shorter leases are not covered. Older books will not mention it.

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"An estate for years must last a year and needs notice to end."** It is any fixed term, even one week, and the end date ends it automatically.
- **"Transferring the last 14 months of a 3-year lease is a sublease."** It is an assignment. Measure against what remains.
- **"After an assignment, the original tenant is free."** She stays secondarily liable unless the landlord releases her.
- **"A subtenant pays the landlord."** The subtenant pays the original tenant, who stays primarily liable.
- **"Month to month needs 15 days' notice."** That is outdated. Current s. 83.57 requires 30 days.
- **"Florida leases over one year need two witnesses."** That is outdated. No witnesses are required, although a lease over one year must still be in writing.
- **"A gross lease is based on gross sales."** A gross lease has fixed rent, and the landlord pays expenses. The lease based on sales is the percentage lease.
- **"When the building is sold, the leases end."** The buyer takes subject to existing leases.

## 10. Memory sheet

- Fixed term = **estate for years** (no notice). Renewing = **periodic** (60 / 30 / 30 / 7). No fixed duration = **at will**. Holdover without consent = **sufferance**.
- Lease = contract + conveyance, and it **survives the sale**.
- Over 1 year → in writing. Witnesses → **none** now (deeds still need two).
- Gross: landlord pays expenses. Net: tenant pays some or all. Triple net: taxes + insurance + maintenance. Percentage: base + % of sales above the breakpoint. Ground: land, tenant builds. Graduated: steps. Index: CPI.
- **All that remains = assignment** (assignee → landlord; tenant secondary). **Part = sublease** (subtenant → tenant; tenant primary).

## 2. Why it matters

When two or more people own one property, the form of co-ownership decides three practical things: **who must sign** to sell it, **whether a creditor of one owner can reach it**, and **who owns it when one owner dies**. You will see the clue on almost every deed you read: "husband and wife", "as joint tenants with right of survivorship", or two names with nothing added.

The exam (Section VIII, Property Rights, 8%, V-02) usually tests this with a **death scenario** or a **silent deed**. Two facts hidden in the question decide the answer: **are the owners married?** and **does the deed say "with right of survivorship"?** A third trap targets people trained in other legal systems: **Florida is not a community-property state**.

## 3. Simple explanation

### Undivided interests

Co-owners do not each own a physical piece of the property. Each owns an **undivided interest**: a share of the whole, together with the right to use the whole alongside the others. Two co-owners of a duplex do not own "the left unit" and "the right unit". Each owns a share of the entire duplex.

### Tenancy in common: Florida's default for unmarried co-owners

When a deed conveys property to two or more **unmarried** people and **says nothing** about how they hold title, they are **tenants in common**.

- Shares **may be unequal**, for example 70% and 30%.
- Each owner may **sell or mortgage his own share** without the others.
- There is **no survivorship**. When an owner dies, his share passes **under his will** (to his devisees) or, if he has no will, **to his heirs**. It does not go to the other co-owners.

Think of it as the "no surprises" form: at death, each share follows its owner's will or goes to his heirs, never automatically to the other co-owners.

### Joint tenancy with right of survivorship

When a joint tenant dies, **his share passes automatically to the surviving joint tenants, outside probate**. (Probate is the court process that settles a deceased person's estate.) His heirs and his will do not receive it.

A joint tenancy has two requirements:

1. **The four unities, PITT:**
   - **P**ossession: each has an equal right to possess the whole.
   - **I**nterest: each holds an equal share.
   - **T**ime: all acquire their interests at the same time.
   - **T**itle: all acquire them through the same document, usually the same deed.
2. **In Florida, express survivorship language in the deed**, such as "as joint tenants with right of survivorship". Without those words, unmarried co-owners are tenants in common.

**Why are the words required?** Survivorship takes a share away from the deceased owner's heirs and will, so Florida does not presume it. The deed must say it.

### Tenancy by the entireties: married couples

A married couple can hold title **as one unit**. A tenancy by the entireties requires the unities **plus marriage**.

- **Automatic survivorship:** when one spouse dies, the survivor owns the whole property. No words are needed.
- **Neither spouse can convey alone:** a deed needs both spouses.
- **Protection from creditors of only one spouse:** a creditor holding a judgment against one spouse alone cannot force a sale. Neither spouse owns a separate "half" that the creditor could take.
- **Presumption:** a deed to a married couple is **presumed** to create a tenancy by the entireties, even if it says nothing more.

### What Florida does not have

Florida is **not a community-property state**; it is a common-law state. Title follows **the deed and marital status**, not a marital property regime. One overlap to keep separate: if the property is the family **homestead**, the spouse must join in any deed or mortgage even when title is in one name (manual; not yet verified). That is a separate homestead rule, covered in L08-4.

### Partition: the exit door

**Any co-tenant may sue for partition.** Partition is a court action that divides the co-owned property or forces its sale. It is the remedy when co-owners cannot agree.

## 5. Practical example

You work for a brokerage in Fort Myers. Three files arrive in the same week.

**File 1: duplex in Fort Myers.** Deed: "to Tiago Mendes, an undivided 70% interest, and Beatriz Faria, an undivided 30% interest". They are not married, and there are no survivorship words. Tiago dies, and his will leaves everything to his nephew Rui.

- Form: **tenancy in common** (unmarried, no survivorship words; unequal shares are allowed).
- Result: **Rui owns 70%** as a tenant in common with Beatriz. Beatriz receives nothing from Tiago's share.
- Practice point: either co-owner may sell his or her own share alone, but a buyer of the whole duplex needs deeds from both Rui and Beatriz. If they cannot agree, either may sue for **partition**.

**File 2: condo in Naples.** Deed: "to Rita Lopes and Paulo Lopes, as joint tenants with right of survivorship". Rita and Paulo are sister and brother, not a married couple. One deed, one date, equal shares. Rita dies, and her will leaves "all my property" to her son.

- Form: **joint tenancy with right of survivorship**. The four unities and the express words are both present.
- Result: **Paulo owns the whole condo**, outside probate. Rita's share passed to Paulo at her death, so her will does not reach the condo.

**File 3: house in Cape Coral.** Deed: "to João Silva and Maria Silva, husband and wife", with nothing more. A supplier wins a judgment against João alone for a business debt and wants the house sold.

- Form: **presumed tenancy by the entireties** (a married couple, and a deed to both).
- Result: the creditor of only one spouse **cannot force a sale**. To sell the house, **both** João and Maria must sign the deed. If João dies, Maria owns the whole house automatically.

## 6. Portugal comparison

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"Two names on a deed means joint tenancy."** In Florida, unmarried co-owners with a silent deed are tenants in common. Survivorship needs express words.
- **"A tenant in common's share goes to the other owner at death."** There is no survivorship; the share goes by will or to heirs.
- **"A joint tenant can leave his share to his children by will."** At his death the share passes to the surviving joint tenants, outside probate.
- **"Florida uses community property."** It does not. Title depends on the deed and on marital status.
- **"A creditor of the husband can take the husband's half of entireties property."** Neither spouse holds a separate half, and a creditor of only one spouse cannot reach the property.
- **"One spouse can sell entireties property if the other refuses."** Neither spouse can convey alone.
- **"Tenants in common must hold equal shares."** Shares may be unequal.
- **"Unmarried partners can hold as tenants by the entireties."** Only married couples can.
- **"PITT stands for possession, income, time, title."** It is Possession, **Interest**, Time, Title.

## 2. Why it matters

"Can the seller's creditors take this house?" In Florida that question has a constitutional answer, and it affects your work directly:

- **Who must sign.** If the home is a homestead and the owner is married, the spouse must join in the deed or mortgage even when title is in one name only (manual; not yet verified). A missing signature is a closing problem you can spot at listing.
- **Which debts follow the house.** A credit-card judgment creditor cannot force a sale of the homestead, and the judgment is not even a lien on it (V-66). Unpaid property taxes, the home's own mortgage and a contractor who was not paid for work on the home can all reach it.
- **Exam.** Property Rights is 8% of the state exam (V-02). Questions test the two size limits, the short list of claims that still reach a homestead, the spouse's role, and, as a favourite trap, whether you keep creditor protection separate from the $25,000 property-tax exemption.

## 3. Simple explanation

### One word, three different rules

In Florida, "homestead" is the name of three separate legal doctrines. They share only the word.

| # | Rule | What it does | Where taught |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Creditor protection** (Florida Constitution, Art. X, s. 4) | Shields the home from forced sale by most creditors | This lesson |
| 2 | **Family protection** (transfer restrictions) | Limits what the owner can do alone with the home when there is a spouse or minor child | This lesson |
| 3 | **Property-tax exemption** | Reduces the taxable value of the home | Chapter 18 |

### Rule 1: the shield against forced sale

Start with three words. A **judgment** is a court decision that a person owes money. A **forced sale** is a court-ordered sale of the debtor's property to pay that judgment. A **lien** is a legal claim against property that secures a debt.

A homestead is the owner's primary residence, the home the owner actually lives in. The Florida Constitution says that a homestead owned by a **natural person** (a human being, not a company) is **exempt from forced sale** under court process, and **no judgment, decree or execution is a lien on it** (V-66, confirmed). A decree and an execution are further court orders; the execution is the order used to enforce a judgment. In plain terms: an ordinary creditor who wins a lawsuit cannot make the court sell the owner's home, and the judgment does not even attach to it. The aim is to keep the family housed even if the owner falls into debt.

The shield protects a home, not unlimited land, so it has size limits:

| Location | Protected area (V-66) | Extra qualification |
|---|---|---|
| Inside a **municipality** (a city or town) | Up to **1/2 acre of contiguous land** | Limited to the residence of the owner or the owner's family |
| Outside a municipality | Up to **160 acres of contiguous land**, plus improvements | If the land is later brought into a municipality, the 160 acres cannot be reduced without the owner's consent |

**Contiguous** means touching: one connected piece of land, not separate lots. For scale: an acre is 43,560 square feet, so half an acre is 21,780 sq ft (roughly 2,000 m²); 160 acres is roughly 65 hectares. These conversions are only to help you picture the area; the legal figures are 1/2 acre and 160 acres.

### Who still gets through the shield

The constitution lists the claims the shield does **not** stop (V-66; s. 222.01(5), F.S. restates the same exceptions):

1. **Taxes and assessments** on the property, such as unpaid county property taxes.
2. **Obligations contracted for its purchase, improvement or repair.** This covers the loan that financed the purchase of the home, and debts for improving or repairing it. The manual states the practical point simply: the protection does not stop **the mortgage on the home itself** (manual; not yet verified).
3. **Obligations for house, field or other labor performed on the property.**

Items 2 and 3 together cover **construction liens** (also called mechanic's liens): liens for labor or materials used to improve the home.

The manual adds that **federal tax (IRS) liens** can also reach a homestead (manual; not yet verified).

**Why these?** Look at the pattern. The claims that get through come from the government that taxes this property and from the people who paid for, built or repaired this very house. The shield protects the home from *unrelated* debts (credit cards, medical bills, car loans, business debts), not from the costs of the home itself. The IRS lien from the manual is the one item that does not fit this pattern: it is not on the constitution's list.

Two smaller verified details from the same section (V-66):

- It also exempts **$1,000 of personal property**. This is a different number for a different thing; do not confuse it with the $25,000 tax exemption.
- The exemptions **pass to the surviving spouse or heirs** when the owner dies.

### Rule 2: the family lock (transfer restrictions)

The second doctrine protects the owner's family from the owner acting alone.

- **The spouse joins.** If the owner is married, the **spouse must join** in any deed or mortgage of the homestead, **even if title is in one spouse's name** (manual; not yet verified). "Join" means the spouse signs the document too.
- **Devise is restricted.** To **devise** is to leave real property by will. If the owner has a **spouse or minor child**, the homestead cannot be freely devised away from them (manual; not yet verified).

**Why?** Without these rules, one spouse alone could sell, mortgage or will away the family's home. This limits the **owner's** power to transfer; it is not a rule about creditors.

### Rule 3: the tax exemption belongs to another chapter

The property-tax exemption (the first $25,000 exempt from all property taxes, plus a second exemption that applies to non-school taxes only; V-09) is taught in Chapter 18. It has its own conditions and numbers. A proposed 2026 homestead property-tax amendment (V-23) is **pending law, not law**. It is a property-tax proposal and is not exam content.

**The Reyes home in Tampa.** Daniel Reyes owns a house on a **0.3-acre** lot inside the City of Tampa and lives there with his wife Sofia and their 9-year-old son. Title is in Daniel's name only. He has fallen behind on several debts. Which creditors can force a sale?

## 2. Why it matters

Florida is full of shared-ownership property: condominium towers, cooperative buildings (for example in Miami Beach), timeshare resorts and HOA communities. For each sale you must know what the buyer actually gets (real property or shares), which documents the buyer must receive and when, and how long the buyer can walk away. A miscounted cancellation right can undo a sale. If a condo buyer validly cancels under s. 718.503, the broker may return the deposit without notifying the Florida Real Estate Commission (FREC) (V-16).

**Exam.** Property Rights is 8% of the state exam (V-02). Two classic questions: "Which buyer owns real property?" and "15, 7, 10 or 3 days?" Older books and the manual give the condo resale period as **3 business days**, which is **outdated** since July 1, 2025.

## 3. Simple explanation

### Start with one question: what does the buyer own?

| Form | The buyer receives | Type of property |
|---|---|---|
| **Condominium** | The unit in fee simple (a deed) + an undivided share of the common elements | Real property |
| **Cooperative** | Shares in the corporation that owns the building + a proprietary lease | Personal property |

### Condominium

A condominium is **created by recording a declaration of condominium** (manual; not yet verified). The buyer owns the **unit** in **fee simple** (full ownership, as in L08-1) plus an **undivided share of the common elements**: the parts owned in common, such as the lobby, pool and roof. "Undivided" means a share of the whole, not a specific piece. That share **cannot be separated from the unit**: you cannot sell the unit and keep your share of the pool (manual; not yet verified). The buyer receives a deed to real estate, so a condo is **real property**.

Buyers receive the declaration, bylaws, rules, FAQ and financial information (manual; not yet verified). The verified resale rule (V-08) refers to the declaration, articles, bylaws, rules and other documents.

### Cooperative (co-op)

In a cooperative, a **corporation owns the whole building**. The buyer purchases **shares** in that corporation plus a **proprietary lease**, which is a lease giving the shareholder the right to occupy a particular unit. The buyer owns shares and a lease, not land, so the interest is **personal property**, and the board usually approves buyers (manual; not yet verified).

### Why cancellation ("cooling-off") rights exist

Buyers often sign before reading long association documents, so Florida law gives certain buyers a window to read and walk away. Each form has its **own** window.

**Condominium bought from the developer** (s. 718.503; V-08, double-checked; the developer period was not changed):

- **Voidable** (the buyer may choose to cancel) within **15 days** after the buyer **signs and receives all required documents**.
- Also voidable within **15 days after receiving an amendment that materially and adversely changes the offering**.
- Waivers are void. The right **ends at closing**.

**Condominium resale** (a nondeveloper sale of a residential unit; s. 718.503; V-08, corrected and double-checked). The contract must say, in conspicuous type (printed so that it stands out), **one** of two things:

1. the buyer received the declaration, articles, bylaws, rules and other documents **more than 7 days** (excluding Saturdays, Sundays and legal holidays) **before signing**; or
2. the contract is **voidable by written notice within 7 days, excluding Saturdays, Sundays and legal holidays**, after the buyer signs and receives the documents.

A contract that does not conform is voidable by the buyer **before closing**. The right **ends at closing**. The 7-day period applies since **July 1, 2025** (CS/CS/HB 913, ch. 2025-175). The 2024 text read 3 days, and older books say **3 business days: outdated**. Some older study notes treat the resale period as unresolved; the official-source check (V-08) has settled it, so learn 7 days.

**Timeshare** (s. 721.10; V-64, confirmed). A timeshare is the right to use a unit for periods (typically a set period each year). The purchaser may cancel **until midnight of the 10th calendar day** after the **later** of (a) signing the contract or (b) receiving the last document the developer must provide. Calendar days include weekends. The right **cannot be waived** by the purchaser or anyone acting for the purchaser. Trying to obtain a waiver, or holding a closing before the period ends, is unlawful, and such a closing is voidable at the purchaser's option. **Flag:** the official excerpts disagree on how long that closing stays voidable (1 year versus 5 years). This does not affect the 10-day rule.

No cooperative cancellation period is taught in this course. Do not apply the condo numbers to a co-op.

### HOA (homeowners' association)

An HOA community has **mandatory membership** and **recorded covenants**, meaning binding rules recorded against the parcels (manual; not yet verified). Under s. 720.401 (V-65, confirmed):

- The buyer must receive the **HOA disclosure summary before signing the contract**.
- If the buyer did not receive it first, the buyer may **void the contract by written notice** to the seller or the seller's agent **within 3 days after receiving the summary or before closing, whichever occurs first**.
- The right **cannot be waived** but **ends at closing**.
- Each covered sale contract must contain a **conspicuous clause** describing this right.
- The section does **not** apply to associations regulated under chapters 718, 719, 721 or 723: **condominiums, cooperatives and timeshares** (and chapter 723 associations) do not use the HOA summary rule.

### CDD (Community Development District)

A CDD is a **special-purpose local government** that finances community infrastructure and charges **assessments** to the property owners. It is named in the FREC section title but is **not covered in the manual**, and this definition is not yet verified. Do not confuse it with an HOA: an HOA is an owners' association governed by covenants, while a CDD is a unit of local government.

## 5. Practical example

**One agent, five buyers in a week.** You work at a brokerage in South Florida.

| Buyer | Facts | What applies |
|---|---|---|
| **Ana**, new condo in Miami | Signs the developer contract on Monday; receives all required documents on Wednesday. Two months later the developer sends an amendment that materially and adversely changes the offering | 15 days run from Wednesday, when both signing and receipt have happened. The amendment opens a new 15-day window after she receives it. Both end at closing |
| **Bruno**, resale condo in Fort Lauderdale | Signs and receives the association documents on a Thursday; the contract has the 7-day clause | 7 days excluding Saturdays, Sundays and legal holidays. With no holiday in between, that window covers at least the whole following week. If he cancels, the broker may return his deposit without notifying the FREC (V-16) |
| **Carla**, co-op in Miami Beach | Receives a stock certificate and a proprietary lease; the board must approve her | She owns **personal property**, not real estate (manual; not yet verified). The condo periods do not apply |
| **Diego**, timeshare in Orlando | Signs on Saturday; receives the last required document on Tuesday | The later date is Tuesday. He may cancel until midnight of the 10th calendar day after it, which is Friday of the following week. The seller may not ask him to waive this or close earlier |
| **Elena**, house in an HOA community near Tampa | Signed without the disclosure summary; it arrives 20 days before closing | She may void within 3 days after receiving it. Here the 3 days end first; after that the right is gone |

**Variation on Elena:** if the summary had arrived the day before closing, closing would come first and her right would end at closing.

## 6. Portugal comparison

**Familiar principle.** In Portugal, apartment buildings are commonly divided under *propriedade horizontal*: separate fractions plus common parts (requires verification).

**Florida rule.** The **condominium** is the closest match: a recorded declaration creates it, and the owner holds the unit in fee simple plus an undivided share of the common elements, which is real property (manual; not yet verified). The **cooperative** is different: a corporation owns the building, and the buyer holds shares plus a proprietary lease, which is **personal property**.

**Precise difference.** A Florida condo will feel familiar. A cooperative, where the buyer owns shares and not real estate, is unfamiliar to a Portuguese professional and is tested. Our comparison entries contain no Portuguese facts on timeshares, HOAs, CDDs or cooling-off periods; any Portuguese parallel there requires verification.

**Exam implication.** Co-op = personal property. Condo developer sale = 15-day cancellation. Every number comes from the Florida statute, never from Portuguese practice.

*Memory aid: Condo = deed; co-op = shares + lease.*

**Memorize exactly**

| Situation | Buyer's right | Ends | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Condo from the developer | Voidable within **15 days** after signing and receiving all required documents; also within 15 days after receiving a materially adverse amendment; waivers void | At closing | Verified (V-08) |
| Condo resale (residential unit) | Documents received more than 7 days (excl. Sat., Sun., legal holidays) before signing, **or** voidable by written notice within **7 days excl. Sat., Sun., legal holidays** after signing and receiving the documents; a non-conforming contract is voidable before closing | At closing | Verified (V-08, corrected). Since July 1, 2025; **3 business days is outdated** |
| Timeshare | Cancel until **midnight of the 10th calendar day** after the later of signing or receiving the last required document; **cannot be waived** | Midnight of the 10th calendar day. A closing before then is unlawful and voidable at the purchaser's option (how long it stays voidable is flagged: 1 vs 5 years) | Verified (V-64) |
| HOA parcel | Summary **before signing**. If not, void by written notice within **3 days after receipt or before closing, whichever occurs first**; not waivable | At closing | Verified (V-65) |

Structural facts:

- Condo created by recording a declaration; unit in fee simple + inseparable undivided share of the common elements (manual; not yet verified).
- Co-op = shares + proprietary lease = personal property; board approval typical (manual; not yet verified).
- The HOA summary rule does not apply to associations under chapters 718, 719, 721 or 723 (V-65).
- CDD = special-purpose local government financing infrastructure through assessments (not in the manual; not yet verified).

**Understand**

- Identify the **form** (condo, co-op, timeshare, HOA), then the **seller** (developer or owner), then pick the number.
- Condo resale days **exclude** Saturdays, Sundays and legal holidays; timeshare days are **calendar** days.
- "Whichever occurs first" (HOA) means closing can cut the 3 days short.
- A question using 3 days for a condo resale reflects pre-July 2025 law: answer with current law unless the question says otherwise.

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"Co-op buyers own real estate."** They own shares and a proprietary lease, which is personal property (manual; not yet verified).
- **"Resale condo buyers have 3 business days."** That is outdated. Since July 1, 2025, it is 7 days excluding Saturdays, Sundays and legal holidays (V-08).
- **Mixing 15 / 7 / 10.** 15 = developer condo, 7 = resale condo, 10 = timeshare (and 3 = HOA).
- **"Timeshare buyers have 10 business days and can waive them to close sooner."** They are calendar days, counted from the *later* of signing or receiving the last document, and cannot be waived; seeking a waiver or closing early is unlawful.
- **"An HOA buyer who got the summary late can cancel any time before closing."** Only within 3 days after receiving it, or before closing, whichever occurs first.
- **"Condo buyers get the HOA disclosure summary."** The HOA rule does not apply to condominiums, co-ops or timeshares.
- **"Cancellation rights survive closing."** The condo and HOA rights end at closing.
- **"A condo is created by the first sale or by forming the association."** It is created by recording the declaration of condominium (manual; not yet verified).
- **"A CDD is just another HOA."** A CDD is a special-purpose local government; an HOA is an owners' association.
