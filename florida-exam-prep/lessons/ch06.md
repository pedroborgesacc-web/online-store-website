## 2. Why it matters

When someone reports a Florida licensee, the case does not go straight to a penalty. It moves through a fixed sequence of steps, and each step belongs to a different body: the DBPR, a FREC panel, the FREC itself, a judge, and an appeals court. The exam tests the **order of the steps**, **who acts at each step**, and the **30-day deadline** to appeal.

This lesson, with L06-2 and L06-3, makes up Section 6 of the official exam outline (*Violations, Penalties and Procedures*, **3%**, about 3 of the 100 questions). In practice, if a letter from the DBPR ever arrives, this lesson tells you where you are in the process and which choices are yours.

## 3. Simple explanation

### Why the process has separate steps

A license is a person's livelihood, so the state does not act on an accusation alone. The people who **investigate** (DBPR) are not the people who **decide** (FREC); a **probable cause panel** screens out weak cases before formal charges; the licensee gets a **hearing**; and an independent **court** can review the result.

### The seven steps

The sequence comes from the study manual and is **partially verified**: the probable cause panel (V-42) and the appeal (V-43) are confirmed from official sources; the other steps are taught as the manual states them (manual; not yet verified).

1. **Complaint.** Filed with the **DBPR**. It must be **legally sufficient**: it alleges facts that, if true, would violate a statute or rule. It need not come from someone who was harmed, and an anonymous complaint can be investigated if it is legally sufficient and substantial.
2. **Investigation.** The **DBPR** investigates. The licensee is generally notified and may respond.
3. **Probable cause.** A FREC **probable cause panel** reviews the investigation and decides, by **majority vote**, whether there is enough evidence to justify formal charges. If not, no formal complaint is filed.
4. **Formal complaint.** If probable cause exists, a **formal (administrative) complaint** is filed against the licensee.
5. **Hearing.** The licensee chooses an **informal hearing** before the FREC when the facts are **not disputed**, or a **formal hearing** before an **administrative law judge (ALJ)** when the facts **are disputed**. The ALJ writes a **recommended order**.
6. **Final order.** The **FREC** issues the final order, which sets any penalty (L06-2).
7. **Appeal.** A party adversely affected may seek review in a **District Court of Appeal**. The notice of appeal must be filed within **30 days after rendition** (official issuing) of the order (s. 120.68, F.S.).

**Withdrawing the complaint does not stop the process.** The logic: discipline protects the public, so once the DBPR has a legally sufficient complaint, the case belongs to the state, not to the person who complained.

### Who sits on the probable cause panel

| Source | What it says |
|---|---|
| s. 455.225(4), F.S.; Rule 61J2-20.009, F.A.C. (verified, V-42) | **At least two members**, including a **present** FREC member; a **former** FREC member may serve. Probable cause is decided by **majority vote**. |
| Flag | The current rule's wording on panel size was seen only in fragments. |
| Exam answer (manual) | **Two members, at least one a current FREC member.** This fits the verified rule. |

### The appeal, precisely

- **Deadline:** notice of appeal within **30 days after rendition** of the final order (s. 120.68; Fla. R. App. P. 9.110(c)).
- **Filing:** with the **agency clerk**; a copy, with the filing fees, goes to the court clerk.
- **Court:** the District Court of Appeal for the district where the agency has its headquarters or where a party resides (or as otherwise provided by law).
- **Late notice:** an **irremediable jurisdictional defect**. The court cannot hear the appeal at all.

## 5. Practical example

**Renata**, a sales associate in Orlando, sells a townhouse to **Mr. Okafor**. After closing he learns the community has a mandatory homeowners' association with monthly fees. He says Renata wrote to him that there was none, and he complains to the DBPR.

| Step | What happens | Who acts |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Complaint | Alleges a false statement of a material fact: legally sufficient | DBPR |
| 2. Investigation | Listing, contract and emails collected; Renata responds | DBPR |
| (meanwhile) | Mr. Okafor settles privately and withdraws. **The case continues.** | — |
| 3. Probable cause | Two-member panel (one current, one former FREC member) votes yes | FREC panel |
| 4. Formal complaint | Formal charges filed | DBPR |
| 5. Hearing | Renata says a later email corrected her mistake. Facts disputed → formal hearing → recommended order | ALJ |
| 6. Final order | Fine and probation | FREC |
| 7. Appeal | Notice filed with the agency clerk within 30 days after rendition; copy and fees to the court clerk | District Court of Appeal |

**Variations**

- If Renata agreed with the facts, she could choose an **informal hearing** before the FREC.
- A notice filed on day 34 is too late; the court has no power to hear the appeal.
- Mr. Okafor gets no money from this process. The FREC does not award damages; he would need a civil judgment (and possibly the Recovery Fund, L06-3).

## 6. Portugal comparison

**Familiar principle.** You are used to a public regulator supervising real estate intermediaries. In Portugal this is usually associated with **IMPIC, I.P.**, which licenses real estate mediation businesses (*requires verification*: this course has no checked Portugal comparison for this topic).

**Florida rule.** A complaint follows a fixed path split across bodies: DBPR investigates, a FREC panel decides probable cause, the FREC issues the final order, and a District Court of Appeal reviews it if a notice is filed within 30 days.

**Precise difference.** Florida separates investigating, screening, deciding and reviewing, in a fixed order. How a complaint against a Portuguese mediator is investigated, decided and appealed, and with what deadlines, *requires verification*, so no step-by-step comparison is offered here.

**Exam implication.** Answer procedure questions with the Florida sequence and body names only. Never use a Portuguese rule or deadline to answer a Florida question.

## 7. Legal and numerical requirements

**Memorize exactly**

- Complaint → investigation → probable cause → formal complaint → hearing → final order → appeal.
- The **DBPR** receives complaints and **investigates**.
- Probable cause: **majority vote** of a panel of **at least two members**, including **at least one current FREC member**; a **former** member may serve (s. 455.225(4); Rule 61J2-20.009; verified, V-42).
- Informal hearing = facts **not disputed**, before the **FREC**. Formal hearing = facts **disputed**, before an **ALJ**, who issues a **recommended order** (manual; not yet verified).
- The **FREC** issues the **final order** and chooses the penalty within s. 475.25(1) (L06-2).
- Appeal: notice within **30 days after rendition**, to a **District Court of Appeal**; filed with the agency clerk, copy and fees to the court clerk (s. 120.68; Fla. R. App. P. 9.110(c); verified, V-43).

**Understand**

- The pipeline as a whole is manual-based and **partially verified**: the panel (V-42) and the appeal (V-43) are verified; the order of the other steps comes from the manual.
- Legal sufficiency, complaints from people who were not harmed, anonymous complaints, notice to the licensee, the DBPR filing the formal complaint, the FREC issuing the final order, and "withdrawal does not stop the case" (manual; not yet verified).
- A missed 30-day notice is an irremediable jurisdictional defect (verified, V-43).
- Panel size: the verified rule is "at least two members, including a present FREC member" (V-42); the current rule's wording on size was seen only in fragments. The manual's "two members, at least one a current FREC member" fits it and is the exam answer.

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"Probable cause comes first, then the investigation."** The DBPR investigates first; the panel decides probable cause on the results.
- **"The formal complaint starts the case."** An ordinary complaint starts it; the formal complaint comes after probable cause.
- **"If the client withdraws the complaint, the case ends."** It continues; the case belongs to the state.
- **"Only a harmed client can complain."** Anyone may file a legally sufficient complaint; even an anonymous complaint can be investigated if it is legally sufficient and substantial.
- **"The FREC investigates."** The DBPR investigates; the FREC decides.
- **"The ALJ decides the penalty."** The ALJ recommends; the FREC issues the final order.
- **"Informal hearing means disputed facts."** Reversed: disputed facts go to an ALJ.
- **"Appeals go to the Florida Supreme Court or a county court, and a late notice can be excused."** A District Court of Appeal, and a late notice is fatal to the appeal.

## 10. Memory sheet

- **Complaint → Investigation → Probable cause → Formal complaint → Hearing → Final order → Appeal.**
- Traps: **I before P** (investigation before probable cause); **P before F** (probable cause before the formal complaint).
- **DBPR digs, panel screens, FREC decides, court reviews.**
- Panel: at least **2** members, at least **1 current** FREC member, **majority vote**.
- Facts agreed → **informal** (FREC). Facts disputed → **formal** (ALJ → recommended order → FREC).
- Appeal: **30 days after rendition** → District Court of Appeal. Late = no appeal.
- Withdrawn complaint ≠ closed case.

## 2. Why it matters

When a case reaches the FREC's final order (L06-1), the FREC chooses from a fixed menu of penalties with exact limits. The exam tests those limits (**$5,000 per count**, **10 years** of suspension), the line between **administrative** penalties (FREC) and **crimes** (courts), and the labels for misconduct: fraud, misrepresentation, concealment, false promise, culpable negligence, breach of trust.

This is part of Section 6 of the exam outline (3%, shared with L06-1 and L06-3). In practice, one bad habit can become several counts, each with its own fine.

## 3. Simple explanation

### Three separate tracks

| Track | Who decides | Possible result |
|---|---|---|
| Administrative (the license) | FREC, after the L06-1 process | Fine, probation, suspension, revocation and others; fines go to the state |
| Criminal | Criminal court | Jail and criminal fines |
| Civil | Civil court | Damages paid to the harmed person (and possibly the Recovery Fund, L06-3) |

The FREC never imprisons anyone and never awards damages to consumers. The tracks can run together: breaking the rules on selling rental information to prospective tenants is a crime **and** also exposes the license to discipline (s. 475.453; V-21).

### The administrative penalty menu

Under **s. 475.25(1), F.S.** (verified, V-41) the FREC may deny an application or renewal, issue a reprimand, fine up to **$5,000 for each count or separate offense**, place a licensee on probation, suspend a license for up to **10 years**, or revoke it.

| Penalty | Meaning | Limit or note |
|---|---|---|
| Notice of noncompliance | Written notice for a first minor violation, letting the licensee correct it | Manual; not yet verified |
| Citation | A set fine for a listed minor violation, instead of formal proceedings | Manual; not yet verified |
| Reprimand | Formal written disapproval | — |
| Administrative fine | A money penalty; the manual says it is paid to the state | Up to **$5,000 per count** |
| Probation | Continued work under FREC conditions | — |
| Required education | Courses the FREC orders the licensee to complete | Manual; not yet verified |
| Suspension | The license exists but cannot be used | Up to **10 years** |
| Revocation | The license is cancelled | Permanent, with narrow exceptions (manual; not yet verified) |
| Denial | Application or renewal refused | — |

A **count** is one separate violation charged. Three counts mean fines of up to 3 × $5,000 = **$15,000**.

### Criminal penalties (courts only)

| Conduct | Classification | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Operating as a broker or sales associate without a **valid, current, active** license | **Third-degree felony**: up to 5 years and $5,000 under the general sentencing statutes | s. 475.42(1)(a) (V-12) |
| Any other violation of s. 475.42(1) | **Second-degree misdemeanor**, unless Chapter 475 sets a different punishment | s. 475.42(2) (V-12) |
| Violating the rules on selling rental information lists to prospective tenants (for example, not making the required refunds) | **First-degree misdemeanor**, plus license discipline | s. 475.453 (V-21) |

A **felony** is the more serious class of crime; a **misdemeanor** the less serious. Note "valid, current, **active**": the felony is not limited to people who never held a license.

### The violation vocabulary

The FREC may discipline a licensee who commits these **in any business transaction**, not only in real estate (s. 475.25; manual; not yet verified). A licensee who cheats partners in a restaurant venture answers to the FREC too.

| Term | Meaning | Florida example |
|---|---|---|
| Fraud | Intentional deception | Inventing a "second offer" to push a Tampa buyer to pay more |
| Misrepresentation | False statement of a material fact | "The roof was replaced last year," when it is 15 years old |
| Concealment | Hiding a material fact | A rug placed over a rotten floor section before showings |
| False promise | A promise to induce action that the licensee does not intend to keep or cannot keep | "Sign today and the seller will pay all your closing costs," when the seller already refused |
| False pretenses | Pretending a false situation exists to obtain something | Charging an owner a "marketing fee" by pretending a buyer is lined up |
| Dishonest dealing by trick or scheme | A planned arrangement to cheat | Two contracts for one sale, one with a false price for the lender |
| Culpable negligence | Reckless indifference to others' rights: more than carelessness; no intent to deceive is needed | Again and again leaving deposit checks in the car for days because "nothing has gone wrong yet" |
| Breach of trust | Betraying the confidence a principal placed in the licensee | Telling a buyer the seller's lowest price while acting as the seller's single agent |

**Puffing** ("the best sunset in Naples") is opinion, not misrepresentation. A one-time careless slip is **simple** negligence, not culpable negligence.

### Other named grounds

- **Commingling** (mixing personal or business funds with escrow funds). A broker may lawfully keep up to **$1,000** of personal or brokerage funds in a sales escrow account and **$5,000** in a property management escrow account (V-38).
- **Conversion** (using escrow funds for personal or unauthorized purposes).
- **Failure to account and deliver** money or property to the person entitled to it.
- Operating as a **broker** while licensed as a **sales associate**.
- Conviction of a crime involving **moral turpitude** (serious dishonesty or immorality).
- **Adjudication of mental incompetence** (manual wording: confinement in a mental institution).
- **Records** and **crime reporting**, below.

**Records (s. 475.5015; verified, V-40).** A broker keeps at least one legible copy of all books, accounts and records for **at least 5 years**, counted from receipt of entrusted funds or, if none, from the date any party signs the listing, offer, rental management agreement, lease or other agreement engaging the broker. If the records are involved in litigation: at least **2 years after** the case and any appeal end, and **never less than 5 years** in total.

**Reporting a crime (s. 455.227(1)(t); verified, V-35).** Within **30 days**, in writing, to the FREC: any conviction or finding of guilt, or plea of guilty or **nolo contendere** ("no contest"), to **any crime** in any jurisdiction, **regardless of adjudication** (even if the court "withholds adjudication", that is, does not formally enter a conviction). Chapter 475's own rule (s. 475.25(1)(p)) covers only felonies.

## 4. Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Count | One separate violation charged; fines apply per count |
| Reprimand | Formal written disapproval on the licensee's record |
| Felony / misdemeanor | More serious / less serious class of crime |
| Material fact | A fact that would affect a reasonable party's decision or the property's value |
| Puffing | Exaggerated sales opinion; not a statement of fact |
| Moral turpitude | Serious dishonesty or immorality |
| Nolo contendere | "No contest" plea: no admission of guilt, but the penalty is accepted |
| Withheld adjudication | The court does not formally enter a conviction; the crime must still be reported |

## 5. Practical example

**Bruno**, a sales associate in Fort Myers, faces a formal complaint with three counts:

1. On a Friday he received a $10,000 deposit check and kept it in his car until the next Thursday. He had to deliver it to his broker by the end of the next business day: Monday, if not a legal holiday (Rule 61J2-14.009; V-36).
2. He told the buyer, "The seller replaced the roof last year," knowing it is 15 years old: misrepresentation.
3. Two months ago he pleaded nolo contendere to a misdemeanor, adjudication withheld, and never told the FREC. Chapter 475's felony-only rule would not reach it; s. 455.227(1)(t) does.

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Maximum total fine? | 3 counts × $5,000 = **$15,000** |
| Other FREC options? | Reprimand, probation, suspension up to 10 years, revocation |
| Can the FREC order him to pay the buyer for the roof? | No: the buyer must sue in civil court |
| Can the FREC jail him? | No: only a criminal court |

Bruno's unlicensed cousin shows homes for $150 per showing: operating without a license, a **third-degree felony** for a criminal court.

## 6. Portugal comparison

**Familiar principle.** A regulator supervises the sector. In Portugal this role is usually associated with IMPIC, I.P., which licenses real estate mediation businesses (*requires verification*: this course has no checked Portugal comparison for this topic).

**Florida rule.** Each licensee holds a personal license, and the FREC disciplines that personal license. Crimes go to the courts; unlicensed practice is a third-degree felony.

**Precise difference.** Whether the Portuguese license is held by the mediation business rather than by each consultant, how consultants inside an agency operate, and the types, amounts and procedure of Portuguese sanctions all *require verification*. In Florida the sales associate personally can be fined, and his or her own license suspended or revoked, for conduct in any business transaction.

**Exam implication.** Use only Florida's limits: $5,000 per count, 10 years, revocation by the FREC; jail only by a court. Never let a Portuguese rule answer a Florida question.

## 7. Legal and numerical requirements

**Memorize exactly**

- FREC (s. 475.25(1); V-41): denial, reprimand, fine up to **$5,000 per count or separate offense**, probation, suspension up to **10 years**, revocation.
- No valid, current, active license: **third-degree felony**, up to 5 years and $5,000 (s. 475.42(1)(a); V-12).
- Other s. 475.42(1) violations: **second-degree misdemeanor**, unless the chapter sets a different punishment (s. 475.42(2); V-12).
- Rental information violations: **first-degree misdemeanor** plus license discipline (s. 475.453; V-21).
- Records: **5 years**; if litigated, **2 years** after the case and appeals end, never less than 5 in total (s. 475.5015; V-40).
- Crimes: written report within **30 days**; any crime; guilty or nolo contendere pleas included; regardless of adjudication (s. 455.227(1)(t); V-35).

**Understand**

- **Outdated figure:** the fine cap was **$1,000 per count** in the 2002 statute; it was $5,000 by 2010 and is $5,000 in the 2024 and 2025 editions. The 2026 edition's penalty text was not retrieved, but the excerpts checked showed no change (V-41).
- Manual-only (not yet verified): notice of noncompliance and how citations work; ordered education; exceptions to permanent revocation; that fines go to the state; first-degree misdemeanor penalties (up to $1,000 and 1 year); DBPR citations of up to $5,000 and injunctions against unlicensed persons; the violation vocabulary and the "any business transaction" reach; the commingling, conversion, failure to account and deliver, broker-while-associate, moral turpitude and mental incompetence grounds.
- Rule 61J2-14.012 also requires the broker to keep deposit slips, depository statements and agreements, and to reconcile escrow accounts monthly (V-40).

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"The FREC can order restitution or jail."** Damages need a civil judgment; jail needs a criminal court.
- **"The maximum fine is $5,000 per case."** It is $5,000 **per count**.
- **"The fine cap is $1,000."** That was the 2002 cap. ($1,000 is also the personal-funds limit in a sales escrow account, a different rule.)
- **"Suspension and revocation are the same."** Suspension is temporary (up to 10 years); revocation cancels the license.
- **"Culpable negligence is any mistake."** It is reckless indifference, more than carelessness.
- **"Only real estate deals count."** The vocabulary applies in any business transaction.
- **"Only felony convictions must be reported."** Any crime, guilty and nolo contendere pleas included, regardless of adjudication.
- **"Records are kept 3 years."** At least 5; if litigation is involved, also at least 2 years after it and any appeal end.
- **"Unlicensed practice is a misdemeanor."** It is a third-degree felony.

## 10. Memory sheet

- **Fine 5K per count · suspend 10 · revoke = cancel · records 5 · report crimes in 30.**
- FREC: deny, reprimand, fine, probation, suspend, revoke. Never jail, never damages.
- No active license = **3rd-degree felony**. Other s. 475.42(1) = 2nd-degree misdemeanor. Rental lists = 1st-degree misdemeanor + license action.
- Fraud = intent · misrepresentation = false fact · concealment = hidden fact · false promise = promise to induce · culpable negligence = reckless · breach of trust = betrayed confidence.
- Any business transaction. Old $1,000 cap = outdated.

## 2. Why it matters

The FREC can fine and suspend a licensee, but it cannot give a harmed consumer any money (L06-2). A consumer can win a court judgment against a licensee and still collect nothing if the licensee has no assets or has disappeared. The **Real Estate Recovery Fund** is the state's last-resort answer to that problem.

The exam asks four things: the **two caps** ($50,000 and $150,000), **what the Fund does not pay**, **who may not claim**, and **what happens to the licensee** when the Fund pays. It is part of Section 6 of the exam outline (3%, shared with L06-1 and L06-2). In practice, a Fund payment caused by your conduct suspends your license until you pay the money back with interest.

## 3. Simple explanation

### From the foundation up

1. A licensee harms a consumer in a brokerage transaction (for example, by misrepresentation).
2. The FREC may discipline the licensee, but it **does not award damages**.
3. The consumer sues in civil court and wins a **judgment** (a court order that the licensee must pay).
4. The licensee cannot or does not pay: the judgment is **unsatisfied**.
5. The consumer applies to the **Recovery Fund**, which pays within strict limits (s. 475.484, F.S.; verified, V-17).
6. The licensee's license is **automatically suspended** until the Fund is repaid in full plus interest (see the note in section 7).

### The conditions

The Fund pays a member of the public who:

- obtained a **final civil judgment** against a **licensee**;
- for a **violation arising from a real estate brokerage transaction**;
- and **cannot collect** it.

### What the Fund pays

| Rule | Detail |
|---|---|
| Type of loss | **Actual or compensatory damages** only |
| Not paid | **Treble damages** (triple damages), **court costs**, **attorney's fees**, **interest** |
| Per claim | The lesser of the unsatisfied judgment (actual or compensatory damages) or **$50,000** |
| Per transaction | **$50,000 in total**, however many people claim |
| Per licensee | **$150,000 in total** across all transactions; if claims exceed it, payments are **prorated** (shared out) |

### Who may and may not recover

| Person | May claim? |
|---|---|
| Buyer, seller or other member of the public harmed by a licensee in a brokerage transaction, with a final judgment they cannot collect | **Yes** |
| A licensee harmed while acting **solely as a buyer or seller** (a principal, not an agent) | **Yes**, like any consumer |
| A licensee who acted **as a licensee** in the transaction, for example a commission dispute | **No** |
| A sales associate who sues her broker over an unpaid commission | **No**: she acted as a licensee, and commission disputes are excluded |
| A licensee's spouse, in certain claims | **No** (manual; details not given) |

The logic: the Fund protects the **public** that deals with licensees. Disputes among licensees about their own pay are business disputes between professionals.

### The licensee's consequence

When the Fund pays because of a licensee's conduct, that license is **automatically suspended** until the licensee repays the Fund **in full plus interest**. According to the manual, bankruptcy does not restore the license.

### A second use: protecting a broker who obeys an EDO

When a broker holding a disputed deposit obtains an **Escrow Disbursement Order (EDO)** from the FREC, follows it, and is later sued for obeying it, the Fund also pays the broker's **defense costs** and the judgment (manual; not yet verified).

## 4. Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Real Estate Recovery Fund | State fund that pays consumers who cannot collect a judgment against a licensee arising from a brokerage transaction |
| Judgment | A court's decision ordering one party to pay another |
| Unsatisfied judgment | A judgment that has not been paid |
| Actual (compensatory) damages | Money that makes up for the real loss suffered |
| Treble damages | Three times the actual damages, awarded under some laws as a penalty |
| Attorney's fees / court costs | The cost of the lawyer and of the lawsuit itself |
| Aggregate cap | A maximum for all claims added together |
| Prorate | Share a limited amount out among several claims |
| Principal | A party to the deal (buyer or seller), as opposed to an agent |
| Escrow Disbursement Order (EDO) | FREC order deciding who receives disputed escrow funds held by a broker |

## 5. Practical example

**Lucas**, a sales associate in Clearwater, tells **Mrs. Duarte** that a condo "has never had water damage," knowing the unit flooded twice. She buys it, pays for repairs, and sues him. The court awards her a judgment:

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Compensatory damages (repairs and losses) | $41,000 |
| Attorney's fees | $9,000 |
| Court costs | $1,500 |
| Interest | $2,000 |
| **Total judgment** | **$53,500** |

Lucas has no assets, so the judgment is unsatisfied. Mrs. Duarte applies to the Fund.

- **Fund pays:** the lesser of $41,000 (compensatory damages) or $50,000 = **$41,000**.
- **Fund does not pay:** the $12,500 of fees, costs and interest ($9,000 + $1,500 + $2,000).
- **Lucas:** his license is automatically suspended until he repays the Fund $41,000 plus interest.

**Two other claims:**

- **Ana**, a sales associate at Lucas's brokerage, wins a judgment against their broker for unpaid commissions and cannot collect it. She **cannot** claim: she acted as a licensee, and this is a commission dispute.
- **Paulo**, a licensed sales associate, bought a different unit through Lucas for himself, acting only as a buyer, and was misled the same way. After winning his own judgment that Lucas cannot pay, he **can** claim like any consumer. His claim is a separate transaction, with its own $50,000 cap, and counts toward Lucas's $150,000 total.

## 6. Portugal comparison

**Familiar principle.** Both systems want money to be available when a licensed intermediary harms a client. In Portugal, a mediation license is commonly understood to require **civil liability insurance (or an equivalent guarantee)** (*requires verification*: this course has no checked Portugal comparison for this topic).

**Florida rule.** The exam's protection is a **state fund**. It pays only after a **final civil judgment** that cannot be collected, only **actual or compensatory damages**, within **$50,000 per transaction** and **$150,000 per licensee**, and the licensee must repay it (automatic suspension until repaid with interest).

**Precise difference.** The Florida Fund is a last resort that requires a court judgment first, and it is a debt the licensee must repay. Whether and how a Portuguese client claims against a mediator's insurance or guarantee, and any limits on it, *requires verification*.

**Exam implication.** For any Florida question about an uncollectable judgment, apply the Recovery Fund rules. Do not answer "the agent's insurance pays", and never let a Portuguese rule answer a Florida question.

## 7. Legal and numerical requirements

**Memorize exactly**

- **$50,000 per transaction**, all claimants combined (s. 475.484; verified, V-17).
- **$150,000 aggregate per licensee**; prorated when exceeded (V-17).
- Pays the lesser of the unsatisfied judgment for actual or compensatory damages or $50,000 (V-17).
- Never pays **treble damages, court costs, attorney's fees or interest** (V-17).
- Fund payment → license **automatically suspended** until repaid **in full plus interest** (V-17: seen in the 2010 statute text, not re-confirmed in the 2026 excerpt; see below).
- A licensee who acted **as a licensee** (for example, a commission dispute) cannot recover (manual; not yet verified; the standard exam answer).

**Understand**

- The caps and the excluded items are confirmed in s. 475.484 (V-17). The automatic suspension appears in the 2010 statute text; the 2026 excerpt checked did not show that sentence, so its current wording is not re-confirmed. It remains the standard exam answer.
- The judgment must arise from a violation in a real estate brokerage transaction, and the claimant must be unable to collect it.
- Who is excluded (licensees acting as licensees, a licensee's spouse in certain claims), the right of a licensee acting solely as a buyer or seller to claim, and the bankruptcy point (manual; not yet verified).
- The Fund's use for a broker sued after obeying an EDO (manual; not yet verified).
- The manual says the Fund never pays "punitive damages or interest". The statute's wording, as checked, is actual or compensatory damages only, with treble damages, court costs, attorney's fees and interest excluded (V-17).

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"Sales associates can claim for unpaid commissions."** No. A licensee who acted as a licensee is excluded.
- **"The Fund pays legal fees."** No attorney's fees, court costs, interest or treble damages.
- **"Each claimant can get $50,000."** $50,000 is the total for the transaction.
- **"$150,000 per transaction."** $150,000 is the total per licensee; per transaction it is $50,000.
- **"The FREC orders the Fund to pay after its hearing."** The claimant first needs a civil court judgment that cannot be collected.
- **"Once the Fund pays, the licensee is off the hook."** The license is automatically suspended until the Fund is repaid in full plus interest.
- **"The Fund payment revokes the license."** It suspends it until repayment.
- **"Licensees can never claim."** A licensee harmed while acting solely as a buyer or seller may claim.
- **"The cap is $25,000."** $25,000 is not a Recovery Fund cap. Use $50,000 per transaction and $150,000 per licensee (V-17).

## 10. Memory sheet

- **50 per deal, 150 per licensee.** Public only.
- Judgment first → cannot collect → Fund.
- Pays actual or compensatory damages only. **No** treble damages, fees, costs or interest.
- Licensee acting as licensee (commission dispute) = **no claim**. Licensee acting solely as buyer or seller = may claim.
- **Payment = automatic suspension** until repaid in full plus interest.
- EDO followed and broker sued → Fund pays defense costs and judgment (manual).
