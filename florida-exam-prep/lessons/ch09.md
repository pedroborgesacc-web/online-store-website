## 2. Why it matters

Every listing starts with two questions: **who owns this property, and how did they get it?** The answer changes the paperwork. A seller who bought the home signs a deed. An heir may need probate first. A neighbor who claims a strip of land after years of use is a different problem again. If you recognize how title arrived, you spot trouble early and know when to send the client to an attorney.

For the exam, this lesson opens Section 9 of the course outline, "Title, Deeds and Ownership Restrictions", about **7%** of the Sales Associate exam (roughly 7 of the 100 questions). Expect vocabulary and classification: voluntary or involuntary, devise or descent, escheat or eminent domain. The key number is Florida's adverse possession period: **7 years**, plus color of title or payment of taxes. The favorite trap is **20 years**, the prescriptive easement period.

## 3. Simple explanation

### Title and transfer

**Title** means ownership and the evidence of it. A **transfer** moves title from one person to another. In L08-1 you met the **disposition** right: the owner's right to sell, give away or leave property by will. Some transfers use that right. In others, the law, a creditor or the government moves title without the owner's choice.

### Voluntary and involuntary transfer

| Method | Type | Who decides | Who receives title |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Deed** (sale or gift) | Voluntary | The owner, during life | The buyer or the person receiving the gift |
| **Will** | Voluntary | The owner, in a will that operates at death | The **devisees** |
| **Descent** (intestate succession) | Involuntary | Florida's statute of descent | The **heirs** |
| **Escheat** | Involuntary | The law: no will and no heirs | The state |
| **Eminent domain** | Involuntary | The government, for public use, with just compensation | The government |
| **Foreclosure** | Involuntary | A creditor (usually the mortgage lender) enforces the debt after the owner defaults | The buyer at the foreclosure sale |
| **Adverse possession** | Involuntary | The statute, once its conditions are met | The possessor |

(Classification and the definitions of devise, descent, escheat and eminent domain: manual; not yet verified. The description of foreclosure is a general explanation, not taken from this course's sources.)

**Why is a will voluntary but descent involuntary?** In both cases the owner has died. With a will, the owner chose the recipients while alive. With descent, the owner made no valid choice, so a statute chooses. **Eminent domain** (the process is called **condemnation**) is involuntary even though the owner is paid: just compensation is not consent.

### Deeds: title moves on delivery

A **deed** is the written document by which the **grantor** (the person transferring) conveys title to the **grantee** (the person receiving). Title passes by **delivery and acceptance of the deed during the grantor's lifetime**: not when the grantor signs, and not when the deed is recorded (manual; not yet verified). L09-2 covers valid deeds; L09-3 covers recording.

### Wills, descent and escheat

When an owner dies, ask first: is there a valid will?

- **Testate** = with a valid will. Real property passes by **devise**; the recipient is the **devisee**.
- **Intestate** = without a valid will. **Florida's statute of descent** distributes the property to the **heirs**: this is **descent**.
- **No will and no heirs:** the property passes to the state by **escheat**.
- **Probate** is the court process that proves the will and clears title.
- If the property was the owner's **homestead** and a spouse or minor child survives, Florida's **homestead devise restrictions override the will**.
- A **will takes effect only at death** and can be **revoked at any time before**. A deed takes effect during life, on delivery and acceptance.

(All points in this subsection: manual; not yet verified.)

### Adverse possession in Florida (verified: V-68)

**Adverse possession** lets someone who is not the owner acquire title by possessing land for a long time. The law expects owners to watch over their land and, after many years, prefers a settled situation to an old, neglected claim. Because the owner loses the land without payment, the conditions are strict.

**The period is 7 years of continuous possession.** Exam books describe the possession as **open, notorious, hostile and continuous**: visible rather than hidden, claimed as the possessor's own rather than used with the owner's permission, and without breaks. (These labels are the course's standard description. The statutes use other words: s. 95.16 speaks of "continued possession", and s. 95.18 of "actual continued possession" under a claim of title "exclusive of any other right". Labels: manual; not yet verified.)

Florida then requires **one of two routes**:

| | **With color of title** (s. 95.16) | **Without color of title** (s. 95.18) |
|---|---|---|
| Basis of the claim | A written instrument (a conveyance), decree or judgment that appears to give title | No written instrument: a claim of title exclusive of any other right |
| Recording | For possession beginning after December 31, 1945, the instrument is **recorded** with the clerk of the circuit court in the county where the land lies | — |
| Taxes | — | Pay **all outstanding taxes** and matured installments of special improvement liens **within 1 year** after entering |
| Filing | — | File a **return with the county property appraiser within 30 days** after that payment, on the Department of Revenue (DOR) form, with a legal description and a notarized attestation |
| During the 7 years | Continued possession of the property included in the instrument | Keep paying all taxes and liens |
| What counts as possessed | Land usually cultivated or improved, or protected by a substantial enclosure | Only land actually possessed (enclosed, cultivated, maintained or improved) |

A dash means V-68 lists no such step for that route. Exam short version: **7 years + color of title or payment of taxes.**

Earlier notes in this course marked this rule "manual; not verified". The October 2026 check of ss. 95.16 and 95.18 confirmed it (V-68, confirmed, single-checked).

**Not to be confused with a prescriptive easement:** 20 years of continuous, open, adverse use without the owner's consent can create a right to *use* someone else's land, not ownership. That rule comes from Florida case law, not a statute (V-69).

## 5. Practical example

You work for a brokerage in Lakeland (Polk County). Three estate and government files reach your desk:

| File | Transfer type | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| Mr. Albuquerque died with a will leaving his rental house to his niece, Clara | Testate → **devise**; Clara is the devisee | Probate proves the will and clears title before Clara sells. Had it been his homestead, with a surviving spouse or minor child, the homestead restrictions would override the will |
| Mrs. Grant died without a will; two sons survive her | Intestate → **descent** | The statute, not the family's wishes, decides who takes |
| The county widens a road and takes a 20-foot strip from a client's lot | **Eminent domain** | The client receives just compensation; the transfer does not depend on his consent |

**The fenced lot.** A buyer wants a vacant lot whose owner of record lives out of state. The neighbor, Rui, has fenced and farmed the whole lot and says it is his "by adverse possession". Test the claim against V-68:

| Question | Rui's facts | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Color of title? | No deed, decree or judgment | Route **without** color of title (s. 95.18) |
| Possession began | May 1, 2019 | 7 years end May 1, 2026 at the earliest |
| Taxes within 1 year? | Paid all outstanding taxes April 10, 2020 | Yes (limit: May 1, 2020) |
| Return within 30 days after paying? | Filed with the property appraiser April 28, 2020 | Yes (18 days) |
| Kept paying? | Every year | Yes |
| Open, notorious, hostile, continuous? | Fenced, farmed, never asked permission, no gaps | Yes |
| How much land? | All of it is fenced and farmed | Only land actually possessed counts: here, all of it |

By October 2026 Rui's 7 years have run, and he appears to meet the statutory conditions. Whether and how his claim becomes clean record title is a question for an attorney. Your job is to spot the issue before anyone treats the out-of-state owner's title as clear.

**Variations:** if Rui never paid the taxes, he fails the s. 95.18 route however long he farms. If he farmed under a written agreement with the owner, his possession was with permission, so not hostile. If he fenced and farmed only half the lot, only that half is held adversely.

## 6. Portugal comparison

**Familiar principle.** Transfer by sale, gift, will and inheritance, and the state's power to take land for public works, will feel familiar (Portuguese side: requires verification). Portuguese law also knows acquisition by long possession (*usucapião*), but its periods and conditions are not in this course's comparison entries (requires verification).

**Florida rule.** Voluntary: deed or will. Involuntary: descent, escheat, eminent domain, foreclosure, adverse possession. Adverse possession: 7 years, either with a recorded color-of-title instrument, or with taxes paid within 1 year, a return filed within 30 days after paying, and taxes kept paid for the rest of the period (V-68).

**Precise difference.** This course has no Portugal comparison entry for this lesson, so any difference in substance requires verification. The exam tests only the Florida period, conditions and vocabulary (devise, descent, heirs, escheat, testate, intestate).

**Exam implication.** Never answer with a Portuguese period or inheritance rule. If a choice offers 20 years, that is Florida's prescriptive easement period (V-69).

## 7. Legal and numerical requirements

**Memorize exactly**

- Voluntary: **deed** (sale or gift), **will**. Involuntary: **descent, escheat, eminent domain, foreclosure, adverse possession** (manual; not yet verified).
- Adverse possession: **7 years** of continuous possession **plus color of title or payment of taxes** (V-68, confirmed, single-checked).
- With color of title (s. 95.16): written instrument, decree or judgment; for possession beginning after **December 31, 1945**, recorded with the clerk of the circuit court in the county (V-68).
- Without color of title (s. 95.18): taxes **within 1 year** of entering; return to the property appraiser **within 30 days** after paying; keep paying for the rest of the 7 years; only land actually possessed (V-68).
- Possession described as open, notorious, hostile, continuous (manual; not yet verified as statutory wording).
- **20 years** = prescriptive easement, case law (V-69), not adverse possession.
- Testate → **devise** → devisee. Intestate → **descent** → heirs. No will and no heirs → **escheat** (manual; not yet verified).
- Title passes on **delivery and acceptance during the grantor's lifetime**, not on signing or recording (manual; not yet verified).
- A will operates **only at death** and is revocable until then; homestead devise restrictions (spouse or minor child) override it (manual; not yet verified).

**Understand**

- Probate exists so a buyer can trust that an heir or devisee really holds title.
- Payment in eminent domain does not make it voluntary.
- Adverse possession is strict because the owner loses land without payment.

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"Adverse possession takes 20 years in Florida."** It takes **7**. Twenty years is the prescriptive easement period, and an easement is only a right to use (V-69).
- **"Seven years of living on the land is enough."** Florida also requires color of title (a recorded instrument) or the tax payment, the return and continued payments (V-68).
- **"Paying the taxes on someone's land makes it mine."** Taxes are one condition, not a substitute for 7 years of possession; only land actually possessed counts (V-68).
- **"Using the land with the owner's permission still counts."** Permission means the possession is not hostile (manual; not yet verified).
- **"Descent means passing property under a will."** Descent is the **no-will** route; a will passes real property by **devise** (manual; not yet verified).
- **"Bequest and devise mean the same thing."** Real property left by will is a **devise**. "Bequest" usually means a gift of personal property by will (general vocabulary, not in this course's sources; not yet verified).
- **"Escheat and eminent domain are the same."** Escheat: owner dies with no will and no heirs. Eminent domain: a taking for public use with just compensation (manual; not yet verified).
- **"Signing a will or recording a deed transfers title."** A will operates only at death; a deed transfers title on delivery and acceptance during the grantor's lifetime (manual; not yet verified).

## 10. Memory sheet

- **Voluntary:** deed (sale or gift), will. **Involuntary:** descent, escheat, eminent domain, foreclosure, adverse possession.
- **7 years + color of title or taxes** (V-68). Color of title = recorded instrument, decree or judgment. No color = taxes within 1 year, return within 30 days, keep paying.
- **Open, notorious, hostile, continuous** (exam labels: manual; not yet verified as statutory wording). Only land actually possessed counts (V-68).
- **20 years = prescriptive easement** (V-69), not adverse possession.
- **Devise** = will. **Descent** = no will. **Escheat** = no will and no heirs → state.
- Probate proves the will and clears title. Homestead restrictions override the will.
- Will: effective at death, revocable before. Deed: title passes on delivery and acceptance during life.
- Items without a V-number: manual; not yet verified.

## 2. Why it matters

The deed is the document that actually moves ownership at a Florida closing. Two practical questions come up in almost every sale: **what kind of deed will the buyer receive**, and **is the deed valid**? The deed type tells your buyer how much the seller promises about the title. A missing witness, or a deed that was never delivered, can stop a closing or leave a buyer without title. Two Florida details deserve special attention because they are easy to get wrong: the **grantor signs before two witnesses**, and the **grantee (the buyer) does not sign the deed at all**.

This is a high-priority lesson in Section 9 of the course outline (about 7% of the exam). Expect "Which deed gives the most protection?" (general warranty), "Which deed cures a cloud on title?" (quitclaim) and "How many witnesses?" (two). Expect also the classic trap "Which is essential to a valid deed?", where the grantee's signature, recording and notarization are the wrong answers.

## 3. Simple explanation

### What a deed does

A **deed** is a written instrument by which the **grantor** (the person transferring) conveys title to the **grantee** (the person receiving). Besides transferring title, a deed may contain **warranties**, also called **covenants**: promises by the grantor about the quality of the title. The more the grantor promises, and the longer the period the promises cover, the more protection the buyer has. That is the whole logic of the ranking below.

### Four deeds, from most to least protection

| Rank | Deed | What the grantor warrants | Period covered |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **General warranty** | Title against defects arising **at any time**, even before the grantor owned the property. Gives the five covenants below | All time |
| 2 | **Special warranty** | Only defects arising **during the grantor's own ownership** | The grantor's ownership |
| 3 | **Bargain and sale** | **Implies** that the grantor owns the property, but gives **no warranties** | None |
| 4 | **Quitclaim** | Nothing. It conveys **whatever interest the grantor has**, if any | None |

(manual; not yet verified)

**Why a quitclaim is useful although it promises nothing.** A **cloud on title** is a claim or defect that impairs title, such as an ex-spouse's stray interest or a misspelled heir. The person who might hold that interest signs a quitclaim deed, handing over whatever he or she may have, and the cloud is removed. The other side of the coin: a quitclaim from someone who owns nothing conveys nothing.

### The five covenants of a general warranty deed

| Covenant | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| **Seisin** | The grantor owns the property and has the right to convey it |
| **Quiet enjoyment** | The grantee will not be disturbed by someone with a better claim to the title |
| **Against encumbrances** | There are no encumbrances (claims such as liens or easements) other than those stated in the deed |
| **Further assurance** | The grantor will sign or do whatever else is later needed to perfect the grantee's title |
| **Warranty forever** | The grantor will defend the title against lawful claims, with no time limit |

(The list of covenants and the meaning of seisin are from the manual and glossary; the other plain-language meanings are general explanations, not found in this course's sources. Not yet verified.)

### What a valid Florida deed requires

Validity answers one question: **does this document transfer title from the grantor to the grantee?**

| # | Requirement | Why it exists |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | A **written** instrument | Proof of exactly what was transferred |
| 2 | A **competent grantor**: of legal age (18 or older) and mentally competent | The grantor is the one giving up rights |
| 3 | An **identifiable grantee**, who may be a minor | The grantee only receives, so competence is not required |
| 4 | **Consideration**; a nominal amount (such as ten dollars) is enough | Shows that something of value was given; the deed need not show the real price |
| 5 | **Words of conveyance** (the granting clause) | Shows the intent to transfer |
| 6 | A **legal description** | Identifies the land exactly; a street address is never a legal description |
| 7 | The **grantor's signature** in the presence of **two subscribing witnesses** (s. 689.01) | Florida's statutory formality for conveyances (V-05) |
| 8 | **Delivery and acceptance** during the grantor's lifetime | The moment title passes |

Rows 1–6 and 8: manual; not yet verified. Row 7: verified (V-05). The legal description systems are covered in a later lesson.

**The witnesses, in detail (V-05).** A **subscribing witness** watches the grantor sign and then signs the deed as a witness. Section 689.01 requires **two** of them for conveyances of freehold estates (ownership estates, L08-1) and of terms over one year. Witnesses may also sign electronically through audio-video technology (s. 117.201). The same section now adds a proviso that **no subscribing witnesses are required for a lease** of real property, so the general wording about terms over one year no longer makes a lease need witnesses. Older books say a lease over one year needs two witnesses: that rule is **outdated** (the lease proviso appears in the 2024 text and is absent from the 2010–2019 texts). A lease over one year must still be **in writing** under the statute of frauds. For the exam, follow V-05: **deeds, two witnesses; leases, none.**

### What is NOT required for validity

- **The grantee's signature.** Only the grantor signs.
- **Recording.** Recording protects the grantee against the **world** (L09-3). Delivery makes the deed valid between the **parties**.
- **A notary's acknowledgment.** An **acknowledgment** is a formal declaration before a notary that the signer signed voluntarily. It is required to **record** the deed, not to make it valid.

(manual; not yet verified)

Because every buyer wants the deed recorded, a real closing will include the acknowledgment as well. On the exam, keep the two purposes apart: **delivery makes it valid; recording warns the world.**

## 5. Practical example

Beatriz, who recently moved from Porto to Orlando, is buying a townhouse in Kissimmee (Osceola County) from Daniel Price, who bought it in 2019. The title work shows that Daniel's ex-wife, Karen, may still hold an interest from their 2021 divorce.

| Question | Answer | Rule applied |
|---|---|---|
| Beatriz wants the deed that protects her most. Which one? | A **general warranty deed**: Daniel would warrant against defects from any time, even before 2019 | Ranking |
| The contract allows a special warranty deed instead. What does Daniel promise? | Only that no defect arose **while he owned it** (2019 to the sale). A defect created in 2015 is outside his promise; Beatriz would rely on title insurance (L09-3) | Special warranty = grantor's ownership only |
| How is Karen's possible interest removed? | Karen signs a **quitclaim deed** releasing whatever interest she may have | Quitclaim cures a cloud |
| At closing, the second witness leaves early and Daniel signs before one witness. Is that acceptable? | No. The deed must be signed before **two** subscribing witnesses (s. 689.01, V-05) | Witness requirement |
| Must Beatriz sign the deed? | No. The grantee does not sign | Grantee's signature not required |
| The deed says "ten dollars and other consideration", not the real price. Is that a problem? | No. Nominal consideration is enough | Consideration |
| When does Beatriz become the owner? | When the properly signed deed is **delivered** to her and she **accepts** it | Delivery and acceptance |
| The deed is recorded three days later. Did recording make it valid? | No. It was already valid. Recording gives constructive notice to the world (L09-3) | Recording is not a validity requirement |

**A different file: the deed in the drawer.** In 2022 Ms. Ortiz signed a deed of her Kissimmee condo to her son, but she kept it in her desk. It was found only after her death in 2025. The deed was never delivered during her lifetime, so it did not transfer title. The condo passes under her will or by descent (L09-1), not under the deed.

## 6. Portugal comparison

**Familiar principle.** In Portugal a sale is formalized in a public deed or an authenticated private document, and Portuguese practice does not use graded "warranty deed" types (Portuguese side: requires verification; PT-12).

**Florida rule.** Florida deeds are ranked by the warranties the grantor gives: general warranty (all time), special warranty (the grantor's ownership period), bargain and sale (implied ownership, no warranties) and quitclaim (no warranties). A valid deed needs the grantor's signature before two witnesses (V-05) and delivery and acceptance.

**Precise difference.** In Florida, the **choice of deed type** decides how much the seller promises about the title, and **two witnesses** are a validity requirement for a deed. Do not assume that a notary's involvement is what makes the transfer valid: in Florida the acknowledgment is needed to **record** the deed, not for validity (manual; not yet verified). Whether Portuguese formalities give the buyer any comparable promises about title requires verification.

**Exam implication.** The grantee does not sign; notary acknowledgment is for recording. To clear an ex-spouse's possible interest, the ex-spouse signs a quitclaim deed. Answer every deed question from Florida rules only.

## 7. Legal and numerical requirements

**Memorize exactly**

- Ranking: **general warranty > special warranty > bargain and sale > quitclaim** (manual; not yet verified).
- General warranty covenants: **seisin, quiet enjoyment, against encumbrances, further assurance, warranty forever** (manual; not yet verified).
- Valid deed: written instrument; competent grantor (18 or older, mentally competent); identifiable grantee (may be a minor); consideration (nominal is enough); words of conveyance; legal description; delivery and acceptance during the grantor's lifetime (manual; not yet verified).
- Grantor signs before **2 subscribing witnesses** (s. 689.01; V-05, corrected). Witnesses may sign electronically through audio-video technology (s. 117.201).
- **Leases: no witnesses required** (V-05). "Two witnesses for leases over one year" is outdated. A lease over one year must still be in writing (statute of frauds).
- **Not required for validity:** the grantee's signature, recording, acknowledgment. Acknowledgment is required to **record** (manual; not yet verified).

**Understand**

- Validity is about the **parties**; recording is about the **world**.
- A special warranty deed leaves defects from before the grantor's ownership outside the grantor's promise.
- A quitclaim conveys only what the grantor has, which may be nothing.
- The grantor must be competent because the grantor gives up rights; the grantee only receives.

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"The buyer signs the deed at closing."** Only the grantor signs (manual; not yet verified).
- **"A deed is not valid until it is recorded."** Delivery and acceptance make it valid; recording protects against the world (manual; not yet verified).
- **"The notary makes the deed valid."** The acknowledgment is needed to record, not for validity (manual; not yet verified).
- **"One witness is enough."** A Florida deed needs **two** subscribing witnesses (s. 689.01, V-05).
- **"A lease over one year needs two witnesses."** Outdated. Leases need no witnesses now, but a lease over one year must still be in writing (V-05).
- **"A special warranty deed covers every past defect."** It covers only defects arising during the grantor's ownership.
- **"A bargain and sale deed gives warranties because it implies ownership."** It implies ownership but gives no warranties.
- **"A quitclaim deed proves the grantor owns something."** It promises nothing and conveys whatever interest the grantor has, if any.
- **"A signed deed kept in a drawer transfers title."** Title passes only on delivery and acceptance during the grantor's lifetime (manual; not yet verified).
- **"A minor cannot receive a deed."** The grantee may be a minor; it is the grantor who must be competent (manual; not yet verified).
- **"The deed must state the full price."** Nominal consideration is enough (manual; not yet verified).

(Corrections without a V-number: manual; not yet verified.)

## 10. Memory sheet

- **General > special > bargain & sale > quitclaim.**
- General = all time + 5 covenants (seisin, quiet enjoyment, against encumbrances, further assurance, warranty forever). Special = grantor's ownership only. Bargain and sale = implied ownership, no warranties. Quitclaim = no warranties, cures clouds.
- Valid deed: writing · competent grantor (minor grantee OK) · consideration (nominal) · granting clause · legal description · **2 witnesses** (V-05) · **delivery and acceptance**.
- Not required: grantee's signature · recording · acknowledgment (needed to record).
- Leases: no witnesses (V-05); "2 witnesses for leases over 1 year" is outdated.
- **Delivery makes it valid; recording warns the world.**

## 2. Why it matters

Buyers ask two questions you should be able to answer in plain words: "How do I know the seller really owns this?" and "What protects me if an old problem with the title appears after I buy?" In Florida the answers are the **public records** and **title insurance**. Recording also explains why a buyer is bound by an easement recorded decades ago, even if nobody mentioned it during the sale.

For the exam (Section 9 of the course outline, about 7% of the exam), expect questions on constructive versus actual notice, on whether recording is needed for a deed to be valid (it is not), on an abstract with an attorney's opinion versus title insurance, on the owner's versus the lender's policy, and on what title insurance does **not** cover: problems created after the policy date, and physical damage.

## 3. Simple explanation

### Why recording exists

From L09-2: a properly signed and witnessed deed is valid between grantor and grantee once it is delivered and accepted; recording is not required. But nobody else can see a deed kept in a drawer. **Recording** places the document in the **county public records**, where anyone can search it. Recording does not make a deed valid. It **warns the world**.

### Two kinds of notice

- **Constructive notice** is notice the law presumes from the **public records** and from **visible possession** of the property, whether or not the person actually looked. Once a document is recorded, everyone is treated as knowing it.
- **Actual notice** is what a person actually knows, because she was told, read a document or saw something herself.

Why does the law presume knowledge? The records are open to everyone, so a buyer who skips the search cannot rely on her own failure to look. **Visible possession** works the same way: if someone other than the seller is living on the property, a buyer is expected to find out on what basis, for example a tenant with a lease. (manual; not yet verified)

### Who wins when two people claim the same property

Florida's recording system protects a **good-faith purchaser who records without notice of prior claims** (manual; not yet verified). Follow the logic:

1. A seller signs and delivers a deed to Buyer A, who does **not** record it.
2. The seller dishonestly signs a second deed to Buyer B. B searches the records and finds nothing, has no other knowledge of A's deed, and records.
3. B is a good-faith purchaser without notice who recorded, so the rule protects B. A's deed was valid between A and the seller, but A carried the risk of not recording.

The protection depends on **no notice**. If B had actual notice of A's deed, B would not be protected. If A had already moved into the house, A's visible possession would give B constructive notice. The practical lesson: **record promptly**.

### Title evidence: proving marketable title

**Marketable title** is title a reasonable buyer would accept, free of serious defects and clouds (general definition, not from this course's sources; not yet verified). A Florida buyer evidences it in one of two ways:

| | Abstract of title + attorney's opinion | Title insurance |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | An **abstract** is a summary of all recorded documents affecting the title. An attorney examines it and gives an **opinion** on the title | A policy that protects against losses from title defects existing **before the policy date** |
| What it rests on | What the records show | The records, plus the insurer's promise covering defects that existed before the policy date but were **undiscovered** |
| Status | The traditional method | The **modern standard** |

(manual; not yet verified)

An abstract and an opinion can only describe what the records show. Title insurance adds protection against a hidden past defect, such as a forged deed in the property's history.

### Owner's policy and lender's policy

| | Owner's policy | Lender's policy |
|---|---|---|
| Protects | The owner (the buyer) | The lender |
| Amount | The **purchase price** | The **loan amount** |
| Over time | Protects the owner **as long as she owns** the property | **Declines** as the loan balance is paid down |
| Transfer | **Not transferable** to the next buyer | **Follows the loan** |

(manual; not yet verified)

### Title insurance looks backward

Title insurance covers defects existing **before** the policy date, even undiscovered ones. It **never** covers defects the owner creates later, such as a lien she allows to attach after closing. It does not cover later events such as a zoning change after closing, and it is not property insurance, so it does not pay for physical damage from a hurricane or flood. Why? The title work examines the property's history up to the policy date. Whatever arises after that date, whether the owner's own debts or outside changes, is outside what the policy insures. (manual; not yet verified)

**Related federal rule (V-53, confirmed).** Under Section 9 of RESPA (the federal Real Estate Settlement Procedures Act), which applies to federally related mortgage loans on 1–4 family residential property, a seller may not require the buyer to use a particular title insurer. A seller who does is liable for 3 times the title charges. RESPA is covered in a later chapter.

## 2. Why it matters

**In practice.** Almost every Florida property carries something that limits the owner's title: a mortgage, a strip along the back of the lot for power lines, subdivision rules recorded on the plat, sometimes a neighbour's fence over the line. You will see these in the title evidence and on the survey. You must tell the buyer which ones will be paid off and which ones stay with the land, and spot problems early enough to fix them.

**On the exam.** This lesson belongs to Section 9, "Title, Deeds and Ownership Restrictions", about 7% of the state exam, roughly 7 of the 100 questions (V-01, V-02). Favourite questions: which lien is paid first from a foreclosure sale, specific versus general liens, what kind of easement a power line is, how long a prescriptive easement takes (20 years, not 7), and what to call a fence over the boundary.

## 3. Simple explanation

**Source status.** The prescriptive easement rule is verified (V-69, confirmed), and so are the cross-references to adverse possession (V-68) and homestead (V-66). The other rules in this lesson come from the study manual and are marked *(manual; not yet verified)*.

### The foundation: title and encumbrances

**Title** means ownership. An **encumbrance** is any claim or limitation on title held by someone else. The owner still owns the property, but *subject to* that claim. There are two families:

| Family | What the other person has | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Money claims | A right to be paid, with the property as security | Liens: mortgage, property tax lien, judgment lien |
| Use and physical limitations | A right to use the land, a limit on its use, or an object on it | Easements, deed restrictions, encroachments |

### Liens: money claims against property

A **lien** is a claim against property as security for a debt. If the property is sold at foreclosure (a forced sale to pay a debt), the lienholders are paid from the sale money in a fixed order called **priority**.

**Specific or general?** It depends on how much of the debtor's property the lien reaches *(manual; not yet verified)*:

| | Specific lien | General lien |
|---|---|---|
| Attaches to | One property | All the debtor's property |
| Examples | Property tax lien · special assessment lien · mortgage · vendor's lien · construction (mechanic's) lien | Judgment lien · IRS (federal tax) lien · estate lien |

A **special assessment** is a charge on the properties that benefit from a particular local public improvement (a new sidewalk or sewer line). A **vendor's lien** is the seller's lien for unpaid purchase price. An **estate lien** is a claim connected with a deceased person's estate. An **IRS lien** is a lien for unpaid federal taxes (the IRS is the US federal tax agency). A **judgment lien** comes from a court judgment (for example, a lost lawsuit) and reaches all the debtor's property in the county where the judgment is recorded *(course question bank; not yet verified)*. Florida's homestead protection (V-66) limits what an ordinary judgment creditor can reach: see section 7.

**Priority: who is paid first** *(manual; not yet verified)*:

1. **Property tax and special assessment liens** come first, regardless of date ("superpriority"), even ahead of a first mortgage recorded 20 years earlier.
2. **Every other lien** ranks by recording date: *first in time, first in right*.
3. **Construction liens relate back** to the **notice of commencement** (a public-record document marking the start of a construction project): the lien ranks from the date the notice was recorded, not from the later date the lien claim was recorded.
4. **Subordination**: a lienholder can agree to a lower priority, for example so that a new construction loan can take first position.

**Why recording order?** Recording gives *constructive notice* to the world: the law treats everyone as knowing what is recorded. A lender who lends after another lien is recorded could have found it, so it accepts the later place. Relation back follows the same logic: once the notice of commencement is recorded, every later lender is warned that construction liens may follow.

### Easements: rights to use someone else's land

An **easement** is a right to use another person's land for a particular purpose (a driveway, utility lines). It is an interest in land. Classify it with two questions.

**Who benefits?** *(manual; not yet verified)*

- **Appurtenant**: benefits an adjoining parcel. The benefited parcel is the **dominant estate**; the burdened one is the **servient estate**. It **runs with the land**: it transfers automatically when either parcel is sold, because it belongs to the parcel, not to a person.
- **In gross**: benefits a person or company, such as a utility. There is **no dominant estate**.

**How did it arise?**

- **By necessity**: created by law for a **landlocked** parcel (no access to a road) *(manual; not yet verified)*.
- **By prescription** (V-69, confirmed, single-checked): **20 years** of actual, continuous and uninterrupted use of a limited, defined area; under a claim of right that conflicts with the owner's use, **without the owner's consent** (that is what "adverse" means); with the owner's actual knowledge, or use so open, notorious and visible that knowledge is imputed (the law treats the owner as knowing).

The 20 years come from **case law, not a statute**; s. 95.14, which the manual tentatively cited, is not the source. Florida courts disfavour prescription and resolve doubts in the owner's favour. When Florida cut the adverse possession period to 7 years, the prescriptive period stayed at 20 (V-69).

**Adverse possession is different** (V-68): 7 years of continuous possession gives **title** (ownership). With color of title, the possession is under a recorded written instrument; without color of title, the possessor pays the taxes within 1 year of entering, files a return with the property appraiser within 30 days after paying, and keeps paying the taxes. Prescription takes 20 years and gives only a **right to use**.

### License: permission, not an interest in land

A **license** is revocable personal permission to use land, such as letting a neighbour launch a kayak from your lawn. It is **not an interest in land** *(manual; not yet verified)*. Since prescription requires use *without* consent (V-69), use under a license never counts toward the 20 years. False friend: this has nothing to do with your real estate license.

### Encroachments and deed restrictions

An **encroachment** is a physical intrusion: a structure or improvement extending onto another's land (fence, shed, driveway, roof overhang). The public records do not show a physical object, so a **survey** (a professional measurement of the boundaries and what stands on them) reveals it *(manual; not yet verified)*.

A **deed restriction** (**restrictive covenant**) is a private limitation on use, recorded in a deed or on a **plat** (the recorded subdivision map), that **binds future owners** *(manual; not yet verified)*. Examples: single-family use only; no boats parked in front yards. It is created by an owner or developer, not by a government.

The protection does not apply against property taxes and assessments, debts for purchase, improvement or repair, or labour performed on the property: for example the tax lien, a mortgage that financed the purchase, or a construction lien for work on the home.

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"The oldest recorded lien is paid first."** Property tax and special assessment liens come first regardless of date; recording order governs only the rest.
- **"A first mortgage always stays first."** Taxes beat it, its lender can subordinate, and a construction lien ranks ahead of it if the notice of commencement was recorded before the mortgage.
- **"A mortgage is a general lien."** No: one property, so specific. A judgment lien is general.
- **"Prescription takes 7 years."** No: 7 years is adverse possession of title (V-68); a prescriptive easement takes 20 (V-69), set by case law, not s. 95.14.
- **"A power-line easement is appurtenant."** No dominant estate, so it is in gross.
- **"Years of permitted use become an easement."** Permission makes it a license; prescription requires use without consent.
- **"An encroachment is a kind of easement."** An easement is a right; an encroachment is a physical intrusion found by a survey.
- **"Deed restrictions are government rules."** They are private, recorded in a deed or plat.

## 10. Memory sheet

- Encumbrance = claim or limit on title: liens (money); easements, restrictions, encroachments (use).
- Specific = one property (taxes, assessments, mortgage, vendor's, construction). General = everything (judgment, IRS, estate).
- Taxes and assessments first → recording order → subordination steps a lien down → construction liens date from the notice of commencement.
- Appurtenant = parcel to parcel, runs with the land. In gross = person or company. Necessity = landlocked.
- Prescription = 20 years, open, continuous, adverse, no consent; case law (V-69). Adverse possession = 7 (V-68).
- License = revocable, no interest. Encroachment = over the line, survey. Deed restriction = private, binds future owners.

## 2. Why it matters

**In practice.** "Does the chandelier stay?" is a classic fight between buyer and seller. The answer depends on whether the item is a **fixture** (part of the real property, sold with the house) or **personal property** (the seller's to take). In commercial leasing you will meet business tenants' equipment bolted to the floor. And Florida has rivers, large lakes and two coastlines, so waterfront buyers ask what they own and what happens when water or a hurricane moves the shoreline.

**On the exam.** Section 9, "Title, Deeds and Ownership Restrictions", carries about 7% of the exam (V-02). Expect a fact pattern where you apply the fixture tests (intent wins), the trade-fixture trap (equipment bolted down by a business tenant), riparian versus littoral, and accretion versus avulsion after a storm.

## 3. Simple explanation

**Source status.** No verification entry covers this lesson. Every rule below comes from the study manual *(manual; not yet verified)*. It is standard exam vocabulary, but it has not been checked against an official Florida source in this course.

### The foundation: real property or personal property?

**Real property** is the land and what is permanently attached to it; **personal property** is movable. The deed conveys the real property. A **fixture** is personal property that becomes real property when it is attached: a ceiling fan in a box at the hardware store is personal property; once the owner wires it into the ceiling, it is part of the house. Because a fixture is real property, it goes to the buyer with the land unless the sale contract excludes it (see the chandelier in section 5).

### The four fixture tests (intent wins)

When it is unclear whether an item became a fixture, courts apply four tests *(manual; not yet verified)*:

| Test | Question | Points toward a fixture | Points away |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Intent** (dominant) | Did the person who attached it mean it to stay permanently? | Owner installs a kitchen island to improve the home | Seller states in writing that the antique chandelier is excluded |
| **Method of annexation** | How is it attached? | Bolted, wired in, plumbed, cemented | Resting on the floor, plugged into a socket |
| **Adaptation to the realty** | Was it made or fitted for this property? | Hurricane shutters custom-made for these windows, on permanent tracks | A standard item that fits anywhere |
| **Relationship of the parties** | Who installed it, in what role? | Owner improving their own home | Tenant installing equipment for its business |

**Why intent dominates.** The other three tests are really *evidence* of intent. Bolts, custom fitting and the installer's role are clues; intent is the conclusion. In practice, the clearest evidence of intent is what the parties write: list in the contract what stays and what goes.

### Trade fixtures: the business tenant's exception

**Trade fixtures** are items a business tenant installs for its trade (restaurant ovens, salon chairs, store shelving). They **remain the tenant's personal property**, even if bolted down, and the tenant **must remove them by the end of the lease**. If the tenant leaves them behind, they become the landlord's by **accession** (ownership gained because something was added to, or left on, your property) *(manual; not yet verified)*.

Why? Relationship and intent: a business tenant installs equipment to run its business, not to give it to the landlord. That is why the trade-fixture rule beats the method of attachment.

### Emblements: crops as personal property

**Emblements** are annual crops produced by labour, such as a tenant farmer's tomatoes. They are **personal property**, and a tenant farmer may harvest crops planted before the tenancy ended *(manual; not yet verified)*.

### Water rights: which water, which word

*(manual; not yet verified)*

| Term | Type of water | Florida example |
|---|---|---|
| **Riparian rights** | Rivers and streams | A home on the St. Johns River |
| **Littoral rights** | Oceans, seas and large lakes | A condo on the Atlantic; a home on Lake Okeechobee |

Memory aid: **ri**parian = **ri**ver; *littoral* sounds like "litoral", the coast.

On the coast, **the State of Florida owns the land seaward of the mean high-water line** (the average high-tide line). The beach seaward of that line is state land, not part of the private lot.

### When the water moves the land

| Event | Speed | What happens | Boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Accretion** | Gradual | Water slowly deposits soil or sand; the owner gains the new land | Moves |
| **Erosion** | Gradual | Water slowly washes land away; the owner loses it | Moves |
| **Avulsion** | Sudden | A hurricane rips the shoreline or a river suddenly changes channel | **Does not move** |

One way to understand it: gradual change cannot be seen from day to day, so the boundary follows the water. A sudden change leaves a clear "before", so the old boundary can still be identified and stays where it was.

## 5. Practical example

**The house.** Rui, a sales associate in Jacksonville, lists Linda's home on the St. Johns River. Before writing the listing, he goes through the items with her:

| Item | Tests | Likely result |
|---|---|---|
| Ceiling fans wired into the ceilings | Method (wired in); owner installed them to improve the home | Fixture: stays |
| Hurricane shutters custom-made for the windows, on permanent tracks | Adaptation; method | Fixture: stays |
| Free-standing refrigerator, plugged in | Not attached | Personal property: goes with Linda unless the contract includes it |
| Grandmother's chandelier in the dining room | Attached, so it looks like a fixture; Linda intends to keep it | Make the intent clear: exclude it in writing in the contract, or replace it before showings |
| TV on a bolted wall bracket | Mixed signals | Uncertain under the tests: write it into the contract |

**The shop.** Linda also owns a small retail building leased to a hair salon. The salon bolted styling chairs to the floor and plumbed in shampoo sinks. The lease ends next month and Linda is selling the building. These are **trade fixtures**: they belong to the salon, not to Linda and not to the building buyer. The salon must remove them by the end of the lease; whatever it leaves behind becomes the landlord's by accession.

**The river.** Over 15 years, sand has slowly built up along Linda's riverbank: **accretion**, so the new strip is hers. A neighbour's bank has slowly washed away: **erosion**, so the neighbour lost land. Rui explains that if a hurricane suddenly cut a new channel across a lot, that would be **avulsion**: the boundary would stay where it was. Linda's rights here are **riparian** (a river).

**The beach.** The buyer is also looking at an oceanfront condo at Jacksonville Beach. Those are **littoral** rights (the Atlantic), and the beach seaward of the mean high-water line belongs to the State of Florida.

**Memorize exactly**

- In a sale, avoid fixture disputes by listing in the contract what stays and what goes.
- On the exam keep the textbook distinction: river = riparian, ocean = littoral. Outside the exam you may hear "riparian" used loosely for any waterfront (not verified in this course).

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"If it is bolted down, it is a fixture."** Not for a business tenant's trade fixtures, and intent, not the method of attachment, is the dominant test.
- **"Trade fixtures pass to the building buyer with the deed."** They remain the tenant's personal property.
- **"The tenant can come back for the equipment whenever it likes."** It must remove trade fixtures by the end of the lease; what it leaves becomes the landlord's by accession.
- **"Growing crops are always part of the land."** Emblements (annual crops produced by labour) are personal property, and a tenant farmer may harvest crops planted before the tenancy ended.
- **"An oceanfront owner has riparian rights."** On the exam, ocean, sea or large lake = littoral; river or stream = riparian.
- **"After a hurricane moves the shoreline, the boundary follows the new water line."** A sudden change is avulsion: the boundary does not move.
- **"Erosion and avulsion are the same thing."** Both lose land, but erosion is gradual (boundary moves) and avulsion is sudden (boundary stays).
- **"A beachfront owner owns down to the water."** Florida owns the land seaward of the mean high-water line.
