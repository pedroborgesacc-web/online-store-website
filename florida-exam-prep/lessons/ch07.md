## 2. Why it matters

Fair housing law shapes ordinary moments of the job: which homes you show a buyer, how you word an ad, how you answer "what kind of people live here?", and what you do when an owner gives you a discriminatory instruction. Breaking it exposes you and your broker to a HUD complaint or a lawsuit.

On the exam, Section VII (*Federal and State Laws Pertaining to Real Estate*) is **3%**, about 3 questions shared by the four lessons of this chapter. Fair housing questions come in three shapes:

- **vocabulary:** steering, blockbusting or redlining?
- **dates:** which protected class was added in which year?
- **exemptions:** does one apply, and was it lost?

## 3. Simple explanation

### Two federal laws with different reach

1. **Civil Rights Act of 1866.** Bans discrimination based on **race** in the sale or rental of **all property**, real or personal. It has **no exceptions** and is enforced by a lawsuit in federal court.
2. **Fair Housing Act of 1968** (Title VIII). Covers housing, protects more classes, and has a few narrow **exemptions**.

Why this matters: a Fair Housing Act exemption never protects **racial** discrimination, because the 1866 Act still applies. That is why every exam answer offering a "racial exemption" is wrong.

### The protected classes, in order

- **1968:** race, color, religion, national origin.
- **1974:** **sex** (added by the Housing and Community Development Act).
- **1988:** **familial status** and **handicap** (added by the Fair Housing Amendments Act, enacted September 13, 1988). HUD's rules now say **disability** instead of handicap.

**Familial status** covers households with children under 18, pregnant women, and people in the process of obtaining custody of a child. Age and marital status are **not** on the federal fair housing list. They are credit (ECOA) categories, covered in L07-2.

### What is prohibited

Because of a protected class, no one may refuse to sell or rent, offer different terms, publish a discriminatory ad, or falsely say a home is unavailable. Three practices have names:

- **Steering:** directing buyers or renters **toward or away from** areas because of a protected class. It targets **buyers**.
- **Blockbusting** (panic peddling): persuading owners to sell by suggesting that a protected class is moving into the neighborhood. It targets **sellers**.
- **Redlining:** a **lender** refusing loans, or changing loan terms, in certain areas for discriminatory reasons. It targets **loans**.

### Disability: modification vs accommodation

- **Reasonable modification:** a **physical change** to the unit, such as grab bars or a ramp, generally **at the tenant's expense**.
- **Reasonable accommodation:** a change to a **rule or policy**, such as allowing an assistance animal despite a no-pets rule.

### Exemptions, and how they are lost

| Exemption | Conditions |
|---|---|
| Private owner of single-family houses | Owns **no more than 3** such houses at one time. **No broker, agent or salesperson** is used, and no one "in the business" of selling or renting. **No discriminatory advertising.** An owner who was not living in the house, or was not its most recent occupant, gets the exemption for only **one sale in any 24 months**. |
| "Mrs. Murphy" (owner-occupied) | A building with living quarters for **no more than 4 families** living independently, and the **owner lives in one** of the units. |
| Religious organizations | May limit noncommercial housing they own or operate to members of the same religion, or give them preference, **unless** membership is restricted by race, color or national origin. |
| Private clubs | A club not in fact open to the public may limit lodgings it provides as an incidental, noncommercial part of its purpose to its members. |
| Housing for older persons | May exclude children, so the **familial status** protection does not apply. Two tests: **62+**, where all occupants are 62 or older; or **55+**, where at least **80% of occupied units** have at least one person 55 or older, with published policies and age verification. |

A person is "in the business" if, in the preceding 12 months, they acted as principal in 3 or more dwelling transactions, or as agent in 2 or more.

**The two exemption killers:** using a **broker** and publishing a **discriminatory ad**. For the single-family exemption, both are written into the statute. The manual applies the same rule to the other owner and organization exemptions (Mrs. Murphy, religious organizations, private clubs). **Housing for older persons** is judged differently: by its own tests (62+, or 55+ with 80%, published policies and age verification).

### Enforcement

- A complaint goes to **HUD within 1 year** after the discriminatory practice occurred or ended.
- A civil **lawsuit** must be filed **within 2 years**. The clock stops while a HUD proceeding on the same practice is pending, and a person may sue without filing with HUD first.
- Brokerage offices display the **equal housing opportunity poster**.
- Florida has its own Fair Housing Act (Chapter 760, F.S.), which mirrors the federal law. Its details were not verified for this course.

## 6. Portugal comparison

**Familiar principle.** Portuguese law forbids discrimination in general, through constitutional equality and specific anti-discrimination legislation. The details *require verification*.

**Florida rule.** The exam tests the US federal framework: the 1866 Act (race, no exceptions), the Fair Housing Act's seven classes, three named practices, and narrow exemptions lost when a broker or a discriminatory ad is used.

**Precise difference.** US law works from a **closed list** of classes and **named** practices. General ideas of fairness will not tell you which year sex was added, or whether a fourplex owner is exempt.

**Exam implication.** No exemption ever applies to race. Answer with the federal list and dates, never by analogy with Portuguese law.

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"Sex and familial status were both added in 1988."** Sex was added in **1974**. 1988 added familial status and handicap.
- **"Age and marital status are fair housing classes."** Not under the federal Fair Housing Act. They are ECOA (credit) classes.
- **"An owner who qualifies for an exemption can still use a broker."** Using a broker ends the exemption.
- **"An exempt owner may refuse a buyer because of race."** Never: the 1866 Act has no exceptions.
- **"Showing buyers 'where people like them live' is good service."** It is steering, even if the buyer seems pleased.
- **"Blockbusting is done to buyers."** It targets **owners**, pushing them to sell.
- **"The tenant's grab bars are an accommodation."** A physical change is a **modification**. A rule change, like allowing an assistance animal, is an **accommodation**.
- **"A 55+ community needs a person 55 or older in every unit."** It needs one in at least **80% of occupied units**. The "everyone" rule belongs to **62+** housing, where all occupants are 62 or older.

## 2. Why it matters

These laws reach three parts of your working day:

- **your office**, which must be accessible to the public (ADA);
- **your buyers' loans**, which must be granted without discrimination (ECOA) and are reported to regulators (HMDA);
- **your prospecting** by phone, email and fax (Do Not Call, CAN-SPAM, junk fax rules).

Prospecting by phone and email is routine in Florida brokerage, so these rules affect most licensees every week.

On the exam (Section VII, **3%**, shared with L07-1, L07-3 and L07-4), the questions are of two kinds:

- **matching**, such as "a wheelchair ramp at the office: which law?";
- **numbers**: **18 months / 3 months** for Do Not Call and **10 business days** for email opt-outs.

## 3. Simple explanation

### Four laws, four subjects

The exam describes a situation and asks which law governs. Find the **subject** first.

| Subject in the question | Law |
|---|---|
| **Housing**: selling or renting a dwelling | Fair Housing Act (L07-1) |
| **Credit**: a loan application | **ECOA**, the Equal Credit Opportunity Act |
| **Public places and businesses**: an office, a store | **ADA**, the Americans with Disabilities Act |
| **Lending data**: reports on where and to whom lenders lend | **HMDA**, the Home Mortgage Disclosure Act |

**ADA.** Places of **public accommodation** and commercial facilities, including **brokerage offices**, must be accessible to people with disabilities. Barriers must be removed where this is **readily achievable**. The ADA governs business premises, **not private homes**. Housing is fair housing territory.

**ECOA.** A creditor (lender) may not discriminate against a credit applicant on any of these bases:

1. **race, color, religion, national origin, sex, marital status, or age** (if the applicant is old enough to sign a binding contract);
2. **receipt of public assistance income**;
3. the applicant's **good-faith exercise of consumer credit rights**.

These bases do not stop a lender from judging whether the applicant can repay. Insufficient income, a poor history of paying debts on time and lack of funds for the down payment are not protected bases, so they can be legitimate reasons to deny a loan.

**HMDA.** Lenders **report data** about their mortgage lending. The law is about reporting, not about banning anything. The data reveals patterns such as **redlining** (L07-1).

**CFPB.** The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau administers the TRID disclosure rules (L07-3).

### Marketing rules

**Do Not Call.** A telemarketing call is a call made to sell something; for you, that means prospecting for listings or buyers. You may not make such a call to a number on the **National Do Not Call Registry**, or on Florida's list, unless one of two exceptions applies:

- **written permission** from the consumer; or
- an **established business relationship** (EBR) with your firm.

Two federal rules define the EBR, with almost the same windows:

| Rule | After a purchase or transaction | After an inquiry or application |
|---|---|---|
| FCC rule (47 CFR 64.1200) | **18 months** | **3 months** |
| FTC Telemarketing Sales Rule (16 CFR 310.2(q)), since May 16, 2024 | **540 days** | **90 days** |

Under the FCC rule, the relationship must come from a voluntary two-way communication and must not have been ended by either party. A consumer's request to **your company** not to call ends it for telemarketing, even inside the window.

**CAN-SPAM** (commercial email):

- Honor an opt-out (unsubscribe) within **10 business days**. Once someone opts out, you also may not sell or transfer their address.
- The opt-out link or reply address must keep working for **at least 30 days** after the email is sent.
- Opting out cannot cost a fee or require more than an email address, a reply email, or a visit to a single web page.
- Every commercial email must include a **valid physical postal address**: a current street address, a registered USPS post office box, or a private mailbox registered with a commercial mail receiving agency.
- Header and subject lines must be truthful, and the email must be identified as an advertisement where required.

**Junk Fax Prevention Act.** Do not send unsolicited fax advertisements without an established business relationship.

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"A wheelchair ramp at the office is a fair housing issue."** It is an **ADA** issue. Fair housing covers dwellings.
- **"The ADA requires sellers to make private homes accessible."** The ADA governs public accommodations and commercial facilities, not private homes.
- **"Another brokerage's expired listing is fair game."** You have no relationship with that owner. Do not call a registered number.
- **"An inquiry gives you 18 months."** An inquiry gives **3 months**; 18 months follows a transaction.
- **"Past customers on the registry can be called forever."** Only within 18 months of the transaction, and never after they ask your company to stop.
- **"540 days is a wrong answer."** It is correct under the FTC rule since 2024. Choose by the units the question uses.
- **"ECOA stops a lender from turning down anyone with low income."** Insufficient income, poor payment history and lack of down-payment funds are not protected bases. Receiving public assistance income is.
- **"ECOA and the Fair Housing Act protect the same classes."** ECOA adds marital status, age, public assistance income and the exercise of credit rights. Familial status and handicap are fair housing classes.
- **"HMDA prohibits redlining."** HMDA is a **reporting** law; its data exposes redlining.
- **"CAN-SPAM gives 30 days to honor an opt-out."** **10 business days.** Thirty days is how long the opt-out mechanism must keep working.

## 2. Why it matters

Most Florida buyers finance, and two federal laws, RESPA and TILA, govern the loan from application to closing. In practice you plan closing dates around the Closing Disclosure wait, you write ads that mention financing, and title companies or lenders may offer you "referral fees".

On the exam (Section VII, **3%**, shared with the other lessons of this chapter), expect three themes:

- the **3 / 3 / 3** business-day rules;
- **trigger terms** in advertising;
- the classic trap: **a buyer cannot rescind a purchase mortgage**.

## 3. Simple explanation

### Two laws, one set of forms

- **RESPA**, the Real Estate Settlement Procedures Act, governs **settlement**, the American word for the closing. It covers kickbacks and the buyer's free choice of title insurer.
- **TILA**, the Truth in Lending Act (Regulation Z), governs the **cost of credit**: the APR, advertising and the right of rescission.
- **TRID**, the TILA-RESPA Integrated Disclosure rule, merged the two laws' forms into two documents: the **Loan Estimate** and the **Closing Disclosure**. The CFPB administers it.

### RESPA: which loans?

RESPA applies to **federally related mortgage loans** on **residential property of 1 to 4 units** (1–4 families). Both parts of that phrase matter:

- **1–4 residential units.** A loan on a single-family home or a duplex can be covered. A loan on a 20-unit apartment building or an office building is outside RESPA.
- **Federally related.** This is a defined federal term. Its detailed test is not covered in this course's sources; for the exam, learn the whole phrase "federally related loans on 1–4 unit residential property".

### RESPA Section 8: no kickbacks, no unearned fees

- No one may give or accept a **kickback** (a fee or anything of value) for **referring** settlement-service business (title, inspection, lending).
- No one may accept an **unearned fee**: a charge, or a share of a charge, for which no service was actually performed.
- **Disclosure does not cure it.** A referral kickback is prohibited even if the buyer is told about it in writing.

The idea: the consumer pays for settlement services, so money paid for steering the consumer's business adds cost without adding service.

### RESPA Section 9: the buyer chooses the title insurer

A seller may not require the buyer to buy title insurance from a **particular title company**. A seller who does is liable for **3 times the title insurance charges**.

### TRID: the two disclosures

| Form | Deadline | "Business day" for this deadline |
|---|---|---|
| **Loan Estimate** | Delivered or mailed **no later than the 3rd business day after** the lender receives the application | A day the lender's offices are open for substantially all business |
| **Loan Estimate** (second limit) | Delivered or mailed **no later than the 7th business day before** consummation | Every day except Sundays and federal public holidays |
| **Closing Disclosure** | **Received at least 3 business days before** consummation | Every day except Sundays and federal public holidays (**Saturdays count**) |

**Consummation** is Regulation Z's word for the loan closing.

Further rules on the two disclosures:

- **When the 3-day clock starts.** It starts when the lender has all **six application items** listed in Regulation Z.
- **Denied or withdrawn.** If the lender denies the application, or the consumer withdraws it, within the 3-day window, no Loan Estimate is required.
- **Mailed or e-mailed.** A disclosure not handed over in person is treated as received **3 business days after** it was sent.
- **Waiver.** The consumer may waive the Closing Disclosure waiting period only for a **bona fide personal financial emergency**, and only after receiving the disclosures.
- **Corrected Closing Disclosure.** It starts a **new** 3-business-day wait **only** if (1) the **APR becomes inaccurate**, (2) the **loan product changes**, or (3) a **prepayment penalty is added**.

### TILA: the APR and advertising trigger terms

The **APR** (annual percentage rate) is the yearly cost of credit, including interest and certain fees. Compare loans by APR, not by the note rate.

There are four **trigger terms**: the amount or percentage of the **down payment**; the **number of payments** or **period of repayment**; the amount of **any payment**; the amount of any **finance charge**. An ad that states any one of them must also state the **down payment**, the **terms of repayment** and the **APR**.

These are **not** triggers: "no down payment"; "no closing costs"; stating the APR alone; vague phrases like "low monthly payments" or "easy financing".

### TILA: the right of rescission

When a loan is secured by the consumer's **principal dwelling** (main home), such as a **home-equity loan** or a **refinance with a new lender**, each owner may cancel the loan:

- until **midnight of the 3rd business day** after the **latest** of three events: consummation, delivery of the rescission notice, or delivery of all material disclosures;
- **business days include Saturdays**, but not Sundays or federal public holidays;
- if the notice or material disclosures are never delivered, the right lasts up to **3 years** after consummation (or ends earlier if the property is sold or the consumer's whole interest is transferred).

If the consumer rescinds, the lender's security interest becomes **void** and no finance charge is owed.

**No right of rescission:**

- a **residential mortgage transaction**, meaning a loan to **buy or initially build** the principal dwelling;
- a refinance by the **same** lender, except for new money above the unpaid balance, earned unpaid finance charges and refinancing costs;
- **investment property**, because it is not the borrower's principal dwelling.

The idea: rescission protects owners who pledge a home they **already own** as security. A loan used to buy or build that home is part of acquiring it, so it is excluded.

| Closing Disclosure | Final disclosure, received at least 3 business days before consummation |
| APR | Annual percentage rate: yearly cost of credit including interest and certain fees |
| Trigger term | A specific credit term in an ad that requires full disclosure |
| Right of rescission | Right to cancel certain loans secured by the principal dwelling within 3 business days |

## 5. Practical example

**Lucas and Ana** buy a single-family home in Orlando, to live in, with a federally related mortgage loan, so RESPA applies. Assume no federal holidays and a lender open Monday to Friday.

| Step | What happens | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| Monday | The lender receives all six application items | The Loan Estimate clock starts |
| By Thursday | Loan Estimate delivered or mailed | 3rd business day after application: Tue (1), Wed (2), Thu (3) |
| Negotiation | The seller says: "I'll sign only if you use my cousin's title company" | Section 9 violation; the seller would owe 3 times the title charges |
| Same week | A title company offers their sales associate $100 per buyer referred | Section 8 kickback, even if disclosed |
| Closing set for a Thursday | Closing Disclosure received by **Monday** | 3 business days before consummation: Wed (1), Tue (2), Mon (3) |
| If it is e-mailed instead | Must be sent by the **previous Thursday**: received Fri (1), Sat (2), Mon (3) | Saturdays count; Sunday does not |
| Tuesday | The lender corrects a fee typo; APR, product and prepayment terms unchanged | No new 3-day wait |
| After closing | Ana wants to cancel the purchase loan | **No** rescission right on a purchase loan |

**Two years later**, still living in the home, they take a home-equity loan secured by it from a different lender. They sign on a **Friday** and receive the notice and all disclosures that day. They may rescind until **midnight Tuesday**: Saturday (1), Monday (2), Tuesday (3).

Years later, their listing ad for a sale of the home reads "Only $15,000 down". That is a trigger term, so the ad must also state the repayment terms and the APR.

## 7. Legal and numerical requirements

**Memorize exactly**

- RESPA: **federally related** loans on **1–4 unit** residential property (V-53).
- Section 8: no **kickbacks** or **unearned fees** for settlement-service referrals; disclosure does not cure.
- Section 9: a seller may not require a particular **title insurer**; liability is **3×** the title insurance charges.
- Loan Estimate: delivered or mailed within **3 business days after application** (and at least 7 business days before consummation) (V-50).
- Closing Disclosure: **received at least 3 business days before consummation**. A new wait applies only for an inaccurate **APR**, a changed **loan product**, or an added **prepayment penalty** (V-50).
- Trigger terms: **down payment** (amount or %), **number of payments / repayment period**, **payment amount**, **finance charge**. Then disclose the down payment, repayment terms and **APR** (V-52).
- Rescission: **3 business days** (Saturdays count), principal dwelling, refinance or home equity. **Not** for loans to buy or build the home, or investment property (V-51).

**Understand**

- Different "business day" definitions apply (TRID table in section 3). For the Closing Disclosure and for rescission, Saturdays count; for the 3-day Loan Estimate deadline, only days the lender is open for substantially all its business count.
- Rescission runs from the **latest** of consummation, notice and material disclosures (up to 3 years if they are never given). A same-lender refinance can be rescinded only for the new money.
- No 2025 or 2026 rule changing TRID timing was found. A July 2026 CFPB request for information only asks for comment.
- From the manual only (manual; not yet verified): "compare loans by APR, not note rate"; the examples of settlement services (title, inspection, lending); and the CFPB administering TRID.

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"A buyer can rescind her purchase mortgage within 3 days."** Rescission does not apply to loans to buy or build the home.
- **"Disclosing a referral fee makes it legal under RESPA."** RESPA prohibits the kickback outright.
- **"The Loan Estimate comes 3 days before closing."** The Loan Estimate comes within 3 business days **after application**. The Closing Disclosure must be received **3 business days before closing**.
- **"Saturdays never count."** They count for the Closing Disclosure and for rescission.
- **"Any change to the Closing Disclosure restarts the 3 days."** Only an inaccurate APR, a changed loan product or an added prepayment penalty.
- **"'Low monthly payments' is a trigger term."** Only specific terms trigger. Stating the APR alone does not.
- **"RESPA covers every real estate loan."** Only federally related loans on 1–4 unit residential property.
- **"A seller may insist on her title company if she discloses it."** Section 9 forbids it, with 3× liability.
- **"A refinance of a rental property can be rescinded."** It is not the principal dwelling.

## 10. Memory sheet

- **RESPA** = closing + no kickbacks (§8) + buyer picks the title insurer (§9, 3×). 1–4 units, federally related.
- **TILA** = APR + trigger terms + rescission.
- **3 after applying** (Loan Estimate) · **3 before closing** (Closing Disclosure) · **3 to rescind** a refinance or home-equity loan.
- New Closing Disclosure wait only for **APR, product, prepayment penalty**.
- Triggers: **down, number, payment, finance charge** → disclose down payment, repayment terms, APR.
- Rescind the **refi**, never the **purchase**. Saturdays count.

## 2. Why it matters

Many Florida licensees lease and manage rentals for owners, and almost every sales associate eventually writes a lease. The Florida Residential Landlord and Tenant Act (Chapter 83, F.S.) sets the deadlines a landlord or property manager must meet. Missing one can cost the landlord the right to keep any of the deposit.

On the exam (Section VII, **3%**, shared with L07-1 to L07-3), this lesson is mostly **day counts**: **30** (deposit notice), **15 / 30 / 15** (move-out) and **60 / 30 / 30 / 7** (ending tenancies). Several rules have changed since the study manual was written: the month-to-month notice (15 days in the 2014 statute, 30 days now), e-mail delivery of deposit notices (since July 1, 2025) and a new flood disclosure for leases (since October 1, 2025).

## 3. Simple explanation

### Holding the tenant's money

A **security deposit** protects the landlord against damage or unpaid rent. **Advance rent** is rent paid before the period it covers. In both cases the landlord is holding money that came from the tenant, so the law says how the landlord must hold it and how the tenant must be told.

- **How it is held.** The money is kept in a separate Florida bank account, which may or may not pay interest, or the landlord posts a **surety bond** (a guarantee from a bonding company) instead.
- **Written notice, in the lease or within 30 days of receipt.** The notice gives:
  - the **name and address of the depository** (bank) holding the money, or a statement that a **surety bond** was posted;
  - whether the tenant is **entitled to interest**;
  - the disclosure text required by the statute.
- **How the notice is delivered.** In person or by mail. Since **July 1, 2025** it may also go by **e-mail under s. 83.505**, but only if both parties signed the statutory e-mail addendum.
- **Changes.** If the landlord later changes how or where the money is held, the tenant must be told **within 30 days after the change**.
- **Exception.** This notice rule does **not** apply to a landlord who rents **fewer than five** individual dwelling units.
- **Rent is still due.** A missing notice is **not** a defense to paying rent on time.

### After the tenancy ends: 15 / 30 / 15

The clock starts when the **rental agreement ends**.

1. **No claim.** If the landlord will not claim any of the deposit, it is returned, with interest if required, **within 15 days**.
2. **Claim.** If the landlord intends to keep some or all of it, the landlord sends **written notice within 30 days**, stating the reason. The notice goes by **certified mail** (mail with proof of mailing and delivery) to the tenant's last known mailing address. Since **July 1, 2025**, it may also go **by e-mail**, if both parties signed the s. 83.505 e-mail addendum and gave e-mail addresses.
3. **Objection.** The tenant has **15 days after receiving the notice** to object. Without an objection, the landlord deducts the claim and sends the balance **within 30 days after the date of the notice**.
4. **Missed deadline.** A landlord who misses the 30-day notice **forfeits** the right to claim against the deposit and may not set the damage off against it. The landlord may still **sue for damages** after returning the deposit.

In a dispute over the deposit, the winning party recovers attorney's fees.

The idea behind the deadlines: the tenant should not wait indefinitely for their own money. A landlord who stays silent past day 30 loses the right to take any of it from the deposit.

### Ending a tenancy without a specific term (s. 83.57)

Some tenancies have no end date. They simply renew each week, month, quarter or year. To end one, **written notice** must be given at least this long **before the end of a period**:

| Tenancy | Minimum written notice |
|---|---|
| Year to year | **60 days** |
| Quarter to quarter | **30 days** |
| Month to month | **30 days** |
| Week to week | **7 days** |

**Correction (V-04).** The study manual lists **15 days** for month to month and **45 days** for quarter to quarter. Current law says **30 and 30**. Older books show 15 days for month to month because the statute later changed (the 2014 text had 15 days). No version of the statute with 45 days was found. If a practice question uses 15 days for month to month, it reflects the old law: answer with current law (30 days) unless the question says otherwise.

### Flood disclosure for leases (s. 83.512, since October 1, 2025)

- **When.** Before or at the signing of a residential lease for a term of **1 year or longer**. Shorter leases are not covered.
- **Form.** A **separate document** on the statutory form. It covers:
  - flood damage the landlord **knows of** during the landlord's ownership;
  - **flood insurance claims**, including claims under the NFIP (National Flood Insurance Program);
  - federal **assistance** received for flood damage, such as from FEMA (Federal Emergency Management Agency);
  - notices about flood insurance.
- **Remedy.** If the landlord does not comply, and the tenant suffers a **substantial loss** of personal property from flooding, the tenant may **end the lease**. A substantial loss means repair or replacement costs of **50% or more** of the property's market value. The tenant ends the lease by **written notice** and by giving up possession **within 30 days after the damage**. The landlord refunds rent paid in advance for the period after termination.
- **Origin.** The rule was enacted by CS/CS/SB 948 (ch. 2025-166, Laws of Fla.). A companion House bill, HB 1015, was tabled. Older books will not mention it.

### Other lease disclosures (taught elsewhere)

- **Radon:** the statutory radon notice appears on at least one document signed at or before a rental agreement for any building (L11-4, L19-2).
- **Lead-based paint**, for housing built before 1978: lessors give the disclosure (EPA pamphlet, known lead paint and hazards, available reports), but tenants do **not** get the buyer's 10-day inspection opportunity (L19-2).

## 5. Practical example

**Carla**, a sales associate in Gainesville, manages 8 apartments for one owner. A tenant signs a **12-month lease** running July 1 to June 30 and pays a **$2,000** deposit.

| Moment | What Carla must do | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| At or before signing | Give the flood disclosure form | Lease of 1 year or longer |
| In the lease (or within 30 days of receiving the deposit) | Name the bank holding the deposit and say whether interest is paid | Owner rents 5 or more units |
| Lease ends June 30, no damage | Return the $2,000 by **July 15** | 15 days |
| Lease ends June 30, broken door ($400) | Send notice of the claim by **July 30**, by certified mail (or e-mail if the addendum was signed) | 30 days |
| Notice sent July 10, received July 13 | The tenant may object until **July 28** | 15 days after receipt |
| No objection | Deduct $400 and send **$1,600** by **August 9** | 30 days after the date of the notice |
| Notice sent August 4 instead | Too late: no claim against the deposit. Return the full $2,000; the owner may sue for damages separately. | Forfeiture |

In the same building, a **month-to-month** tenant's period ends September 30. To end that tenancy, the owner gives written notice **at least 30 days** before September 30, for example on August 25. A notice sent 15 days ahead follows outdated law.

A snowbird's **6-month** seasonal lease does **not** require the flood disclosure.

## 6. Portugal comparison

**Familiar principle.** In Portugal, urban leases are governed by the Civil Code and the NRAU (Novo Regime do Arrendamento Urbano). Their written-form and registration requirements *require verification*.

**Florida rule.** A lease of **1 year or less** may be oral. A longer lease must be in writing (statute of frauds), but leases no longer need witnesses (s. 689.01, V-05). Florida sets exact day counts: **60 / 30 / 30 / 7** to end tenancies without a specific term, **30 days** for the deposit notice, and **15 / 30 / 15** at move-out.

**Precise difference.** The exam tests these specific Florida day counts. Portuguese notice periods and deposit customs must not be carried over.

**Exam implication.** A month-to-month tenant in Orlando gets written notice at least **30 days** before the end of a monthly period. Use the current s. 83.57 periods.

## 7. Legal and numerical requirements

**Memorize exactly**

- Deposit notice: **in the lease or within 30 days** of receipt: the depository's name and address (or a surety bond) and whether interest is paid. **Not required** for landlords who rent **fewer than 5 units**. A change in how it is held: notice within **30 days** (V-62).
- Move-out: **15 days** to return (no claim) · **30 days** to send the claim notice (certified mail, or e-mail with a signed s. 83.505 addendum since **July 1, 2025**) · tenant objects within **15 days** of receipt · balance within **30 days** of the notice date (V-63).
- A missed 30-day notice **forfeits** the claim against the deposit. A separate lawsuit for damages remains possible.
- Termination notices (s. 83.57): **60 / 30 / 30 / 7** (year / quarter / month / week) (V-04).
- Flood disclosure (s. 83.512, since **October 1, 2025**): residential lease of **1 year or longer**, **at or before signing**, separate statutory form (V-07).

**Understand**

- **Outdated figures:**
  - 15 days (month to month) and 45 days (quarter to quarter) are **outdated or incorrect**.
  - "Certified mail only" is outdated since July 1, 2025 (ch. 2025-16, Laws of Fla.).
  - The old wording, notice of "the manner in which the deposit is held", has been replaced by the current disclosure, but the answer is still **30 days**.
- Flood remedy: a substantial loss (**50% or more** of market value) lets the tenant terminate within **30 days** after the damage, with a refund of prepaid rent.
- Leases over 1 year must be in writing (statute of frauds), and leases need no witnesses (V-05).
- From the manual only (manual; not yet verified): the separate Florida account, interest-bearing or not, as a way to hold the deposit; attorney's fees for the prevailing party in a deposit dispute.

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"Month to month needs 15 days' notice."** That is the old law. It is **30 days**.
- **"Quarter to quarter needs 45 days."** It is **30 days**. No version with 45 days was found.
- **"A late claim notice still lets the landlord deduct, because the damage is real."** A missed 30-day notice forfeits the claim against the deposit. The landlord must sue separately.
- **"The landlord has 30 days to return the deposit in every case."** With no claim, it is **15 days**.
- **"The tenant has 15 days from move-out to object."** The 15 days run from **receiving the notice**.
- **"Only certified mail is valid."** Since July 1, 2025, e-mail also works if both parties signed the e-mail addendum.
- **"Every landlord must send the deposit notice."** Landlords who rent fewer than 5 units are exempt.
- **"No deposit notice means the tenant can stop paying rent."** It is not a defense to paying rent.
- **"The flood disclosure applies only in flood zones, or to every lease."** It applies to residential leases of **1 year or longer**, with no flood-zone condition.

## 10. Memory sheet

- Deposit notice: **in the lease or 30 days** · bank or surety bond · interest yes/no · exempt if **<5 units**.
- Move-out: **15 return · 30 claim · 15 object** · balance within 30 days of the notice. Late notice = **forfeit**.
- Notice delivery: certified mail **or** e-mail with a signed addendum (since 7/1/2025).
- End a tenancy: **60 / 30 / 30 / 7** (year / quarter / month / week). 15 and 45 are outdated.
- Lease of **1 year or more** → flood form **at or before signing** (s. 83.512, since 10/1/2025).
