## 2. Why it matters

Every exam question is answered by one specific source of law, and many questions are worded straight from Florida's statutes. If you know *which* source governs a situation and *which body* acts, you can eliminate wrong answers even when you do not remember a detail. This is also the first place where the Portuguese and Florida systems differ in structure, not just in detail.

This is a bridge lesson: the textbook assumes you already know how American law is layered. Everything in Chapters 2–7 builds on it.

## 3. Simple explanation

**Two levels of government make law in the United States.**

- **Federal** law applies in every state. In real estate it covers national issues: fair housing, lending disclosures (RESPA, TILA), lead-based paint, disability access (ADA), environmental cleanup (CERCLA) and income tax.
- **State** law applies inside one state. **Licensing real estate professionals is a state matter.** There is no national real estate license; a Florida license is valid only in Florida.

**Inside Florida there are four layers you will meet constantly:**

1. **The Florida Constitution** — the top state law. Example: homestead protection from creditors (Article X).
2. **Florida Statutes (F.S.)** — laws passed by the Legislature, organized in chapters. The real estate license law is **Chapter 475**. **Chapter 455** sets general procedures for every profession the DBPR regulates. Others you will meet: Chapter 83 (landlord–tenant), 689 (deeds and conveyances), 718 (condominiums), 201 (documentary stamp tax).
3. **Florida Administrative Code (F.A.C.)** — rules adopted by state agencies using power a statute gives them. The Florida Real Estate Commission's rules are **Chapter 61J2** (escrow deadlines, advertising, offices, education). A rule has the force of law but cannot go beyond its statute.
4. **Case law** — decisions of the courts. Florida, like most of the US, is a **common-law** system: courts' decisions create binding precedent. For this exam the key case is ***Johnson v. Davis* (1985)**: a seller must disclose known hidden defects that materially affect value.

**Who does what.** Two state bodies regulate real estate licensees, and the exam constantly asks which one acts:

- **FREC (Florida Real Estate Commission)** — 7 members appointed by the Governor. It **makes rules** (a *quasi-legislative* power) and **decides discipline** — fines, suspension, revocation (a *quasi-judicial* power).
- **DBPR (Department of Business and Professional Regulation)**, through its Division of Real Estate — **processes applications, issues licenses, keeps records and investigates complaints**. It provides the FREC's administrative support.
- **Courts** — only a court can impose **criminal** penalties (prison) or award **civil damages** to a consumer.

**How to read a citation.** "s. 475.25(1)(d)1., F.S." means Florida Statutes, chapter 475, section 25, subsection (1), paragraph (d), subparagraph 1. "Rule 61J2-14.009, F.A.C." means rule chapter 61J2, part 14, rule .009. You do not need to memorize citations for the exam, but recognizing them lets you check sources yourself.

## 4. Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Statute | A law passed by the Legislature (e.g., Chapter 475 F.S.) |
| Administrative rule | A rule adopted by an agency under a statute (e.g., 61J2 F.A.C.) |
| Case law | Law created by court decisions |
| Common law | A legal system that relies on court precedent as well as written law |
| FREC | Florida Real Estate Commission: rules and discipline |
| DBPR | Department of Business and Professional Regulation: licensing, records, investigations |
| Quasi-legislative | An agency's power to make rules |
| Quasi-judicial | An agency's power to decide cases and impose discipline |
| Licensee | A person holding a real estate license (sales associate or broker) |

## 5. Practical example

A buyer signs a contract for a resale condo in Fort Lauderdale and gives a $25,000 deposit to the seller's broker. The deal falls apart and both parties demand the deposit.

| Question | Source that answers it |
|---|---|
| Must the broker account for the money? | Chapter 475 F.S. (statute) |
| How many business days to notify the FREC and choose a procedure? | Rule 61J2-10.032 F.A.C. (15 / 30 business days) |
| Could the buyer cancel because the condo documents arrived late? | Chapter 718 F.S. (condominium statute) |
| Did the seller have to disclose the roof leak she knew about? | *Johnson v. Davis* (case law) |
| Who investigates if the buyer files a complaint? | DBPR |
| Who can fine or suspend the broker? | FREC |
| Who can send the broker to prison if he stole the money? | A criminal court |

## 6. Portugal comparison

**Familiar principle:** both countries regulate brokerage through a statute and a regulator. In Portugal that is Lei n.º 15/2013 and IMPIC; in Florida it is Chapter 475 and FREC/DBPR.

**What changes:**

- **Level of government.** Portuguese real estate law is national. In the US, licensing is decided state by state, while some topics (fair housing, lending, environment) are federal.
- **Who is licensed.** In Portugal the IMPIC license belongs to the mediation business. In Florida **each individual** needs a license, and a sales associate can only work under a broker.
- **Entry test.** IMPIC requires commercial good standing and liability insurance. Florida requires a 63-hour course and a state exam.
- **Legal method.** Portugal is a civil-law system built on codes (Código Civil). Florida is a common-law system where court decisions such as *Johnson v. Davis* create binding rules.

**Exam implication:** never reason from the Portuguese structure. If a question asks who issues licenses, the answer is the DBPR; who disciplines, the FREC; who imprisons, a court.

## 7. Legal and numerical requirements

**Memorize exactly**

- Chapter 475 F.S. = Florida real estate license law.
- Chapter 455 F.S. = general law for all DBPR-regulated professions.
- Chapter 61J2 F.A.C. = FREC's rules.
- DBPR issues licenses and investigates; FREC makes rules and disciplines; only courts imprison or award damages.

**Understand**

- Federal law → Florida Constitution → Florida Statutes → administrative rules. A lower layer cannot contradict a higher one.
- Agencies (FREC, DBPR) act administratively. Criminal and civil liability belongs to the courts.

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"The FREC is a court."** It is an administrative commission. It can revoke a license but cannot imprison anyone or order a licensee to pay a consumer's damages.
- **"The DBPR punishes licensees."** The DBPR investigates and prosecutes; the FREC decides discipline.
- **"A rule is weaker than a statute, so it is optional."** Rules are binding law. They simply cannot exceed the statute that authorizes them.
- **"The REALTOR® association regulates agents."** It is a private trade association. The state regulates licensees.
- **"My Portuguese experience or AMI license counts."** It does not; Florida licenses individuals under Chapter 475.
- **"Federal law licenses agents."** Licensing is state law.

## 10. Memory sheet

- **475** = license law · **455** = all DBPR professions · **61J2** = FREC rules.
- **DBPR digs, FREC decides, courts jail.**
- Federal: fair housing, RESPA/TILA, ADA, lead paint, CERCLA, tax. State: licensing, escrow, disclosures, landlord–tenant.
- *Johnson v. Davis*: disclose known latent material defects.
- Florida licenses each **person**; Portugal licenses the **business**.

## 2. Why it matters

This is one of the smallest exam areas (1% in the official content outline), and much of it is familiar from your work in Portugal. It still matters because it introduces the vocabulary the exam uses for property types and roles, and it contains one classic trap: confusing REALTOR® membership with a license.

## 3. Simple explanation

**Property types.** The course groups real property by use:

- **Residential** — homes, condos, apartments.
- **Commercial** — income-producing business property: offices, retail, hotels.
- **Industrial** — manufacturing, warehouses, distribution.
- **Agricultural** — farms, groves, ranches, timberland.
- **Special-purpose** — properties with a unique use: churches, schools, hospitals, cemeteries.

**Specializations.** Brokerage (residential or commercial sales and leasing); appraisal (estimating value — a separate license in Florida); property management (leasing, rent collection, maintenance for owners); financing (mortgage lending); development and construction (turning land into finished property); counseling (advice for a fee).

**Organizations.** The **National Association of REALTORS® (NAR)** is the largest trade association. **REALTOR®** is a registered trademark that only members may use; it is *not* a license. In Florida the state association is **Florida Realtors**, and local boards and associations run the MLS in their areas. The **National Association of Real Estate Brokers (NAREB)** uses the term **Realtist**.

**Real estate vs real property vs personal property.** *Real estate* is land plus permanent improvements. *Real property* is real estate plus the bundle of ownership rights. *Personal property* is movable (furniture, cars — and, importantly, cooperative shares).

## 4. Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Special-purpose property | Property with a unique, limited use (church, school, hospital) |
| Property management | Managing property for owners: leasing, rent collection, maintenance |
| REALTOR® | Trademark for NAR members; not a license |
| Realtist | Member of NAREB |
| Real property | Land, improvements, and the rights of ownership |
| Personal property | Movable items not attached to land |

## 5. Practical example

You meet a new Miami colleague whose card says "REALTOR®". That tells you she belongs to NAR (through her local association). It tells you nothing about whether her license is active — you would check that on the DBPR license search. Another agent who is not an NAR member can be fully licensed and may not call herself a REALTOR®.

## 6. Portugal comparison

**Familiar:** the sectors (residential, commercial, industrial) and specialties (sales, management, valuation) work the same way in practice.

**Different:** in Florida the *trade association* (NAR/Florida Realtors, which runs much of the MLS infrastructure) is separate from the *state regulator* (FREC/DBPR). Membership is voluntary; the license is mandatory. Appraisal is also separately licensed in Florida (Part II of Chapter 475), and a sales associate may not call a market analysis an "appraisal".

## 7. Legal and numerical requirements

**Memorize exactly**

- Five property categories: residential, commercial, industrial, agricultural, special-purpose.
- REALTOR® = NAR membership trademark, not a license.

**Understand**

- Licenses are issued by the state; associations are private.

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"REALTOR® is a license level."** It is a trade-association mark.
- **"Every licensee must join NAR."** Membership is voluntary.
- **"A CMA is an appraisal."** A licensee may prepare a CMA, but must not call it an appraisal (Chapter 16).
