## 2. Why it matters

Many Florida sales involve a loan, and in a financed sale the buyer signs two separate loan documents at closing. If you know which one does what, you can read a payoff statement, explain to a seller why the old loan must be formally "satisfied", and spot the clauses that limit what an owner can do. One example is whether a buyer can take over the seller's loan.

For the exam, Residential Mortgages is about 9% of the state exam (V-02). This lesson gives you the vocabulary those questions use: note vs mortgage, mortgagor vs mortgagee, lien theory, the 60-day satisfaction rule and the clause names.

## 3. Simple explanation

> **Source status.** The 60-day satisfaction rule has been checked against the statute (s. 701.04, Fla. Stat.; verified, V-71). Lien theory and the clause definitions come from the study manual (manual; not yet verified). They are standard exam vocabulary, and the course's practice questions test them.

### Two documents, two jobs

A lender wants two things from a borrower: a **promise to pay**, and **something it can take if that promise is broken**. US practice puts them in two separate documents.

| Document | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Promissory note** | The **debt**: the borrower's personal written promise to repay, stating the amount, the interest rate and the terms |
| **Mortgage** | The **security instrument**: it pledges the property as collateral for the note |

**Language warning.** In everyday American speech, "my mortgage" often means the loan. On the exam, the **mortgage is the document that pledges the property**, not the loan. The debt itself is the note.

**Why two documents?** The note makes the borrower **personally** liable, so the lender can pursue the person. The mortgage gives the lender a claim on the **property**, so it can pursue the house. Some clauses adjust one side. For example, the exculpatory clause removes the personal side.

### Mortgagor and mortgagee

The borrower **gives** the mortgage (pledges the property) to the lender:

- **Mortgagor** = **borrower** (gives)
- **Mortgagee** = **lender** (receives)

The rule "-or gives, -ee receives" works across the exam: grantor/grantee, lessor/lessee, optionor/optionee.

### Florida is a lien-theory state

In Florida the borrower keeps **legal title**, and the mortgage gives the lender a **lien**: a claim on the property as security for the debt (manual; not yet verified). In **title-theory** states, the lender holds title, or a trustee holds it under a **deed of trust**.

In practice, a buyer who finances in Florida receives the deed and owns the property, and the lender's protection is its lien. Because the lender holds only a lien, not title, an unpaid lender reaches the property through foreclosure (L12-2).

### Payoff: satisfaction within 60 days

Paying the last dollar ends the debt, but the mortgage stays in the public records until it is released. Florida law puts that duty on the lender. Within **60 days after receiving full payment**, the mortgagee (or the holder of the loan, or its servicer, the company that collects the payments) must:

1. sign a written **satisfaction** (or release);
2. have it acknowledged (the signer formally confirms the signature before an official such as a notary);
3. record it in the county official records;
4. send the recorded satisfaction to the borrower (mortgagor) or the record owner.

The rule is in s. 701.04, Fla. Stat. (verified, V-71). The current text also covers payment of the payoff amount in an **estoppel letter**, which is the lender's written statement of what it takes to pay the loan off.

### The clauses: each answers a "what if?"

| Clause | What it does | When it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Acceleration** | Makes the **entire** balance due on default | The borrower stops paying. The lender can then demand the whole balance at once, not just the missed payments. This is the clause that makes foreclosure practical |
| **Due-on-sale (alienation)** | Makes the balance due if the property is transferred | The owner sells. It blocks an **assumption** without the lender's consent, because the lender chose this borrower, not her buyer |
| **Prepayment** | Gives a right to pay early, or sets a penalty for doing so | Early payoff |
| **Defeasance** | The lien is defeated (cancelled) on full payment | The loan is paid in full |
| **Subordination** | A lien agrees to take a **junior** (lower-priority) position | Common when a land seller finances the land and a construction lender demands first place |
| **Release** | Frees individual parcels from a **blanket mortgage** (one mortgage on several parcels) | A developer sells lots one at a time |
| **Receivership** | A receiver collects the rents during foreclosure | Income property in default |
| **Exculpatory** | The borrower is **not personally liable**. The lender's only recourse is the property | A foreclosure sale falls short: **no deficiency** claim against the borrower |

**How the end of the loan fits together.** Defeasance is the promise in the mortgage that the lien ends on full payment. The **satisfaction** is the recorded proof, due within 60 days.

## 5. Practical example

**Camila buys a condo in Fort Lauderdale** for $380,000 and borrows $304,000. At closing she signs a **promissory note** for $304,000, which is her personal promise to repay at the stated rate and terms. She also signs a **mortgage** pledging the condo as security. She is the **mortgagor** and the bank is the **mortgagee**. Under lien theory, the seller's deed gives title to Camila and the bank holds a lien.

Her mortgage has an **acceleration** clause, so a default makes the whole balance due. It has a **due-on-sale** clause, so a future buyer cannot take over the loan without the bank's consent. Its **prepayment** clause says whether she may pay early without a penalty, and its **defeasance** clause ends the lien once the loan is paid.

**Twelve years later she sells.** The closing agent obtains an estoppel letter and pays the payoff amount from the sale proceeds. From the date the bank receives that payment, it has **60 days** to sign the satisfaction, have it acknowledged, record it in the county official records and send it to Camila. If it does not and she sues to enforce the duty, the **prevailing party** recovers attorney fees and costs (V-71).

**A developer's loan, for contrast.** Near Ocala, a landowner finances part of a developer's land purchase. The construction lender demands first place, so the landowner **subordinates** her mortgage. The construction loan is a **blanket mortgage** with a **release clause**, so each lot can be freed from it when that lot is sold.

## 7. Legal and numerical requirements

**Memorize exactly**

- **Note** = the debt (personal promise). **Mortgage** = the security instrument (pledge of the property).
- **Mortgagor** = borrower. **Mortgagee** = lender.
- Florida is **lien theory**: the borrower keeps legal title and the lender holds a lien. In title-theory states the lender, or a trustee under a deed of trust, holds title (manual; not yet verified).
- **Satisfaction: 60 days.** Within 60 days after receiving full payment (including payment of an estoppel-letter payoff amount), the mortgagee, holder or servicer must:
  - sign a written satisfaction or release;
  - have it acknowledged;
  - record it in the county official records;
  - send the recorded satisfaction to the mortgagor or record owner.

  (s. 701.04(2), Fla. Stat.; verified, V-71)
- In a lawsuit to enforce that duty, the **prevailing party** recovers attorney fees and costs (V-71).
- Parallel duty: satisfactions of other **liens and judgments** are also recorded within 60 days after full payment (s. 701.04(3); V-71).
- The clause meanings in section 3: acceleration, due-on-sale, prepayment, defeasance, subordination, release, receivership, exculpatory (manual; not yet verified).

**Understand**

- The note creates **personal** liability and the mortgage creates a claim on the **property**. An exculpatory clause removes the personal liability, so no deficiency can be claimed.
- Acceleration and due-on-sale both make the whole balance due. The trigger differs: **default** for acceleration, **transfer** for due-on-sale.
- Defeasance is the promise that the lien ends; the satisfaction is the recorded proof.
- A **release clause** frees single parcels from a blanket mortgage. The "satisfaction or release" recorded after payoff ends the whole mortgage.

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"The mortgage is the loan."** No. The **note** is the debt. The mortgage pledges the property.
- **"The mortgagor is the bank."** No. The mortgagor is the **borrower**; the lender is the mortgagee.
- **"In Florida the bank owns the house until payoff."** No. Under lien theory the borrower holds title and the bank holds a lien.
- **"The lien disappears by itself after the last payment."** No. The lender must record a satisfaction within **60 days** and send it to the borrower.
- **"Acceleration and due-on-sale are the same."** Both make the whole balance due, but acceleration is triggered by **default** and due-on-sale by **transfer**.
- **"With an exculpatory clause the borrower can walk away and nothing happens."** The lender can still take the **property**. It just cannot claim a deficiency from the borrower.

## 10. Memory sheet

- **Note = debt** (personal promise). **Mortgage = security** (pledge). "Mortgage" means the document, not the loan.
- **"-or gives, -ee receives":** mortgagor = borrower, mortgagee = lender.
- **Florida = lien theory**: the borrower keeps title (manual; not yet verified).
- **Satisfaction within 60 days** of full payment: signed, acknowledged, recorded and sent to the borrower. The prevailing party gets attorney fees (V-71).
- **Clauses:** acceleration (default), due-on-sale (transfer), prepayment (early payoff), defeasance (paid, so the lien ends), subordination (steps back), release (parcels from a blanket mortgage), receivership (rents during foreclosure), exculpatory (no deficiency).

## 2. Why it matters

Sellers ask: "Can my buyer take over my loan?", "What happens if I stop paying?", "Can I give the house back to the bank?" Each answer depends on **who stays personally liable** and **what happens to the other liens**. A seller who lets a buyer take "subject to" her loan can still owe money years later.

On the exam, Residential Mortgages is about 9% of the content (V-02). This lesson covers three of its most reliable traps:

- who owes a deficiency after an assumption or a "subject to" transfer;
- the exact moment the right to redeem ends;
- whether a deed in lieu wipes out junior liens.

## 3. Simple explanation

> **Source status.** The assumption, foreclosure and redemption rules come from the study manual (manual; not yet verified). They are also what the course's practice questions test. The closing-statement and tax points in Part 1 are verified (V-18, V-19, V-24, V-25).

### Part 1: Taking over an existing loan

A buyer can take over the seller's financing in two ways. The difference between them is **personal liability**, that is, who can be sued for the debt.

**Assumption.** The buyer becomes **personally liable** on the loan. The seller remains **secondarily liable**: if the buyer fails to pay, the lender can still look to her. This changes only if the lender agrees to a **novation**, which substitutes the buyer and **releases the original borrower**.

**"Subject to" the mortgage.** The buyer takes title and makes the payments but promises nothing, so he has **no personal liability**. On default the lender forecloses, and only the **original borrower** faces any deficiency. The buyer keeps paying because otherwise foreclosure takes the property and what he invested in it.

A **due-on-sale clause** (L12-1) makes the balance due on transfer. If the loan has one, any transfer without the lender's consent lets the lender demand the whole balance.

| | Assumption | Assumption + novation | "Subject to" |
|---|---|---|---|
| Buyer personally liable? | **Yes** | **Yes** | **No** |
| Original borrower still liable? | **Yes**, secondarily | **No**, released | **Yes**: alone faces any deficiency |

**At the closing table (verified cross-references).**

- An assumed mortgage is a **debit to the seller and a credit to the buyer** on the closing statement (V-24, which corrected the manual).
- **Note stamps** ($0.35 per $100 or portion) are due on new **and** assumed mortgages (V-18, V-25).
- The **intangible tax** (2 mills, i.e. 0.002 × the mortgage amount) applies to **new** mortgages only. It is not charged again on an assumption when it was paid originally and the debt is unchanged (V-19, V-25).

### Part 2: Default and foreclosure in Florida

1. **Default and acceleration.** The borrower stops paying, and the acceleration clause makes the **entire** balance due.
2. **A court case.** Florida foreclosure is **judicial**: a court action that ends in a **public sale** (the courthouse sale or auction). The lender cannot foreclose without going to court.
3. **Equity of redemption.** Until the moment of the sale, the borrower can **redeem**, that is, stop the foreclosure and keep the property, by **paying in full**. After acceleration, "in full" means the whole debt, not just the missed payments.
4. **No statutory redemption.** Florida gives **no right to redeem after the sale**. An answer offering "10 days" or "1 year" after the sale is a distractor.

### Part 3: After the sale

- **Shortfall.** The lender may seek a **deficiency judgment** (a judgment for the unpaid balance) against the **personally liable** parties. These are the original borrower (unless a novation released her) and any buyer who assumed the loan. So after an assumption without a novation, the seller is still liable secondarily. A "subject to" buyer is never liable. With an **exculpatory clause**, the lender's only recourse is the property, so there is no deficiency judgment.
- **Surplus.** If the sale brings more than the debt, the surplus goes to the **junior lienors first** (holders of lower-priority liens, for example a second mortgagee), **then to the borrower**.

### Part 4: Alternatives to foreclosure

- **Short sale.** The property is sold, and the lender **agrees to accept less than the amount owed**.
- **Deed in lieu of foreclosure.** The borrower deeds the property to the lender. It is **faster**, but the lender takes title **subject to junior liens**, which a foreclosure could have eliminated. So a lender may prefer to foreclose when there is a second mortgage, because otherwise it would own a property still burdened by that lien.

## 4. Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Assumption | The buyer takes over the loan and becomes personally liable; the seller stays secondarily liable |
| Novation | Substitution of a new party that releases the original borrower |
| Subject to | The buyer takes title and pays the loan without personal liability |
| Judicial foreclosure | Foreclosure through a court action ending in a public sale (Florida) |
| Equity of redemption | The borrower's right to pay off the debt and stop foreclosure, up to the sale |
| Statutory redemption | A right to redeem **after** the sale. Florida has none |
| Deficiency judgment | Judgment for the unpaid balance after a foreclosure sale |
| Junior lien | A lien with lower priority than the mortgage being foreclosed (e.g., a second mortgage) |
| Short sale | Sale where the lender accepts less than the amount owed |
| Deed in lieu of foreclosure | The borrower deeds the property to the lender to avoid foreclosure; junior liens survive |

## 5. Practical example

**Case 1: "subject to" in Tampa.** Sofia owes $210,000 on her loan. She sells to Bruno, who takes title **subject to** her mortgage and makes the payments. Two years later Bruno stops paying, and the foreclosure sale brings $190,000. For simplicity, assume the $210,000 already includes everything the court allows.

- Shortfall: $210,000 − $190,000 = **$20,000**.
- The lender can seek a deficiency judgment against **Sofia**. Bruno loses the house but is **not personally liable**.
- If Bruno had **assumed** the loan instead, he would be liable and Sofia would remain secondarily liable, unless the lender had granted a **novation**.

**Case 2: an Orlando owner with two mortgages.** Lucas owes $280,000 on a first mortgage and $35,000 on a second. The first lender accelerates and files a foreclosure lawsuit.

| Option | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Redeem** | Before the sale, he pays the first mortgage **in full** (for example, by refinancing) and keeps the house |
| **Short sale** | He finds a buyer at $265,000 and the first lender **agrees** to accept less than it is owed. The course sources do not say what happens to the second mortgage in a short sale |
| **Deed in lieu** | The first lender would take title **subject to the $35,000 second mortgage**, so it may prefer foreclosure, which can eliminate that lien |
| **Sale brings $330,000** | Ignoring costs for simplicity, the surplus is $330,000 − $280,000 = $50,000. That pays **$35,000 to the second mortgagee**, and the remaining **$15,000 goes to Lucas** |

The day after the auction, Lucas's brother offers the full payoff. **Too late.** In Florida, redemption ends at the sale.

## 6. Portugal comparison

**Familiar principle.** The course's Portuguese entry says a lender enforces a *hipoteca* through court enforcement proceedings (*execução*). This **requires verification** (PT-19). If it is accurate, a court-based process will feel familiar.

**Florida rule.** Foreclosure is judicial. The borrower may redeem by paying in full **until the foreclosure sale**, and there is **no statutory right of redemption after it**. Deficiency judgments are possible against personally liable parties (manual; not yet verified).

**Precise difference.** Portuguese procedure is unverified, so never use it to answer. The exam tests two Florida points: **redemption ends at the sale**, and **junior liens survive a deed in lieu**. For example, a full payment offered one day after the courthouse auction is too late in Florida.

**Exam implication.** There is no post-sale redemption in Florida. "Redeem before the gavel."

- A deed in lieu is a voluntary transfer, not a foreclosure sale, so junior liens stay.

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"After the courthouse sale the borrower has 10 days (or a year) to redeem."** False in Florida. Redemption ends **at the sale**.
- **"A deed in lieu wipes out the second mortgage."** False. The lender takes title **subject to** junior liens. Foreclosure is what can eliminate them.
- **"A 'subject to' buyer owes the deficiency."** False. The original borrower does.
- **"An assumption releases the seller."** Only a **novation** does.
- **"A Florida lender can foreclose without court."** False. Florida foreclosure is judicial.
- **"Any surplus goes straight to the borrower."** Junior lienors are paid **first**.

## 2. Why it matters

Before a buyer falls in love with a house, someone must answer "can they borrow enough?" Lenders answer with two ratios. If you can work them out quickly, you can steer buyers to a realistic price range and understand a lender's "no". Knowing Florida's interest ceiling and the names of the predatory practices also lets you warn homeowners about abusive loan offers.

On the exam, ratio questions are common calculation items (you are given income and payments and asked whether the borrower qualifies), and the usury figures (18%, 25%, $500,000) are exact recall. Residential Mortgages is about 9% of the exam (V-02).

## 3. Simple explanation

> **Source status.** The qualifying ratios and their benchmarks (28/36 and 31/43) are **course conventions** from the study manual (manual; not yet verified, V-26). They are teaching benchmarks, not statutes, and real loan-program rules change over time. Florida's usury limits are verified against the statutes (V-72). The names of the predatory practices and the SAFE Act requirement come from the manual (manual; not yet verified).

### Step 1: What it represents

A lender asks two questions:

1. **Housing expense ratio:** what share of **gross** monthly income goes to the house payment (**PITI**)?
2. **Total obligations ratio:** what share goes to the house payment **plus** all other recurring debts?

**PITI** means Principal + Interest + (property) Taxes + (homeowner's) Insurance. **Gross** income is income before taxes and deductions, not take-home pay.

**Usury** is a separate test: is the loan's annual simple interest rate above Florida's legal ceiling?

### Step 2: Units

| Quantity | Unit | Conversion |
|---|---|---|
| Income | US$ per **month**, **gross** | Annual ÷ 12 |
| Property taxes, insurance | $ per month | Often quoted yearly, so divide by 12 |
| Principal and interest, recurring debts | $ per month | Usually given monthly |
| Ratios | Percent | Decimal × 100. Round to one decimal place only at the end |
| Usury | Percent **per year, simple interest**; loan size = principal in $ | n/a |

Aside: ratios have no units. Just keep every amount monthly and in dollars. There is no metric conversion here.

### Step 3: Formula

- **Housing expense ratio** = PITI ÷ gross monthly income
- **Total obligations ratio** = (PITI + recurring monthly debts) ÷ gross monthly income

For "maximum" questions:

- From the housing ratio: maximum PITI = income × housing benchmark.
- From the total ratio: maximum PITI = (income × total benchmark) − recurring debts.
- **The maximum PITI allowed is the lower of the two.**

**Usury check:** annual simple rate = annual interest ÷ principal. Compare it with the ceiling for that loan size.

### Step 4: When it applies

**Benchmarks (course conventions; manual; not yet verified, V-26)**

| Loan type | Housing expense ratio | Total obligations ratio |
|---|---|---|
| Conventional | 28% | 36% |
| FHA | 31% | 43% |

The borrower must meet **both** ratios. A ratio exactly at the benchmark passes, because the limit is "should not exceed". The manual's example below qualifies at exactly 36%.

**Florida usury (verified, V-72)**

- **Loans of $500,000 or less:** interest above the equivalent of **18% per year simple interest** is usurious and unlawful (ss. 687.02(1), 687.03(1), Fla. Stat.).
- **Loans over $500,000** (a loan, advance, line of credit or forbearance): interest is not usurious unless it exceeds the s. 687.071 rate, which in effect makes the ceiling **25%**.
  - A loan counts as over $500,000 if the principal **initially exceeds** that amount **or may reasonably be expected to exceed it** during the term (s. 687.03(3)).
- **Criminal usury** (s. 687.071), unless otherwise allowed by law. Willfully and knowingly charging:
  - **more than 25% but not more than 45%** a year is a **second-degree misdemeanor**;
  - **more than 45%** is a **third-degree felony**.
- Knowingly making an **extortionate extension of credit** is a **second-degree felony**.
- Credit extended in violation of s. 687.071 is **not an enforceable debt** in Florida.

**Predatory lending (manual; not yet verified).** The exam names four practices:

- **equity stripping**: lending that drains the owner's equity;
- **loan flipping**: repeated refinancing to collect fees;
- **packing**: hidden add-on products;
- **balloon abuse**: misuse of a balloon, which is a lump final payment.

The **SAFE Act** requires mortgage loan originators to be licensed and registered through **NMLS**.

### Step 5: Worked example

**Example A: buyers in Tampa.** Their gross income is $102,000 a year. Principal and interest are $1,780 a month, taxes $4,800 a year and insurance $3,240 a year. They pay $520 a month on a car and $90 a month in credit-card minimums.

1. Gross monthly income: 102,000 ÷ 12 = **$8,500**
2. Taxes: 4,800 ÷ 12 = $400. Insurance: 3,240 ÷ 12 = $270
3. PITI: 1,780 + 400 + 270 = **$2,450**
4. Housing ratio: 2,450 ÷ 8,500 = 0.2882 → **28.8%**
5. Total: 2,450 + 520 + 90 = 3,060. Then 3,060 ÷ 8,500 = 0.36 → **36.0%**
6. **Conventional:** 28.8 > 28 ✗ and 36.0 ≤ 36 ✓, so they **do not qualify**. **FHA:** 28.8 ≤ 31 ✓ and 36.0 ≤ 43 ✓, so they **qualify**.
7. Maximum conventional PITI: 28% × 8,500 = $2,380. The total ratio would allow 36% × 8,500 − 610 = $2,450. The lower is **$2,380**, so the PITI must fall by $70.

**Example B (study manual).** Gross monthly income $7,500; PITI $2,025; car and cards $675.

- Housing: 2,025 ÷ 7,500 = **27%** ✓ (≤ 28).
- Total: 2,700 ÷ 7,500 = **36%** ✓ (≤ 36). The borrower only just qualifies.
- With $750 of car and card payments instead: 2,775 ÷ 7,500 = **37%** ✗.

### Step 6: Reasonableness check

- A result over 100% means you divided upside down. A result of 2% or 3% usually means you used **annual** income.
- The total ratio is **always at least** the housing ratio.
- Multiply back: 8,500 × 0.288 ≈ 2,448, close to the $2,450 PITI ✓.
- Boundaries matter. In Example B, $75 more of debt turns a pass into a fail.

### Step 7: Common errors

- Dividing by **net** or **annual** income instead of gross monthly income.
- Leaving **taxes and insurance** out of PITI, or the recurring debts out of the total ratio.
- Rounding 36.4% down to "36%" so the borrower appears to qualify.
- Mixing up the conventional and FHA benchmarks, or checking only one ratio.
- Applying 18% to a loan over $500,000, or forgetting that a loan **expected** to exceed $500,000 counts as over.

<details><summary>Check your answer</summary>

### Step 10: Exam application

**Ratios:**

1. Convert the income to **gross monthly**.
2. Build the full **PITI**.
3. Compute **both** ratios.
4. Compare them with the right set: conventional 28/36 or FHA 31/43. "At or below" qualifies.

For "maximum PITI" questions, keep the lower of the two limits. If a question states different benchmarks, use its numbers.

**Usury:**

1. Find the loan size, including any **expected** growth above $500,000.
2. Pick the ceiling: 18% or 25%.
3. If the rate is above 25%, place it in the criminal tiers. They apply only if the rate is charged willfully and knowingly, unless otherwise allowed by law.

## 4. Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| PITI | Principal, interest, taxes, insurance: the monthly housing payment |
| Gross monthly income | Income before taxes and deductions, per month |
| Housing expense ratio | PITI ÷ gross monthly income |
| Total obligations ratio | (PITI + recurring debts) ÷ gross monthly income |
| Recurring debts | Monthly debt payments, e.g. a car loan and credit-card minimums |
| Usury | Charging interest above the legal maximum |
| Criminal usury | Willfully and knowingly charging more than 25% a year, unless otherwise allowed by law (s. 687.071) |
| Loan flipping / packing | Repeated refinancing to collect fees / hidden add-on products |
| SAFE Act | Requires mortgage loan originators to be licensed and registered (NMLS) |

## 5. Practical example

**Marta, a nurse in Orlando**, earns **$90,000 a year gross**, and her take-home pay is about $5,700 a month. The home she wants has a PITI of **$1,980**, and she pays **$640** a month on a car and cards.

- **Correct (gross):** $90,000 ÷ 12 = $7,500. Housing: 1,980 ÷ 7,500 = **26.4%** ✓. Total: 2,620 ÷ 7,500 = 0.3493 → **34.9%** ✓. She qualifies under 28/36.
- **Wrong (take-home):** 1,980 ÷ 5,700 = 34.7% and 2,620 ÷ 5,700 = 46.0%. These figures would wrongly make her look unqualified, even under FHA.

**Her neighbor's phone call.** A caller offers her neighbor his **third refinance in two years**, each with large fees (**loan flipping**). The fees drain his equity (**equity stripping**), the paperwork hides an add-on product (**packing**), and a low monthly payment ends in a huge final payment he could not make (**balloon abuse**).

The caller also offers a $60,000 second mortgage at **22%** simple interest. The loan is $500,000 or less, so the ceiling is 18%. At 22% it is **usurious** unless otherwise allowed by law: $13,200 a year of interest against a maximum of $10,800. It is not criminal usury, because the rate is not above 25%.

Marta suggests he check the originator in **NMLS** (SAFE Act) before signing anything.

## 6. Portugal comparison

**Familiar principle.** The course's Portuguese entry says Portuguese banks apply a debt-service-to-income limit (*taxa de esforço*) on **net** income under Banco de Portugal macroprudential measures. Your own Drive guide says the limit was lowered from 50% to 45% in May 2026. All of this **requires verification** (PT-18).

**Florida rule.** The exam convention divides PITI (housing, 28%) and PITI plus recurring debt (total, 36%) by **gross** monthly income. FHA uses 31/43.

**Precise difference.** Gross vs net income is the trap, and PITI includes taxes and insurance. For example, take gross income of $8,000 a month, PITI of $2,200 and a $500 car payment. The housing ratio is 2,200 ÷ 8,000 = **27.5%** and the total ratio is 2,700 ÷ 8,000 = **33.75%**. That borrower qualifies conventionally.

**Exam implication.** Use gross income, and include taxes and insurance in PITI. Never use a Portuguese percentage or net income in a Florida answer. "Florida divides by GROSS."

## 7. Legal and numerical requirements

**Memorize exactly**

- Housing expense ratio = **PITI ÷ gross monthly income**. Total obligations ratio = **(PITI + recurring debts) ÷ gross monthly income**.
- **Conventional 28% / 36%** and **FHA 31% / 43%**: course conventions, not statutes (manual; not yet verified, V-26).
- PITI = principal, interest, **taxes, insurance**.
- Usury: **18% per year simple interest** on loans of **$500,000 or less**. In effect **25%** on loans over $500,000, including loans whose principal may reasonably be expected to exceed it during the term (ss. 687.02(1), 687.03(1) and (3); verified, V-72).
- Criminal usury, unless otherwise allowed by law (s. 687.071(2)-(4); verified, V-72):
  - willfully and knowingly charging **more than 25% up to 45%** is a **second-degree misdemeanor**;
  - **more than 45%** is a **third-degree felony**;
  - an extortionate extension of credit is a **second-degree felony**;
  - such credit is **not an enforceable debt**.
- Predatory practices: equity stripping, loan flipping, packing, balloon abuse. The **SAFE Act** requires mortgage loan originators to be licensed and registered (NMLS) (manual; not yet verified).

**Understand**

- Pass **both** ratios. Exactly at the benchmark passes.
- The maximum PITI is the **lower** of the two limits.
- Above 18% on a loan of $500,000 or less is unlawful (civil usury). The **criminal** tiers begin only above 25%.
- The benchmarks are teaching conventions. If a question gives other figures, use them.

| Annual simple rate | Loan of $500,000 or less | Loan over $500,000 (or expected to exceed it) |

## 9. Common misconceptions

- **"Use take-home pay."** No. Divide by **gross** monthly income.
- **"The house payment is P&I."** No. PITI adds **taxes and insurance**.
- **"28/36 is Florida law."** No. These are course conventions, not statutes.
- **"FHA uses 28/36 too."** No. The FHA benchmarks are **31/43**.
- **"18% caps every Florida loan."** No. Loans over $500,000, including those expected to exceed it, have an effective ceiling of **25%**.
- **"Anything above 18% is a crime."** No. The criminal tiers start **above 25%**.
- **"The Portuguese 45% limit applies here."** No. That figure requires verification, uses net income and has no place in a Florida answer.
